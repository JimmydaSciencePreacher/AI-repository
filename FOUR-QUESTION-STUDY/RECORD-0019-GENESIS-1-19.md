# Study Record 0019 — Genesis 1:19 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 1:19 (KJV)**

> And the evening and the morning were the fourth day.

## Question under examination

What does Genesis 1:19 establish when it records, “And the evening and the morning were the fourth day”? How does this day designation relate to the commands, making, setting, functions, and assessment recorded in verses 14–18?

## KJV text and immediate context

Genesis 1:14 (KJV):
> And God said, Let there be lights in the firmament of the heaven to divide the day from the night; and let them be for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years:

Genesis 1:15 (KJV):
> And let them be for lights in the firmament of the heaven to give light upon the earth: and it was so.

Genesis 1:16 (KJV):
> And God made two great lights; the greater light to rule the day, and the lesser light to rule the night: he made the stars also.

Genesis 1:17 (KJV):
> And God set them in the firmament of the heaven to give light upon the earth,

Genesis 1:18 (KJV):
> And to rule over the day and over the night, and to divide the light from the darkness: and God saw that it was good.

Genesis 1:19 (KJV):
> And the evening and the morning were the fourth day.

The preceding verses record God's command concerning the lights and their purposes (verse 14), the purpose of giving light upon the earth and the affirmation “and it was so” (verse 15), God's making of the two great lights and the stars (verse 16), God's setting of them in the firmament (verse 17), and their described functions together with God's assessment that it was good (verse 18). Verse 19 then records the evening and the morning as the fourth day.

This verse is a day-designation statement. It does not repeat the making, setting, or functions of the lights. It does not explain the physical mechanism of evening or morning, specify the duration of the day in modern units, or provide a calendar system. It identifies the completed sequence as “the fourth day” in the narrative.

## Direct observations

1. The verse begins with “And”, continuing the creation-day account.
2. It names two terms in sequence: “the evening” and “the morning”.
3. The verse states that the evening and the morning “were the fourth day”.
4. The day number is explicitly “fourth”.
5. The verse does not name God as the grammatical subject of a new making, setting, naming, or dividing action.
6. The verse does not repeat the command concerning the lights in verse 14.
7. The verse does not repeat the making of the lights and stars in verse 16.
8. The verse does not repeat the setting of the lights in verse 17.
9. The verse does not repeat the functions or the assessment “good” in verse 18.
10. The verse uses a recurring narrative formula also seen at the close of the first three days: Genesis 1:5, 1:8, and 1:13.
11. In Genesis 1:5, the KJV records “the evening and the morning were the first day”; in Genesis 1:8, “the evening and the morning were the second day”; and in Genesis 1:13, “the evening and the morning were the third day”.
12. Genesis 1:19 continues that same evening-and-morning day-designation pattern with “the fourth day”.
13. The verse does not define the precise duration of an evening, a morning, or the fourth day.
14. The verse does not state a modern clock time for the beginning or ending of the day.
15. The verse does not itself describe the astronomical cause of evening and morning.
16. The verse does not state that the lights created or appointed in verses 14–18 are the cause of every physical detail associated with evening and morning.
17. The verse does not say that the day designation is a scientific measurement or a modern calendar convention.
18. The verse does not say that the Witness determines or creates the day; it records the designation given in the text.
19. The verse follows the account of God's work and assessment on the fourth day and marks the day in the same narrative pattern used for the earlier days.
20. The verse must be read in its immediate sequence without treating the day-designation formula as interchangeable with the actions and functions recorded in verses 14–18.

The direct finding is that Genesis 1:19 closes the account with the recurring formula “the evening and the morning were the fourth day”. It identifies the fourth day in the narrative. The verse itself does not define a modern time measurement or explain the physical mechanism of evening and morning.
