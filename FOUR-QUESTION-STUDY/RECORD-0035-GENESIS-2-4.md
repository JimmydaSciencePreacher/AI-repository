# Study Record 0035 — Genesis 2:4 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:4 (KJV)**

> These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens.

## Question under examination

What does Genesis 2:4 establish when it begins, “These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth when they were created”? Does the verse close the first account, introduce a new section, or connect the account of the heavens and earth with what follows? What does “in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens” mean in its immediate context?

The central question is: How does this verse identify the account that follows, and what distinctions must be preserved between “generations”, “created”, “made”, and the two orders in which “heavens” and “earth” are named?

## KJV text and immediate context

**Genesis 2:1 (KJV):**

> Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them.

**Genesis 2:2 (KJV):**

> And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made.

**Genesis 2:3 (KJV):**

> And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made.

**Genesis 2:4 (KJV):**

> These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens.

**Genesis 2:5 (KJV):**

> And every plant of the field before it was in the earth, and every herb of the field before it grew: for the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground.

Verse 4 follows the account's statements about finishing, ending, resting, blessing, and sanctifying. It then introduces “the generations of the heavens and of the earth”. Verse 5 begins describing conditions concerning field vegetation, rain, and the man to till the ground.

The immediate context is important. Verse 4 is not to be interpreted in isolation from either the completed account preceding it or the description beginning in verse 5.

2. The subject identified is “the heavens and of the earth”.

5. The verse uses both “created” and “made”.

7. The second naming order is “the earth and the heavens”.

16. Genesis 2:5 continues with a description of field vegetation before it was in the earth and before it grew, and gives the stated conditions involving rain and a man to till the ground.
17. The direct finding is that Genesis 2:4 identifies “the generations” of the heavens and earth, associates their creation with the making by the LORD God, and introduces the account that follows.

These observations distinguish the actual wording from possible interpretations of the verse's literary function and temporal expression.

## Examination of “These are the generations”

The KJV translates the Hebrew expression commonly transliterated *'elleh toledot* as “These are the generations”.

The Hebrew noun *toledot* is associated with generations, descendants, or the account/developments proceeding from a source. Its use in Genesis is important because the expression recurs as a heading or organizing formula in the book.

Examples include:

**Genesis 5:1 (KJV):**

> This is the book of the generations of Adam. In the day that God created man, in the likeness of God made he him;

**Genesis 6:9 (KJV):**

> These are the generations of Noah: Noah was a just man and perfect in his generations, and Noah walked with God.

**Genesis 10:1 (KJV):**

> Now these are the generations of the sons of Noah, Shem, Ham, and Japheth: and unto them were sons born after the flood.

These examples show that “generations” is used in Genesis as an organizing expression introducing the account associated with a person or subject. The expression is not limited to a list of biological descendants in every occurrence; it can introduce the account of what proceeds from or is associated with the named subject.

In Genesis 2:4, the named subject is not Adam or Noah but “the heavens and of the earth”.

This is significant. The verse introduces an account associated with the heavens and earth. The following verses describe conditions and events concerning the earth, vegetation, rain, and man.

**Direct finding:** “These are the generations” introduces an account associated with the heavens and earth.

**Not established by this verse alone:** A complete definition of every event or development included in those generations, or a technical GUA-W category called “generation”.

## Examination of “when they were created”

The verse says the heavens and earth “were created”.

The grammatical subject is the heavens and earth. They are described as created, not as the creators.

The preceding account has already identified God as the Creator. Genesis 2:4 continues to preserve the distinction between the created heavens and earth and the One who made them.

The verse does not provide a separate account of the initial creation of the heavens and earth in this clause. It refers to them as created and then connects that statement with the LORD God's making of the earth and heavens.

**Direct finding:** The heavens and earth are identified as created.

**Not admitted:** The heavens and earth are their own originating cause, or Genesis 2:4 alone describes a second independent creation.

## Examination of “in the day”

The verse continues:

> in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens.

The KJV expression “in the day” can function as a temporal expression. In English, it may sound as though the verse is specifying one ordinary day, but that conclusion should not be made solely from the English phrase.

Genesis itself uses “in the day” in contexts where the expression means “when” or identifies the time of an event.

For example:

**Genesis 2:17 (KJV):**

> But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

The expression is part of a temporal statement. Its interpretation must be considered in the context of the event described, rather than assuming that every occurrence of “in the day” means the same thing in every context.

Genesis 2:4 is therefore not sufficient, by itself, to establish that the whole Creation account occurred within one ordinary twenty-four-hour day. Nor does the phrase by itself establish an indefinite period. The exact temporal force must be examined through the Hebrew construction and the immediate context.

**Direct finding:** The verse associates the LORD God's making of the earth and heavens with the creation being described.

**Unresolved:** The precise temporal force of “in the day” in this construction and its relation to the day designations in Genesis 1.

## Examination of “created” and “made”

Genesis 2:4 uses both expressions:

- “when they were created”;
- “that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens”.

The two English verbs should be preserved as written. Their presence in the same verse does not, by itself, prove that they describe two separate creation systems, two different originating causes, or two successive technical stages.

Genesis 2:3 also uses both words:

> all his work which God created and made.

This immediate connection is important. The combined wording in Genesis 2:3 and 2:4 places the language of creation and making in the same concluding and introductory context.

The Hebrew terms commonly represented by “created” and “made” are not identical in form:
- *bara* — the verb translated “created” in Genesis 2:4.
- *asah* — the verb translated “made” in Genesis 2:4.

Their usage across Scripture deserves study. But a lexical distinction is not automatically proof of two separate processes or architectural layers. The context must determine what can be established in this passage.

**Direct finding:** The verse uses distinct Hebrew verbs translated “created” and “made”, while identifying the LORD God as the Maker.

**Not admitted:** A separate technical GUA-W sequence or two independent creation events based solely on the presence of the two verbs.

## Examination of the reversed naming order

The verse uses two orders:

1. “the heavens and of the earth”;
2. “the earth and the heavens”.

This reversal is present in the KJV text and should not be ignored.

The first expression names the two domains in the same order familiar from Genesis 1:1. The second names the earth before the heavens in the statement about what the LORD God made.

The verse does not explain why the order changes. One possible observation is that the second clause may be moving the account's focus toward the earth, which is the subject of the immediate description in verses 5 onward. That is a contextual possibility, not an explicit explanation supplied by verse 4.

We must not assign a technical meaning to the reversal without further Scriptural evidence.

**Direct finding:** The order is reversed within the verse.

**Unresolved:** Whether the reversal carries a deliberate literary emphasis, reflects the movement of the account toward the earth, or has another explanation.

## Examination of “the LORD God”

Genesis 1 repeatedly uses “God” in the Creation account. Genesis 2:4 uses the combined designation “the LORD God”.

The KJV preserves this distinction. It should be recorded rather than flattened into a single English designation.

The verse identifies the LORD God as the One who made the earth and the heavens. It does not introduce a second creator or a different originating authority.

The name change in the wording is a legitimate question for further study. The verse alone does not provide a complete explanation of the significance of the combined divine designation.

**Direct finding:** Genesis 2:4 identifies the Maker as “the LORD God”.

**Not admitted:** The change in designation establishes a different Creator or a second source of Creation.

## Is Genesis 2:4 a new creation account?

The verse follows Genesis 2:1–3 and introduces “the generations of the heavens and of the earth”. The next verses describe conditions concerning vegetation, rain, the ground, and man.

This supports reading Genesis 2:4 as a transition into an account that develops the earth-focused details that follow.

However, the verse does not say, “God created the heavens and earth again.” Nor does it explicitly label the following material a second, independent Creation.

The relationship between Genesis 1 and Genesis 2 must be determined by reading the passages together. Genesis 2:4 connects the account of the heavens and earth with what follows; it does not authorize us to declare a contradiction or a second independent creation without further evidence.

**Admitted:** Genesis 2:4 introduces an account associated with the heavens and earth and connects the Creation account with the material that follows.

**Unresolved:** The full literary relationship between the account in Genesis 1 and the earth-focused description beginning in Genesis 2:4–5.
