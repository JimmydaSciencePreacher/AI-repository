# Study Record 0036 — Genesis 2:5 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:5 (KJV)**

> And every plant of the field before it was in the earth, and every herb of the field before it grew: for the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground.

## Question under examination

What does Genesis 2:5 describe when it speaks of the plant of the field before it was in the earth and the herb of the field before it grew? What relationship does the verse itself establish between those conditions, the absence of rain, and the absence of a man to till the ground? How does this description relate to Genesis 1:11–12, where the earth brought forth vegetation?

The examination must not assume in advance that Genesis 2:5 says no vegetation of any kind existed anywhere. It must also not assume that the verse is merely repeating Genesis 1:11–12 without adding information.

2. It uses the temporal expression “before it was in the earth”.
3. It then says, “and every herb of the field before it grew”.
4. The verse gives a reason introduced by “for”.
5. The first stated condition is that the LORD God “had not caused it to rain upon the earth”.
6. The second stated condition is “there was not a man to till the ground”.
7. The verse names both “the earth” and “the ground”; the wording should not be flattened without examination.

14. Genesis 2:5 does not state that the man is the creator or originating cause of vegetation.
15. The verse does not explicitly explain every botanical category included in “plant of the field” and “herb of the field”.
16. The direct observation is that Genesis 2:5 describes a condition in which the named field growth had not yet appeared or grown, and gives the absence of rain and of a man to till the ground as its explanation.

## Examination of the wording

### “Plant of the field” and “herb of the field”

The KJV uses two expressions:

- “plant of the field”;
- “herb of the field”.

The Hebrew nouns are different. The first is commonly transliterated *śîaḥ* and is often rendered shrub, bush, or plant. The second is *ʿēśeḇ*, commonly rendered herb, grass, or vegetation. Both are qualified by “of the field”.

The exact botanical scope of these expressions is not settled by the English wording alone. Some translations and commentaries distinguish wild or uncultivated growth from cultivated crops or grasses. That distinction is a possible interpretive explanation, not something the KJV verse explicitly defines.

The word “field” also must not automatically be equated with the whole earth or every ecological setting. The verse's repeated phrase is “of the field”.

**Direct finding:** Two related but distinct expressions for field growth are used.

**Unresolved:** The precise range of plants intended by each expression and whether the two phrases distinguish uncultivated growth from cultivated growth.

### “Before it was in the earth” and “before it grew”

The KJV preserves two different descriptions:

- the plant of the field before it “was in the earth”;
- the herb of the field before it “grew”.

The Hebrew temporal word commonly transliterated *ṭerem* means “before” or “not yet”. The first clause uses a form of *hāyâ* (“be”, “become”, or “come to be”); the second uses *ṣāmaḥ* (“sprout” or “grow”).

The two clauses therefore should not be collapsed into one identical statement. The first is expressed in terms of being or coming to be in the earth; the second in terms of sprouting or growth.

The KJV wording does not itself provide a botanical explanation of the difference. Nor does the lexical distinction by itself establish two separate creation events or a technical sequence.

**Direct finding:** The verse uses “before” with two different descriptions: being in the earth and growing.

**Unresolved:** Whether the difference marks two distinct stages, two kinds of field vegetation, or a parallel way of describing the field's condition.

### “For the LORD God had not caused it to rain”

The word “for” connects the stated conditions with the preceding description. The verse identifies the absence of rain as one reason for the field condition being described.

The subject of the action is “the LORD God”. The verse does not describe rain as an independent authority or an originating cause. It records that the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth.

The next verse must be retained in the reading:

**Genesis 2:6 (KJV):**
> But there went up a mist from the earth, and watered the whole face of the ground.

Verse 6 records watering by mist. Therefore, Genesis 2:5–6 distinguishes the absence of rain from the presence of another stated means of watering. It would be inaccurate to say that the ground received no water at all.

**Direct finding:** Rain had not been caused by the LORD God upon the earth in the described condition; verse 6 then records mist watering the ground.

**Not admitted:** Genesis 2:5–6 says the ground was completely without water.

### “And there was not a man to till the ground”

The second condition is the absence of a man to till the ground.

The verse connects the absence of a man with the condition of field growth. The verb translated “to till” is associated with working or cultivating the ground. The text does not say that man originates plant life. Genesis 1:11–12 attributes the bringing forth of vegetation to the earth in response to God's command, and Genesis 2:5 identifies the LORD God as the One who had not caused rain.

Genesis 2:7 then records the formation of man, and Genesis 2:8 records the planting of the garden and the placing of man there. The relationship between the absence of a man in verse 5 and the formed man in verses 7–8 is an important part of the immediate sequence.

The verse does not yet specify all the work the man will be assigned. Genesis 2:15 later states that the LORD God took the man and put him into the garden of Eden “to dress it and to keep it”.

**Direct finding:** The verse records that no man was present to till the ground.

**Not admitted:** Man is the originating cause of plant life, or verse 5 alone defines the full scope of man's later responsibility.

## Relationship to Genesis 1:11–12

Genesis 1:11–12 records God's command that the earth bring forth grass, seed-bearing herb, and fruit tree, followed by the report that the earth brought them forth and God saw that it was good.

Genesis 2:5 does not repeat that same wording. It speaks of the plant and herb “of the field”, before they were in the earth or grew, and gives the absence of rain and a man to till the ground as the explanation.

Several distinctions must therefore be preserved:

1. Genesis 1:11–12 speaks of the earth bringing forth named forms of vegetation in response to God's command.
2. Genesis 2:5 speaks specifically of field growth and its stated conditions.
3. Genesis 2:5 does not say that the vegetation of Genesis 1:11–12 was never created.
4. Genesis 2:5 does not explicitly say that every plant everywhere was absent.
5. Genesis 2:6 records watering by mist, so the absence of rain is not the same as the absence of all watering.
6. Genesis 2:7–8 proceeds to the formation of man and the planting of the garden.

One possible reading is that Genesis 2:5 concerns particular field growth or its cultivation rather than denying the vegetation already recorded in Genesis 1. The distinction between wild growth and cultivated crops appears in some translations and commentary, but the precise scope must remain open until the language and wider context are examined sufficiently.

**Admitted:** The two passages describe vegetation with different wording, focus, and immediate context. Genesis 2:5 must not be made to erase Genesis 1:11–12.

**Unresolved:** The exact relationship between the field vegetation of Genesis 2:5 and the vegetation brought forth in Genesis 1:11–12.

## The sequence in Genesis 2:5–8

The immediate sequence is:

1. **Genesis 2:5:** Field growth described in relation to the absence of rain and a man to till the ground.
2. **Genesis 2:6:** Mist goes up from the earth and waters the ground.
3. **Genesis 2:7:** The LORD God forms man from the dust of the ground and breathes into his nostrils the breath of life.
4. **Genesis 2:8:** The LORD God plants a garden in Eden and puts the man there.

This sequence is directly present in the text. It is not yet a complete explanation of every temporal or botanical relationship. The record must not silently convert literary order into a detailed chronology beyond what the passage establishes.

Genesis 2:15 supplies a later statement of man's placement and work in the garden. It may be examined when the study reaches that verse; it should not be imported as though it were already stated in Genesis 2:5.
