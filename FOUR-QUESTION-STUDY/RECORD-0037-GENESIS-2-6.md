# Study Record 0037 — Genesis 2:6 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:6 (KJV)**

> But there went up a mist from the earth, and watered the whole face of the ground.

## Question under examination

What does Genesis 2:6 state about the mist, its movement, its origin, and its watering of the ground? What relationship does the verse establish with the absence of rain in Genesis 2:5? What can be established from the Hebrew wording, and what remains uncertain about the nature of the phenomenon?

The examination must preserve the KJV text while acknowledging that the Hebrew noun translated “mist” is rare and has been understood in more than one way. No physical mechanism may be supplied merely because one interpretation appears plausible.

## KJV text and immediate context

**Genesis 2:5–8 (KJV):**

**Genesis 2:5**
> And every plant of the field before it was in the earth, and every herb of the field before it grew: for the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground.

**Genesis 2:6**
> But there went up a mist from the earth, and watered the whole face of the ground.

**Genesis 2:7**
> And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

**Genesis 2:8**
> And the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden; and there he put the man whom he had formed.

The immediate sequence is:

1. Verse 5 describes field vegetation in relation to the absence of rain and the absence of a man to till the ground.
2. Verse 6 records something going up from the earth and watering the whole face of the ground.
3. Verse 7 records the LORD God forming man from the dust of the ground and breathing into his nostrils the breath of life.
4. Verse 8 records the LORD God planting a garden in Eden and placing the formed man there.

The KJV begins verse 6 with “But”. The Hebrew begins with the conjunction *waw*, which can be represented as “and” or, according to context, “but”. The contrast is apparent in the English KJV sequence: rain had not been caused in verse 5, yet watering is described in verse 6. The conjunction alone should not be made to carry more meaning than the surrounding statements establish.

## Direct observations

1. The verse names “a mist”.
2. The mist is the grammatical subject of the movement described.
3. The movement is expressed as “went up”.
4. Its stated point of origin is “from the earth”.
5. The verse then says it “watered” the ground.
6. The extent is described as “the whole face of the ground”.
7. The verse does not use the word “rain”.
8. The verse does not name a spring, river, aquifer, cloud, dew, or rainfall cycle.
9. The verse does not explain the physical mechanism by which the mist went up or watered the ground.
10. Genesis 2:5 says the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth.
11. Genesis 2:6 nevertheless records watering of the ground.
12. The verse uses “earth” for the source and “ground” for the surface watered.
13. Genesis 2:7 immediately uses “ground” again in describing the dust from which the LORD God formed man.
14. The verse describes the watering as an action performed by the mist; it does not state that the mist is an independent originating cause.
15. The verse does not identify the mist's composition, temperature, density, or duration.
16. The verse does not state that the mist was the only source or means of water in Creation.
17. The direct finding is that the text records a mist going up from the earth and watering the whole face of the ground, while leaving the precise nature and mechanism of the event unexplained.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

The Masoretic Hebrew text is:

וְאֵד יַעֲלֶה מִן־הָאָרֶץ וְהִשְׁקָה אֶת־כָּל־פְּנֵי הָאֲדָמָה

The main expressions are examined below.

### 1. “Mist” — אֵד (*ʾēḏ*)

The Hebrew noun is *ʾēḏ* (אֵד), traditionally rendered “mist” in the KJV. It is rare: its other occurrence is Job 36:27. The rarity matters because there is little biblical usage from which to establish a broad range of meaning.

The KJV's “mist” is a legitimate traditional rendering, but the precise meaning is disputed. Ancient and modern witnesses reflect alternatives:

- The KJV retains “mist”.
- The Septuagint renders the expression with a Greek word commonly meaning “spring” or “fountain”.
- The Vulgate likewise has a word for spring or source.
- The Targumic rendering has been understood as cloud.
- Some modern translations and notes propose springs, streams, or subterranean water.

The NET Bible note explains that the traditional “mist” is associated with the use of the Hebrew word in Job 36:27, while an Akkadian cognate has been compared with subterranean springs or waterways. That comparison is a lexical proposal, not a demonstration that the Genesis event was an aquifer or spring.

The Cambridge Bible note also reports uncertainty and notes that ancient versions differ. Scholarly discussion therefore does not warrant presenting “spring” or “mist” as an uncontested result.

**Direct finding:** The KJV translates *ʾēḏ* as “mist”.

**Lexical finding:** The Hebrew noun is rare and its exact sense in Genesis 2:6 is disputed.

**Unresolved:** Whether the referent is best understood as mist/vapor, a spring or water source, a stream or flow, or another form of water rising from the earth.

The KJV wording remains the Scriptural text for this study. Alternative translations are considered to understand the lexical question, not to replace the KJV.

### 2. “Went up” — יַעֲלֶה (*yaʿăleh*)

The verb is from *ʿālah* (עלה), meaning to go up, ascend, or rise.

The form in Genesis 2:6 is imperfect. In this narrative setting, some grammatical treatments understand it as describing a customary or repeated action: “would go up” or “used to go up”. The NET note specifically identifies a customary nuance in the narrative context.

The KJV renders it “went up”. The wording establishes upward movement from the stated source. It does not by itself tell us whether the action happened once, periodically, or continuously in a technical physical sense.

**Direct finding:** Something identified as *ʾēḏ* goes up from the earth.

**Supported grammatical observation:** The verb form can carry a customary or repeated sense in this context.

**Unresolved:** The precise temporal pattern intended and the physical process, if any, behind the movement.

### 3. “From the earth” — מִן־הָאָרֶץ (*min-hāʾāreṣ*)

The preposition *min* means “from” or “out of”. The noun *ʾereṣ* means earth, land, or country depending on context.

The verse explicitly locates the origin of the upward movement as “the earth”. It does not state that the water originated in the sky and fell downward as rain.

The distinction is important because verse 5 has just mentioned the absence of rain. Verse 6 describes upward movement from the earth, not rainfall from above.

**Direct finding:** The stated direction is from the earth upward.

**Not admitted:** The claim that the verse identifies a particular underground reservoir, aquifer, or geological formation.

### 4. “Watered” — וְהִשְׁקָה (*wehišqāh*)

The verb comes from *šāqâ* (שקה), whose range includes giving drink, causing to drink, and watering or irrigating.

The form in this verse is causative (Hiphil). In context, the mist is described as causing the ground to receive water or as watering it.

The underlying image is not merely that something was present in the air. The stated result is that the ground was watered.

The verb does not identify how the water reached the ground. It records the action and result in the wording of the verse.

**Direct finding:** The mist is described as watering the ground.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the verb itself proves condensation, precipitation, seepage, capillary action, or irrigation by a modern engineered system.

### 5. “The whole face of the ground” — כָּל־פְּנֵי הָאֲדָמָה (*kol-pĕnê hāʾădāmāh*)

The phrase includes:

- *kol* — all, whole, or entire;
- *pĕnê* — face or surface;
- *ʾădāmāh* — ground, soil, or land.

The KJV renders the phrase “the whole face of the ground”. The wording emphasizes the extent of the surface being watered.

The verse uses *ʾereṣ* (“earth”) for the source and *ʾădāmāh* (“ground”) for the surface watered. These terms are related in the passage but are not the same Hebrew noun. Genesis 2:7 then speaks of the dust of the *ʾădāmāh*, the ground from which man is formed.

“Whole face” should be retained as the verse's comprehensive expression. The text does not supply a map, measurement, or modern geographic boundary by which to calculate the extent.

**Direct finding:** The described watering extends to “the whole face of the ground”.

**Not admitted:** A claim that this phrase specifies a measurable modern planetary coverage model or a particular hydrological boundary.

## Relationship between Genesis 2:5 and Genesis 2:6

Genesis 2:5 states that the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth.

Genesis 2:6 then records a mist going up from the earth and watering the whole face of the ground.

These statements must both be retained.

The distinction is direct:

- Verse 5 names rain and says it had not been caused.
- Verse 6 names a mist, says it went up from the earth, and says it watered the ground.

Therefore, absence of rain is not equivalent in this passage to absence of water or absence of watering.

The passage does not tell us whether the mist was vapor that condensed, a spring or stream rising from below, or another form of water supply. The text's explicit contribution is the stated origin, movement, and watering result.

The NET Bible note offers the subterranean-spring interpretation as a lexical and contextual possibility. Other translation traditions preserve mist or cloud language. These differences should remain visible rather than be resolved by preference.

**Admitted:** Verse 6 describes watering without describing it as rain.

**Not admitted:** The claim that the mist was necessarily rainfall, or that the ground was entirely dry before it.

**Unresolved:** The exact physical identity of *ʾēḏ* and the mechanism by which it watered the ground.

## Relationship to Genesis 2:7

Genesis 2:7 (KJV):

> And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

The word translated “ground” in verses 6 and 7 is *ʾădāmāh*. The repeated term creates a direct textual connection between the ground that is watered and the ground from which man is formed.

The sequence records:

1. The ground is watered in verse 6.
2. The LORD God forms man from the dust of the ground in verse 7.
3. The LORD God breathes into man's nostrils the breath of life.
4. Man becomes a living soul.

The repeated ground terminology is an observation. It is not, by itself, proof that the mist was sent specifically to prepare material for man's formation. That interpretation appears in later Jewish commentary, including Rashi, but Genesis 2:6 does not explicitly state that purpose.

The passage does explicitly identify the LORD God as the One who forms man and breathes life into him. The mist is not identified as the source of man's life.

**Direct finding:** The ground watered in verse 6 is named again as the ground from whose dust man is formed in verse 7.

**Not admitted:** The claim that the mist itself gave man life or that verse 6 explicitly states that its purpose was to prepare the dust for man's formation.

**Unresolved:** Whether the literary placement of the watering immediately before man's formation carries an additional intended connection beyond the repeated ground terminology.
