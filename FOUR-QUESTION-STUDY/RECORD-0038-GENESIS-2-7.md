# Study Record 0038 — Genesis 2:7 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:7 (KJV)**

> And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

## Question under examination

What does Genesis 2:7 distinguish between the LORD God forming man from the dust of the ground and breathing into his nostrils the breath of life? What does the text identify as the result? What does the verse state, and what must not be added beyond its wording?

The study must preserve the KJV wording, the order of the clauses, and the LORD God as the subject of both actions. It must distinguish the material named in the formation, the breath named in the second action, and the resulting description of man. It must not impose a later body/soul theory or a scientific mechanism where the verse itself does not supply one.

**Genesis 2:5–8 (KJV):**

The immediate sequence places the formation of man after the description of the ground and its watering. Verse 8 then records the LORD God planting a garden and placing the man there.

Genesis 1:26–27 has already recorded God's purpose and act concerning humanity:

**Genesis 1:26 (KJV)**

Genesis 2:7 gives a more particular account of man's formation and becoming a living soul. The two passages must be read together without making either passage say what it does not say.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “Formed” — יָצַר (*yāṣar*)

The verb *yāṣar* means to form, fashion, or shape. It is used of a potter's forming work in other Scriptural passages, including Isaiah 29:16 and Jeremiah 18:2–6.

In Genesis 2:7, the LORD God is the subject, man is the object, and dust of the ground is the material named.

The potter imagery in other passages can help explain the ordinary semantic range of the verb, but Genesis 2:7 does not explicitly say that God used a potter's wheel or any particular physical process.

**Direct finding:** The LORD God formed man.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the verb specifies a modern biological or chemical mechanism.

### 2. “Man” — אָדָם (*ʾādām*)

The word *ʾādām* can refer to man or humanity and, in context, to the man formed in this account.

The word is closely associated in the passage with *ʾădāmāh*, ground. This relationship is visible in the Hebrew wording and is reflected in the KJV's “man” and “ground”.

The word relationship is relevant, but it does not independently prove every proposed theological meaning of humanity's relationship to the earth.

**Direct finding:** The one formed is man.

**Supported observation:** The Hebrew wording places *ʾādām* (man) alongside *ʾădāmāh* (ground).

### 3. “Dust” — עָפָר (*ʿāp̄ār*)

The noun *ʿāp̄ār* means dust, dry earth, or loose soil, depending on context.

Genesis 2:7 identifies dust of the ground as the material from which the LORD God formed man.

Other passages use dust language for human mortality and creaturely dependence, including Genesis 3:19 and Ecclesiastes 3:20. Those later references may illuminate canonical themes, but they should not be substituted for the direct statement in Genesis 2:7.

**Direct finding:** Dust of the ground is the material named.

**Not admitted:** A claim that this verse gives a complete chemical inventory of man's physical composition.

### 4. “Breathed” — נָפַח (*nāpaḥ*)

The verb *nāpaḥ* means to breathe, blow, or puff. In Genesis 2:7 it describes the LORD God's action of breathing into man's nostrils.

The text does not describe an independent natural force acting upon the man. It names the LORD God as the One who breathes.

The verb itself does not identify the breath as a separate person, a detachable substance, or a technical biological process. Those are additional claims that would require other evidence and careful Scriptural examination.

**Direct finding:** The LORD God breathed into man's nostrils.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the verb alone establishes a complete doctrine of the Holy Spirit or the soul.

### 5. “Breath of life” — נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים (*nišmat ḥayyîm*)

The expression is made from *nešāmāh* (breath) and *ḥayyîm* (life/lives). The KJV renders it “the breath of life”.

The wording identifies what God breathes into man's nostrils. It does not say that the dust itself generated this breath.

Related expressions appear in Genesis 6:17 and 7:15, 22, where breath of life is associated with living creatures. Job 33:4 also speaks of the Spirit of God making and the breath of the Almighty giving life. These are relevant canonical comparisons, but each passage must retain its own wording and context.

Genesis 2:7 does not explicitly equate the expression “breath of life” with the Holy Spirit as a technical identity. Nor does it explain the metaphysical composition of life.

**Direct finding:** The LORD God breathes the breath of life into man's nostrils.

**Unresolved:** The full theological relationship among breath, life, spirit, and soul across Scripture.

### 6. “Man became a living soul” — נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה (*nepeš ḥayyāh*)

The expression *nepeš ḥayyāh* is commonly translated “living soul”, “living being”, or “living creature”, according to context.

The KJV uses “living soul” in Genesis 2:7. The same Hebrew expression is used in Genesis 1:20 for living creatures in the waters and in Genesis 1:24 for living creatures brought forth by the earth. It is therefore not accurate to claim that the expression, by itself, is used exclusively for human beings.

The verb in Genesis 2:7 is *hāyâ* (היה), “became” or “was”, in the form translated “became”. The result clause says that man became a living soul.

The verse does not say that God inserted a pre-existing, separately described soul into a body. It says that God formed man, breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and man became a living soul.

This observation does not settle every question about the soul elsewhere in Scripture. It does establish that the verse's own description is of man becoming a living soul.

**Direct finding:** The resulting man is described as a living soul.

**Not admitted:** A claim that *nephesh chayyah* alone proves a complete body/soul dualism, or that it is an expression exclusive to humanity.

## The order and relationship of the clauses

The verse presents three connected statements:

1. **Formation:** The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground.
2. **Breathing:** The LORD God breathed into man's nostrils the breath of life.
3. **Result:** Man became a living soul.

The grammar identifies the LORD God as the subject of the two actions. The final clause identifies man as the one who became a living soul.

The text does not describe the dust as an independent agent or the breath as a self-originating power. Nor does it provide a technical account of the transition.

It is important to preserve the distinction between what the verse explicitly says and what later theological systems may infer. The verse is foundational to discussions of human life, but it is not a complete treatise on every aspect of human nature.

**Admitted:** The two divine actions and the resulting living condition are distinguished in the verse.

**Unresolved:** The exact temporal interval, the physical process, and the full metaphysical relationship between breath, life, and soul.

## Relationship to Genesis 1:26–27

Genesis 1:26–27 identifies humanity as made in God's image and likeness and records God creating man, male and female.

Genesis 2:7 identifies the material and actions in the formation of the man described in the garden account.

These statements contribute different details:

- Genesis 1:26 records God's declaration concerning humanity and its appointment.
- Genesis 1:27 records God's creation of humanity in His image, male and female.
- Genesis 2:7 records the LORD God's forming of man from the dust, His breathing into man's nostrils, and man's becoming a living soul.

The passages should be read together. Genesis 2:7 does not cancel the image-bearing statement in Genesis 1:26–27, and Genesis 1:26–27 does not remove the specific formation and breath statements in Genesis 2:7.

**Direct finding:** Both passages attribute humanity's origin to God, while Genesis 2:7 supplies particular details about formation and life.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the image of God is simply the physical dust, the breath, or the phrase “living soul” alone. The text does not reduce the image statement to any one of those components.

## Relationship to the living creatures in Genesis 1

Genesis 1:20 and 1:24 use *nephesh chayyah* for living creatures. Genesis 2:7 uses the same expression for man.

This shared vocabulary matters. It prevents us from assigning the Hebrew phrase an exclusively human meaning merely because the KJV renders it “living soul” in Genesis 2:7.

At the same time, shared vocabulary does not erase the distinctive account of man's formation in Genesis 2:7 or the image-bearing statement in Genesis 1:26–27.

**Direct finding:** The phrase *nephesh chayyah* is used for both human and non-human living creatures in Genesis.

**Not admitted:** Either that humans and animals are identical in every respect, or that the phrase itself proves a complete distinction in their metaphysical constitution. Those conclusions require evidence beyond this shared expression.
