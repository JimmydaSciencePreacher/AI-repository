# Study Record 0040 — Genesis 2:9 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:9 (KJV)**

> And out of the ground made the LORD God to grow every tree that is pleasant to the sight, and good for food; the tree of life also in the midst of the garden, and the tree of knowledge of good and evil.

## Question under examination

What does Genesis 2:9 say the LORD God made to grow out of the ground? What distinctions does it make about the trees, including the tree of life and the tree of knowledge of good and evil? Where does the verse place them, and what remains to be learned about their roles?

The record must preserve the KJV wording and distinguish the general description of the trees from the two specifically named trees. It must not import the later command or events of Genesis 2:16–17 and Genesis 3 as though they were already stated in verse 9.

## KJV text and immediate context

**Genesis 2:8–10 (KJV):**

**Genesis 2:8**
> And the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden; and there he put the man whom he had formed.

**Genesis 2:9**
> And out of the ground made the LORD God to grow every tree that is pleasant to the sight, and good for food; the tree of life also in the midst of the garden, and the tree of knowledge of good and evil.

**Genesis 2:10**
> And a river went out of Eden to water the garden; and from thence it was parted, and became into four heads.

Verse 8 records the LORD God planting the garden and placing the man there. Verse 9 describes trees the LORD God made to grow out of the ground, including two trees identified by name or designation. Verse 10 begins the description of the river and its relation to Eden and the garden.

The immediate context therefore moves from placement of the man, to the garden's trees, to the watercourse. The verses do not yet give the command concerning the two named trees; that appears in Genesis 2:16–17.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “Made ... to grow” — וַיַּצְמַח (*wayyaṣmaḥ*)

The verb is from the root *ṣāmaḥ* (צמח), “to sprout” or “grow”. The form in the verse is commonly understood as a causative form: the LORD God caused the trees to sprout or grow.

The KJV renders the construction “made ... to grow”. The subject remains the LORD God, and the growth is attributed to His action.

**Direct finding:** The LORD God caused the trees to grow out of the ground.

**Not admitted:** A claim that this verb specifies a modern botanical mechanism, timescale, or growth process.

### 2. “Out of the ground” — מִן־הָאֲדָמָה (*min-hāʾădāmāh*)

The expression means “from/out of the ground”. It connects the growth of the trees with the ground, using *ʾădāmāh*, the same Hebrew noun for ground that appears in the preceding account of man's formation from the dust of the ground.

The shared word is a textual connection. It does not mean that the verse equates the formation of man with the growth of trees, or that the processes are identical.

**Direct finding:** The trees are said to grow out of the ground.

**Supported observation:** The same Hebrew term for ground occurs in the immediate account of man's formation and the garden's vegetation.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the shared noun establishes an identical mechanism for human formation and plant growth.

### 3. “Every tree” — כָּל־עֵץ (*kol-ʿēṣ*)

The phrase means “every tree” or “all [the] trees” in the described category. The verse gives a general description before identifying the two named trees.

The wording does not require the conclusion that only two trees existed. Nor does it say that the named trees exhaust the garden's vegetation.

**Direct finding:** The verse describes every tree in the stated category and then identifies the tree of life and the tree of knowledge of good and evil.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the two named trees were the only trees in the garden.

### 4. “Pleasant to the sight” — נֶחְמָד לְמַרְאֶה (*neḥmād le-marʾeh*)

The expression describes something desirable or pleasant in relation to sight/appearance. It identifies visual attractiveness as a quality of the trees in the general description.

The verse does not say that visual attractiveness is proof of moral goodness, nor does it say that appearance alone establishes whether a tree may be eaten.

**Direct finding:** The trees are described as pleasant to the sight.

**Not admitted:** A claim that visual appeal is equivalent to moral goodness or divine permission.

### 5. “Good for food” — טוֹב לְמַאֲכָל (*ṭōb le-maʾăkāl*)

The expression means “good for food” or suitable/good as food. It identifies food value or suitability as another quality in the general description.

The verse does not yet give the command concerning which tree the man may or may not eat. That instruction is stated in Genesis 2:16–17.

**Direct finding:** The trees are described as good for food.

**Not admitted:** A claim that this general description overrides or replaces the specific command given later.

### 6. “The tree of life” — עֵץ הַחַיִּים (*ʿēṣ haḥayyîm*)

The phrase means “the tree of life”. Genesis 2:9 names this tree and explicitly locates it “in the midst of the garden”.

The designation is significant, but verse 9 does not explain the tree's operation, whether eating from it is required, or the conditions under which its fruit may be eaten. Genesis 3:22 later refers to eating from the tree of life and living for ever. That later statement is relevant to the canonical examination, but it must not be mistaken for wording already present in Genesis 2:9.

**Direct finding:** The tree of life is named and placed in the midst of the garden.

**Unresolved in this verse:** The precise nature and operation of the life associated with the tree.

### 7. “The tree of knowledge of good and evil” — עֵץ הַדַּעַת טוֹב וָרָע (*ʿēṣ haddaʿat ṭôḇ wā-rāʿ*)

The phrase means “the tree of the knowledge of good and evil”. *Daʿat* is related to the Hebrew root *yādaʿ*, “to know”. The designation names the tree by reference to knowledge of good and evil.

Genesis 2:9 does not explain the full meaning of that knowledge, nor does it state the command concerning the tree. Genesis 2:16–17 supplies the command, and Genesis 3 narrates the subsequent encounter and eating.

The name of the tree must not be changed into “the tree of evil”. The wording explicitly includes “good and evil”.

**Direct finding:** The verse names the tree of knowledge of good and evil.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the tree itself is called evil, or that verse 9 alone fully explains the nature of the knowledge.

## General description and specifically named trees

The verse first gives a general description of the trees: pleasant to the sight and good for food. It then identifies the tree of life and the tree of knowledge of good and evil.

The grammar presents the two named trees within the garden's description. It explicitly locates the tree of life in the midst of the garden. It does not separately state in this verse that the tree of knowledge is also “in the midst”; its exact position is not specified here.

The general description should not be turned into a conclusion that every tree is interchangeable, or that the names of the two trees are merely decorative labels. The text deliberately identifies them.

**Admitted:** The verse distinguishes a general description of the trees from two specifically named trees.

**Unresolved:** The precise location of the tree of knowledge within the garden and the full significance of both tree designations.

## Relationship to Genesis 2:16–17

Genesis 2:16–17 (KJV):

> And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:
>
> But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

These verses provide the command not yet stated in Genesis 2:9. They distinguish the general permission concerning the trees of the garden from the specific prohibition concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

This later instruction must be examined in its own record. It should not be projected backward as though Genesis 2:9 already contained the command.

**Direct finding from the later passage:** God permits eating from every tree of the garden, with a specific prohibition concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

**Not admitted in this record:** A claim that Genesis 2:9 itself states the prohibition or explains the consequences of disobedience.

## Relationship to Genesis 3

Genesis 3 records the serpent's conversation with the woman, the woman's perception of the tree, the taking and eating of its fruit, and the man's participation.

That account is essential to understanding what follows, but the present record concerns what Genesis 2:9 states before the command and the later encounter are examined.

The tree's visual appeal and food suitability in Genesis 2:9 should not be collapsed into the later description of the woman's seeing that the tree was good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desired to make one wise (Genesis 3:6). The repeated ideas invite comparison, but the passages have distinct narrative functions.

**Admitted:** Genesis 3 later describes the woman's perception and action in relation to the tree.

**Not admitted:** A claim that Genesis 2:9 itself narrates or explains the Fall.
