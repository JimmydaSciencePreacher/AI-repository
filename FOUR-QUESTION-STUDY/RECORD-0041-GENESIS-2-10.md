# Study Record 0041 — Genesis 2:10 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:10 (KJV)**

> And a river went out of Eden to water the garden; and from thence it was parted, and became into four heads.

## Question under examination

The record must distinguish the direct wording from geographical reconstruction. It must not assume that the river's modern identity, the location of Eden, or the precise form of the four heads is established by this verse alone.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “A river” — נָהָר (*nāhār*)

The noun *nāhār* means river or stream. It identifies the watercourse in the verse.

The word does not by itself identify a modern river, determine its size, or establish its geological history.

**Direct finding:** The text introduces a river.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the noun alone identifies a modern river or a specific hydrological system.

### 2. “Went out” — יֹצֵא (*yōṣēʾ*)

The expression comes from the root *yāṣāʾ* (יצא), meaning to go out, come out, or proceed forth. The form in this clause describes the river as going out from Eden.

The wording supplies a directional relationship: the river goes out of Eden. It does not provide a compass direction or a measured route.

**Direct finding:** The river goes out of Eden.

**Not admitted:** A claim that this verb identifies the river's modern course or geographical coordinates.

### 3. “Of Eden” — מֵעֵדֶן (*mēʿēḏen*)

The preposition and name identify Eden as the place from which the river goes out. This is consistent with the preceding description of the garden as being in Eden (Genesis 2:8).

The verse distinguishes the river's relation to Eden from its stated function of watering the garden.

**Direct finding:** Eden is identified as the river's point of departure in the narrative.

**Unresolved:** The geographical extent and precise location of Eden.

### 4. “To water” — לְהַשְׁקוֹת (*lehašqôt*)

This infinitive is from the root *šāqâ* (שקה), meaning to give drink or water, and in this context to irrigate or water. The form expresses the river's stated function in relation to the garden.

The text does not describe the engineering or natural mechanism by which the water reaches every part of the garden.

**Direct finding:** The river is described as watering the garden.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the verse specifies irrigation channels, artificial infrastructure, or a modern water-delivery system.

### 5. “The garden” — אֶת־הַגָּן (*ʾet-haggan*)

The garden is the object of the watering action. This connects Genesis 2:10 directly to the garden planted in Genesis 2:8.

The verse does not say that the river waters all of Eden, the whole earth, or every region named later. Its stated object here is the garden.

**Direct finding:** The garden is what the river waters in this clause.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the verse explicitly describes the river watering the entire earth.

### 6. “From thence” — מִשָּׁם (*miššām*)

The expression means “from there”. It is a locative expression that points back to a place or point already mentioned in the discourse.

The immediate sentence describes the river going out of Eden to water the garden, then says “from thence” it was parted. The precise spatial point intended by “there” should not be fixed more narrowly than the wording and context permit.

The sentence does establish that the division follows the river's described movement to water the garden. It does not give a measured location for the division or a detailed map of the transition.

**Direct finding:** The river is described as being parted “from there”.

**Unresolved:** The precise geographical point intended by “there” and how the river's course should be represented spatially.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the division unquestionably occurs at a particular modern site or at a precisely measured point inside or outside the garden.

### 7. “It was parted” — יִפָּרֵד (*yippārēḏ*)

The verb is from *pāraḏ* (פרד), meaning to divide, separate, or part. The form is commonly understood as a passive or middle/reflexive form in this construction: the river is parted or divides.

The grammatical subject remains the river. The verse describes a division but does not explain the physical cause or mechanism.

**Direct finding:** The river is parted or divided.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the verb identifies a particular geological process, engineered structure, or modern river-capture event.

### 8. “Four heads” — אַרְבָּעָה רָאשִׁים (*ʾarbāʿāh rāʾšîm*)

The phrase means “four heads”. *Rōʾš* (ראש) commonly means head, top, chief, or beginning, depending on context. Here the KJV preserves the expression “four heads”.

The next verses identify these heads by name. The expression should be retained rather than silently replaced with a modern hydrological term that may carry assumptions not established by the passage.

The wording indicates four resulting heads associated with the division of the river. It does not by itself settle whether a modern hydrologist would classify each as a headstream, branch, channel, or source.

**Direct finding:** The river's division results in four heads.

**Not admitted:** A claim that “four heads” necessarily means four modern tributaries or four independent rivers in a technical classification.

## The sequence described by the verse

The wording presents the following sequence:

1. A river goes out of Eden.
2. The river waters the garden.
3. From there, it is parted.
4. It becomes four heads.

This is the order of the statement. It should not be altered into four rivers flowing into Eden, nor should the garden be described as the source of the river.

The sentence distinguishes the place from which the river goes out (Eden), the place it waters (the garden), and the four heads that follow the division.

The precise spatial relation of the division to the garden and Eden remains a matter for careful textual and geographical examination.

## Relationship to Genesis 2:11–14

The descriptions include regions and directional language. They are relevant to any geographical investigation, but they do not authorize a confident modern reconstruction without examining the text, historical place-names, and relevant evidence.

The name “Euphrates” is recognizable from later biblical and historical geography. That recognition does not, by itself, locate Eden or establish the modern identity of all four heads.

The four heads must be studied individually in the following record or records. This record does not decide their modern identities.
