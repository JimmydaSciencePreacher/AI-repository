## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “Pison” — פִּישׁוֹן (*Pîšôn*)

Pison is the proper name assigned to the first of the four heads. The text identifies it by name but does not provide an etymological explanation or modern geographical identification.

**Direct finding:** The first head is named Pison.

**Unresolved:** The name's precise derivation and the river's modern identity.

### 2. “Compasseth” — הַסֹּבֵב (*hassōvēḇ*)

The expression is related to the Hebrew root *sāḇaḇ* (סבב), with a range of meanings including going around, surrounding, turning about, or encircling. In the KJV it is rendered “compasseth”.

The wording describes Pison in relation to the whole land of Havilah. It does not supply a map or specify the precise route by which the river relates to the land.

**Direct finding:** Pison is described as going around or encompassing the whole land of Havilah.

**Unresolved:** The precise course and geographical sense of this relationship.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the verse supplies an exact modern river route or that “compasseth” must mean a fully closed loop in a technical geographical sense.

### 3. “The whole land of Havilah” — כָּל־אֶרֶץ הַחֲוִילָה (*kol-ʾereṣ ha-Ḥăwîlāh*)

The expression identifies the region as the whole land of Havilah. The text gives the land a proper name and associates Pison with it.

The passage does not define Havilah's boundaries or give coordinates. The name Havilah occurs elsewhere in Scripture, but any identification across passages must be examined in context rather than assumed to refer automatically to one precisely bounded location.

**Direct finding:** Pison is associated with the whole land of Havilah.

**Unresolved:** Havilah's precise location, extent, and relationship to other biblical uses of the name.

### 4. “Where there is gold” — אֲשֶׁר־שָׁם הַזָּהָב (*ʾăšer-šām haz-zāhāḇ*)

The clause identifies gold as present in Havilah. The following verse describes the gold of that land as good.

The text does not state how the gold occurs, whether in veins, alluvial deposits, or another geological setting. It does not specify quantity or purity by a modern assay.

**Direct finding:** Gold is present in the land of Havilah.

**Not admitted:** A claim about the deposit type, extraction method, quantity, or measured purity.

### 5. “The gold of that land is good” — וּזְהַב הָאָרֶץ הַהִוא טוֹב (*ûzĕhaḇ hāʾāreṣ hahîʾ ṭôḇ*)

The text makes an affirmative quality statement about the gold. The Hebrew adjective *ṭôḇ* means good, with its specific sense determined by context.

The verse does not state a modern purity percentage, carat value, chemical composition, or grading system. It is not legitimate to convert “good” into a numerical assay without additional evidence.

**Direct finding:** The gold is described as good.

**Unresolved:** The precise criterion or criteria intended by “good”.

### 6. “Bdellium” — בְּדֹלַח (*bĕḏōlaḥ*)

The KJV renders the Hebrew term *bĕḏōlaḥ* as “bdellium”. The term is rare, and its exact identification is uncertain. Traditional explanations have associated it with an aromatic resin or gum; other proposals have been made. The text itself names the material without defining its botanical source, physical composition, or modern commercial identity.

Numbers 11:7 also uses the word *bĕḏōlaḥ* in a comparison describing the appearance of manna. That cross-reference is relevant to the word's biblical usage, but it does not by itself settle the material's exact identity.

**Direct finding:** Bdellium is named among the notable materials associated with Havilah.

**Unresolved:** Its exact ancient material identity and the degree to which any proposed modern identification is secure.

**Not admitted:** A categorical claim that bdellium is a particular modern resin, plant product, mineral, or gemstone without adequate evidence.

### 7. “The onyx stone” — שֹׁהַם (*šōham*)

The Hebrew noun *šōham* is rendered “onyx stone” in the KJV here. Its exact mineralogical identification in ancient usage is not certain. Traditional translations have rendered it as onyx, but the ancient term should not automatically be treated as a fully established match to a single modern mineral species.

Other biblical occurrences of *šōham* can be examined for context, including its use among materials associated with priestly garments and sacred objects. Such comparisons may help establish the term's Scriptural usage, but they do not by themselves resolve its precise mineralogical identity.

**Direct finding:** The KJV identifies the material as “the onyx stone”.

**Unresolved:** The precise mineral or stone denoted by the Hebrew term in its ancient context.

**Not admitted:** A claim that the verse gives a modern mineralogical classification or chemical formula.

## Geography and materials: preserving the distinction

The passage combines a geographical statement and a resource description:

- **Geographical statement:** Pison is described as encompassing the whole land of Havilah.
- **Resource statement:** Havilah is associated with gold, bdellium, and the onyx stone.
- **Quality statement:** The gold is described as good.

These statements are related in the text, but they are not interchangeable. The named resources do not, by themselves, locate Havilah. Nor does a proposed location establish the identity of Pison unless the geographical evidence supports that identification independently.

The passage does not state that the materials are all found in the riverbed, that the river created the deposits, or that the river's course can be reconstructed from the resources alone.
