# Study Record 0047 — Genesis 2:17 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:17 (KJV)**

> But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

## Question under examination

What is the specific prohibition given by the LORD God? What does the name “the tree of the knowledge of good and evil” state, and what does it not yet explain? What is the force of “thou shalt not eat”? What does the Hebrew construction behind “thou shalt surely die” express? What does “in the day” contribute to the statement? How does verse 17 qualify the permission in verse 16, and what can be concluded without prematurely deciding every aspect of death?

The record must distinguish the command from its explanation, the prohibited action from its consequence, the Hebrew wording from later interpretation, and the direct statement from questions that require the subsequent narrative.

## KJV text and immediate context

**Genesis 2:15–17 (KJV)**

> 15 And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.  
> 16 And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:  
> 17 But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

Verse 15 records the man's placement and assigned responsibility. Verse 16 records the command granting broad permission to eat from the garden's trees. Verse 17 begins “But” and identifies a particular tree as excluded from that permission. It then gives the stated consequence for eating from it.

The passage therefore presents responsibility, permission, prohibition, and consequence in immediate sequence. These are observations about the wording and arrangement; they do not by themselves settle every later theological question about the nature or operation of death.

## Direct observations

1. The acting and commanding subject is “the LORD God”.
2. The command is addressed to the man, continuing the direct address in verse 16.
3. The verse begins “But”, contrasting the prohibition with the broad permission just stated.
4. The prohibited object is “the tree of the knowledge of good and evil”.
5. The prohibited action is eating from that tree.
6. The command is expressed directly: “thou shalt not eat of it”.
7. The verse gives a reason or explanation introduced by “for”.
8. The stated temporal expression is “in the day that thou eatest thereof”.
9. The stated consequence is “thou shalt surely die”.
10. The verse does not describe the physical appearance of the tree or identify its species.
11. The verse does not explain in this sentence how the tree produces or conveys knowledge.
12. The verse does not state that the tree itself is the originating authority over good and evil.
13. The verse does not say that the man is forbidden to know anything whatsoever; it names this tree and prohibits eating from it.
14. The verse does not identify the precise biological or physical mechanism of death.
15. The verse does not explain in this sentence every dimension of the death declared.
16. The verse does not state that the man dies physically at the instant of eating; nor does this observation settle whether death begins in another sense at that point.
17. The direct finding is that God prohibits eating from the named tree and declares certain death in connection with eating from it.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “But” — וּמֵעֵץ (*ûmēʿēṣ*)

The verse opens with a contrastive construction: “But of the tree...” The wording connects verse 17 to the broad permission in verse 16 while marking a particular exception.

**Direct finding:** The prohibition is presented as a qualification to the permission, not as an unrelated statement.

**Not admitted:** Reading the permission in verse 16 as if verse 17 were absent.

### 2. “The tree of the knowledge of good and evil” — עֵץ הַדַּעַת טוֹב וָרָע (*ʿēṣ haddaʿat ṭôḇ wā-rāʿ*)

The expression identifies the tree by the phrase “the knowledge of good and evil”. The Hebrew noun *daʿat* is related to the root *yādaʿ*, commonly associated with knowing. “Good” and “evil” are expressed by *ṭôḇ* and *rāʿ*.

The name is explicit in the text. The precise nature and scope of the knowledge, and why the tree bears this designation, are not fully explained in verse 17 itself.

The designation must not be silently converted into a claim that the tree is the source of moral truth, that knowledge itself is evil, or that the man was previously incapable of all discernment. Those are larger interpretive claims requiring further Scriptural examination.

**Direct finding:** The prohibited tree is identified by the text as the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

**Unresolved:** The full meaning and scope of this designation in the narrative and in the wider Scriptural context.

**Not admitted:** Treating the tree's name alone as proof that knowledge itself is evil or that the tree possesses independent authority.

### 3. “Thou shalt not eat of it” — לֹא תֹאכַל מִמֶּנּוּ (*lōʾ tōʾḵal mimmennû*)

The negative particle *lōʾ* with the verb form of *ʾāḵal* expresses prohibition: “thou shalt not eat”. The phrase *mimmennû* means “of it” or “from it”.

The statement is a direct command, not a suggestion or a prediction. The prohibited action is eating from the specified tree.

**Direct finding:** The man is forbidden to eat from that tree.

**Not admitted:** Replacing the stated prohibition with a claim that the man is forbidden to examine, name, or know anything at all. The verse names one prohibited action.

### 4. “For” — כִּי (*kî*)

The conjunction *kî* can introduce a reason, explanation, or ground for the preceding statement, according to context. Here the KJV renders it “for”, introducing the consequence attached to eating.

**Direct finding:** The death declaration is joined to the prohibition as its stated consequence or explanation.

**Not admitted:** Treating the consequence as an unrelated observation with no connection to the prohibited action.

### 5. “In the day” — בְּיוֹם (*bĕyôm*)

The expression literally contains the preposition *bĕ* (“in” or “on”) and *yôm* (“day”). The KJV renders it “in the day”.

The phrase establishes a temporal relation between eating and the declared consequence. The precise force of the expression in this sentence must be considered with the Hebrew construction and the subsequent narrative. The word *yôm* can refer to a day, and in some contexts it can function in a broader temporal expression. That general range alone does not settle its meaning here.

**Direct finding:** The consequence is explicitly connected to the time of eating by “in the day”.

**Unresolved:** Whether the phrase specifies the same calendar day in a narrowly chronological sense, functions idiomatically as “when”, or carries another nuance established by the immediate narrative and wider usage.

**Not admitted:** Deciding the question merely by asserting that *yôm* always means a twenty-four-hour period or always means an indefinite time.

### 6. “Thou shalt surely die” — מוֹת תָּמוּת (*môt tâmût*)

The Hebrew expression places the infinitive absolute of the verb *mût* (“to die”) before a finite form of the same verb. This construction commonly strengthens or emphasizes the verbal assertion. The KJV renders it “thou shalt surely die”.

The construction emphasizes the certainty of the declared death. It does not, by grammar alone, specify every dimension of death, the precise biological process, or the full timing of its manifestation.

The repeated verbal form should not be treated as two separate deaths simply because the verb appears twice. Its grammatical construction is an emphatic expression.

**Direct finding:** God declares death as the certain consequence connected to eating from the prohibited tree.

**Unresolved:** The full scope of death in the declaration, and how its temporal expression relates to the events subsequently narrated.

**Not admitted:** Claiming that the grammatical repetition by itself proves two separate deaths, or that it independently establishes a complete doctrine of spiritual and physical death.

## The relationship between permission and prohibition

Genesis 2:16 states:

> Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:

Genesis 2:17 continues:

> But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it...

The two verses must be read together. Verse 16 gives broad permission; verse 17 identifies a specific exception. The exception does not erase the provision, and the broad permission does not erase the boundary.

The command's source remains the LORD God. The man receives both permission and prohibition. The text does not present the man as the one who determines or revises the boundary.

**Admitted:** The passage presents provision and permission together with a specific prohibition.

**Not admitted:** Either of these reductions:
- “Everything is permitted,” by quoting verse 16 without verse 17.
- “Nothing is permitted,” by allowing the prohibition to erase the broad permission in verse 16.

## What the verse says about knowledge

The tree is named for “the knowledge of good and evil”. The verse prohibits eating from that tree; it does not give a complete definition of knowledge, good, evil, wisdom, discernment, or authority.

The name and prohibition are direct textual facts. Any explanation of what kind of knowledge is involved must be tested against the next events in Genesis 3 and other relevant Scripture. It must not be inserted into this verse before that examination.

In particular, the verse alone does not establish that:
- all knowledge is forbidden;
- the man had no capacity to understand God's command;
- the tree is an independent source of moral authority;
- the man was authorized to redefine good and evil.

These propositions go beyond the direct wording and require separate examination.
