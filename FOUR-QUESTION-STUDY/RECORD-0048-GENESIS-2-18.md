# Study Record 0048 — Genesis 2:18 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:18 (KJV)**

> And the LORD God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him.

## Question under examination

What does the LORD God declare “not good”? What does “alone” describe in this immediate context? What does God purpose to make? What does the KJV expression “an help meet for him” represent in Hebrew? What relationship does the verse establish between the man and the one God purposes to make? What is stated here, and what must be left to the following account?

The examination must distinguish God's assessment from His stated purpose, the man's condition from the proposed provision, and the meaning of the Hebrew expression from assumptions imported from later cultural arrangements.

## KJV text and immediate context

**Genesis 2:15–18 (KJV)**

> 15 And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.  
> 16 And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:  
> 17 But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.  
> 18 And the LORD God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him.

Verse 18 follows the account of the man's formation, placement, responsibility, and reception of God's command. The LORD God now declares that the man's being alone is “not good” and states His intention: “I will make him an help meet for him.”

The verse does not yet narrate the making of the woman. That account follows in Genesis 2:21–22. Between the declaration and that making, verses 19–20 recount the forming of beasts and fowl, their being brought to the man, his naming of them, and the statement that for Adam there was not found an help meet for him. Those details belong to their own examination and must not be silently inserted into verse 18.

## Direct observations

1. The speaking subject is “the LORD God”.
2. God makes an assessment: “It is not good”.
3. The condition identified as not good is “that the man should be alone”.
4. The verse does not say that the man himself is evil or defective.
5. God states His own intention: “I will make”.
6. The intended provision is described as “an help meet for him”.
7. The intended recipient or referent is “him”, the man in the immediate context.
8. The verse does not name the helper as “the woman” in this sentence.
9. The verse does not describe the helper's formation or material origin.
10. The verse does not state that the man independently requests this provision.
11. The verse does not describe the helper as a servant, possession, or lesser kind of human.
12. The verse does not state that the helper is identical to the man in every respect.
13. The verse does not provide a complete account of marriage, household roles, or later social structures.
14. The verse does not state that being alone is universally “not good” in every circumstance or for every person; its direct subject is the man in this narrative.
15. The direct finding is that God identifies the man's being alone as not good and declares His intention to make a corresponding help for him.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “And the LORD God said” — וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים (*wayyōʾmer YHWH ʾĔlōhîm*)

The verse records God's speech. The LORD God is the subject who makes the assessment and announces the intended action.

**Direct finding:** The assessment and purpose originate with the LORD God.

**Not admitted:** Presenting the proposed provision as an independently originated human plan.

### 2. “It is not good” — לֹא־טוֹב (*lōʾ ṭôḇ*)

The expression consists of the negative particle *lōʾ* (“not”) and *ṭôḇ* (“good”). It directly contrasts with the repeated divine assessment “good” in Genesis 1 and the collective “very good” in Genesis 1:31.

The wording is an evaluation made by God concerning the stated condition. It does not say that the man's created nature is evil, nor does it reverse the earlier declaration that God's completed Creation was very good. The subject of the evaluation is specified by the rest of the sentence: the man being alone.

**Direct finding:** God evaluates the man's being alone as “not good”.

**Not admitted:** Recasting the statement as “the man is not good” or as a declaration that the whole Creation has become defective.

### 3. “That the man should be alone” — הֱיוֹת הָאָדָם לְבַדּוֹ (*hĕyôt hāʾādām lĕbaddô*)

The phrase concerns the man's state of being alone. *Lĕbaddô* means “by himself” or “alone”. The verse identifies this condition as the subject of God's assessment.

The immediate narrative has already described the man in relation to the garden and his assigned work. The verse now identifies aloneness as the condition that God declares not good. It does not specify every dimension of that aloneness in this sentence. The following narrative provides further context.

**Direct finding:** The man's being alone is the condition God identifies.

**Unresolved:** The full scope of the condition in the narrative—companionship, correspondence, shared human life, and other implications—must be examined with verses 19–24 rather than assumed from one English word.

**Not admitted:** Treating the verse as a universal rule that no person may ever be alone, or as a complete psychological diagnosis.

### 4. “I will make” — אֶעֱשֶׂה (*ʾeʿĕśeh*)

The verb is from עָשָׂה (*ʿāśâ*), “to do” or “to make”. The first-person form expresses God's stated intention: “I will make”.

The subject of the making is God. The verse does not describe the process yet; it announces what God will do.

**Direct finding:** God declares that He will make the provision.

**Not admitted:** Assigning the originating act of making to the man or inventing a process not described in this verse.

### 5. “Him” — לוֹ (*lô*)

The pronoun means “for him” or “to him” in this construction. The intended provision is related to the man.

**Direct finding:** The provision is expressly oriented toward the man.

**Not admitted:** Treating this grammatical relation alone as a complete statement of ownership, status, or social hierarchy.

### 6. “An help” — עֵזֶר (*ʿēzer*)

The noun *ʿēzer* means help, aid, or assistance. It describes one who provides help. The word itself does not mean “servant” or “inferior”. Elsewhere in the Old Testament, forms of *ʿēzer* are used of help that comes from God (for example, Psalm 121:1–2); therefore, the word cannot by itself encode a lesser rank.

In Genesis 2:18 the term identifies the function or relation of the one God will make in response to the man's being alone. The specific form that this help takes is clarified by the following narrative.

**Direct finding:** God purposes to make a help for the man.

**Not admitted:** Translating or interpreting *ʿēzer* as inherently meaning a subordinate servant, an inferior being, or a mere instrument.

### 7. “Meet for him” — כְּנֶגְדּוֹ (*kĕnegdô*)

The expression is formed from *kĕ* (“as”, “according to”, or “corresponding to”), *neged* (“in front of”, “opposite”, “in the presence of”), and the suffix referring to him. In context it conveys a help corresponding to him, suitable for him, or facing him as his counterpart.

The KJV's “meet for him” uses the older English sense of “fitting” or “suitable”. It should not be read with the modern everyday meaning of “meet” as merely encountering someone.

The phrase contributes an important qualification: the intended help is not simply any aid, but one corresponding to the man. The precise relational implications should be developed from the account that follows, especially the description of the woman in verses 21–23.

**Direct finding:** The help is described as corresponding or suitable to the man—as a counterpart.

**Not admitted:** Reducing the phrase to “someone who works for him”, or claiming that it means an exact duplicate with no distinction.

### 8. The combined expression — עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ (*ʿēzer kĕnegdô*)

Taken together, the expression is commonly understood as “a help corresponding to him”, “a helper suitable for him”, or “a fitting counterpart”. The two parts must be kept together. *ʿĒzer* identifies help; *kĕnegdô* qualifies that help as corresponding to him.

The KJV “an help meet for him” is an older English rendering of this combined idea. Neither part should be discarded: the phrase is not merely generic assistance, and it is not merely similarity without the stated help relation.

**Direct finding:** God purposes a help who corresponds to the man.

**Unresolved:** The full form and relational significance of this correspondence are developed in the following verses and should not be exhaustively decided from verse 18 alone.

## The significance of God's assessment

Genesis 1 repeatedly records God's assessment that what He made was good, culminating in “very good” (Genesis 1:31). Genesis 2:18 then records a specific condition that God says is “not good”: the man is alone.

The statement does not identify a flaw in the Creator's work. It records God's own declaration that the man's solitary condition is not the completed relational arrangement He intends. God immediately states His purpose to make a corresponding help.

This is an observation about the text's sequence. It does not establish that the earlier Creation was unfinished in every respect, nor does it require the conclusion that the man was morally defective.

**Admitted:** The narrative distinguishes the man's being alone from the arrangement God now declares He will make.

**Not admitted:** Treating “not good” as evidence that God made an error, or that the man is himself evil.

## Relationship to the following verses

### Genesis 2:19–20 — the animals and naming

The next verses recount the LORD God's forming of beasts and fowl, bringing them to the man, and the man's naming of them. Verse 20 concludes that for Adam there was not found an help meet for him.

These verses must be examined in their own right. They provide narrative development after God's declaration in verse 18. This record does not assume that the animals are the intended counterpart or decide the precise function of the naming episode.

### Genesis 2:21–22 — the woman made

The subsequent account describes the LORD God causing a deep sleep to fall upon the man, taking from his side, closing up the flesh, and making a woman from what He took. God then brings her to the man.

This is the narrative realization following the announced purpose in verse 18. The details of formation belong to the next records.

### Genesis 2:23–24 — recognition and joining

The man speaks concerning the woman in verse 23, and verse 24 describes the joining of man and wife as one flesh. These statements are essential to the wider account of correspondence, relationship, and joining. They should not be compressed into the wording of verse 18 before each passage is examined.
