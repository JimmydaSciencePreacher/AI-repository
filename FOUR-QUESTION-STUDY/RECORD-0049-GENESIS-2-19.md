# Study Record 0049 — Genesis 2:19 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:19 (KJV)**

> And out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field, and every fowl of the air; and brought them unto Adam to see what he would call them: and whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof.

## Question under examination

What does the LORD God form, and from what? What does He do with the creatures He formed? What purpose does the verse state for bringing them to Adam? What does “to see what he would call them” mean in the sentence, and what must not be inferred about God's knowledge? What does Adam's naming establish? What is explicit in verse 19, and what must be left to the result stated in verse 20?

The examination must distinguish God's forming from His bringing, God's action from Adam's response, the stated purpose from speculative explanations, and the act of naming from claims about the full nature of language or authority.

## KJV text and immediate context

**Genesis 2:18–20 (KJV)**

> 18 And the LORD God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him.  
> 19 And out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field, and every fowl of the air; and brought them unto Adam to see what he would call them: and whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof.  
> 20 And Adam gave names to all cattle, and to the fowl of the air, and to every beast of the field; but for Adam there was not found an help meet for him.

Verse 18 records God's assessment that the man's being alone is not good and His stated intention to make a help corresponding to him. Verse 19 records God forming beasts and fowl from the ground, bringing them to Adam, and Adam naming the living creatures. Verse 20 summarizes the naming and states that no help corresponding to Adam was found among them.

This record examines verse 19 directly. Verse 20 is included to preserve the immediate context, but its distinct conclusion is not to be treated as if it were already stated in verse 19.

2. The stated material origin is “out of the ground”.
3. The creatures named in the first clause are “every beast of the field” and “every fowl of the air”.
4. God brings the formed creatures “unto Adam”.
5. The stated purpose phrase is “to see what he would call them”.
6. Adam is the one who calls or names the creatures in the reported action.
7. The verse states that whatever Adam called each living creature, “that was the name thereof”.
8. God forms and brings; Adam names. The actions and their subjects are distinguishable.

11. The verse does not explain the physical process by which God formed the beasts and fowl.
12. The verse does not provide a catalogue of species or a modern taxonomic classification.
13. The verse does not give the spoken sounds or language Adam used.
14. The verse does not explain whether the names were descriptive, functional, relational, or some combination.
15. The verse does not explicitly state that Adam's naming was a test, examination, or exercise in biological classification.
16. The verse does not state that God lacked knowledge of what Adam would call the creatures.
17. The verse does not say that the animals were created as possible helpers and then rejected; it reports their bringing and naming, while verse 20 states the result concerning a help for Adam.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “Out of the ground” — מִן־הָאֲדָמָה (*min-hāʾăḏāmâ*)

The phrase means “from the ground” or “out of the ground”. It identifies the ground as the stated material source in this clause.

The wording recalls Genesis 2:7, where the LORD God forms the man from the dust of the ground. The same broad ground-related vocabulary appears in both passages, although verse 19 names the beasts and fowl rather than repeating the details of man's formation.

**Direct finding:** The verse expressly connects the formation of these creatures with the ground.

**Not admitted:** Claiming that verse 19 gives a complete biological account of animal formation or that it specifies all material and temporal details.

### 2. “The LORD God formed” — וַיִּצֶר יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים (*wayyiṣer YHWH ʾĔlōhîm*)

The verb is from יָצַר (*yāṣar*), “to form” or “fashion”. It is the same verb family used in Genesis 2:7 for the LORD God's forming of the man from the dust of the ground.

The grammatical subject is the LORD God. The verse attributes the formation to Him, not to Adam or to the creatures themselves.

**Direct finding:** God is the actor who forms the beasts and fowl named in the verse.

**Not admitted:** Transferring the forming action to Adam or treating the verse as if it describes spontaneous self-formation.

### 3. “Every beast of the field” — כָּל־חַיַּת הַשָּׂדֶה (*kol-ḥayyat haśśādeh*)

The phrase identifies the beasts or living creatures of the field. The KJV renders it “every beast of the field”. The word *ḥayyâ* is associated with living creatures or animals; the surrounding expression specifies the field.

**Direct finding:** The verse includes the beasts of the field in the group God formed and brought.

**Not admitted:** Assigning modern species boundaries or a complete zoological taxonomy from this phrase alone.

### 4. “Every fowl of the air” — וְאֵת כָּל־עוֹף הַשָּׁמַיִם (*wĕʾēt kol-ʿôp haššāmayim*)

The phrase identifies the fowl or birds of the heavens/air. The KJV uses “fowl of the air”.

**Direct finding:** The birds of the air are also included in the creatures formed and brought to Adam.

**Not admitted:** Narrowing the phrase to a modern taxonomic class without further evidence.

### 5. “And brought them unto Adam” — וַיָּבֵא אֶל־הָאָדָם (*wayyāḇēʾ ʾel-hāʾāḏām*)

The verb is from בּוֹא (*bôʾ*) in a causative form, conveying that God brought or caused the creatures to come to the man. The destination is Adam.

The verse distinguishes the prior formation from the bringing. God does not merely describe creatures existing somewhere; He brings them to Adam in the narrative.

**Direct finding:** God brings the formed creatures to Adam.

**Not admitted:** Adding a route, distance, duration, or mechanism that the verse does not state.

### 6. “To see what he would call them” — לִרְאוֹת מַה־יִּקְרָא־לוֹ (*lirʾôt mah-yiqrāʾ-lô*)

The infinitive from רָאָה (*rāʾâ*, “to see”) introduces the stated purpose or perspective associated with bringing the creatures to Adam. The question phrase concerns what Adam would call them. The verb קָרָא (*qārāʾ*) means to call, name, or designate.

The wording reports God's bringing the creatures with reference to what Adam would call them. It does not, by itself, state that God was ignorant of the answer or needed information He did not possess. Such a conclusion would require an argument from more than the purpose phrase.

The phrase should be read as the narrative gives it: God brings; Adam names; the names are recorded as the names of the creatures.

**Direct finding:** The stated purpose of bringing the creatures is connected to Adam's naming them.

**Not admitted:** Claiming that “to see” proves divine ignorance, uncertainty, or a need to acquire knowledge from Adam.

### 7. “What he would call them” — מַה־יִּקְרָא (*mah-yiqrāʾ*)

The verb is from *qārāʾ*, “to call” or “name”. The verse focuses on Adam's act of calling the creatures.

This is an action attributed to Adam within an event initiated and arranged by God. It is not the same action as forming the creatures. The verse does not define the full linguistic content or method of Adam's naming.

**Direct finding:** Adam performs the naming action.

**Not admitted:** Treating the naming as Adam's creation of the creatures, or claiming that the verse supplies the full origin and structure of human language.

### 8. “And whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof” — וְכֹל אֲשֶׁר יִקְרָא־לוֹ הָאָדָם נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה הוּא שְׁמוֹ (*wĕḵol ʾăšer yiqrāʾ-lô hāʾāḏām nepeš ḥayyâ hûʾ šĕmô*)

The clause connects Adam's naming with the resulting designation: what he called the living creature was its name. *Šēm* means “name”; *nepeš ḥayyâ* is the expression “living creature” or “living being” in this context.

The text records the name as the result of Adam's act. It does not explain whether the names reveal an inherent essence, a function, a perceived characteristic, or another naming principle.

**Direct finding:** The names are attributed to Adam's naming, and the text says that the name he gave was the creature's name.

**Unresolved:** The nature, linguistic form, and semantic basis of the names.

**Not admitted:** Claiming that Adam created the creatures' identities or that the verse establishes a complete theory of naming and ontology.

## The distinction between forming, bringing, and naming

The verse presents three distinguishable actions:

1. **Forming:** The LORD God forms the beasts of the field and fowl of the air from the ground.
2. **Bringing:** The LORD God brings them to Adam.
3. **Naming:** Adam calls the living creatures, and the text records the resulting names.

The order and subjects matter. Adam's naming does not replace God's forming. God's bringing does not erase Adam's real participation in naming. The verse attributes each action to its stated actor.

This is a textual distinction. It does not by itself define every possible relation between divine sovereignty and human agency.

**Admitted:** The Creator forms and brings; Adam names the creatures presented to him.

**Not admitted:** Treating Adam as the source of the creatures, or treating his naming as an empty action with no stated result.

## “To see” and the question of divine knowledge

The phrase “to see what he would call them” must be preserved. It is part of the inspired narrative as presented in the KJV. But the wording does not state that God was unaware of what Adam would say.

A narrative can describe an action as being done “to see” an outcome without thereby defining the actor's total knowledge or asserting ignorance. The text's direct point is the occasion for Adam to name the creatures and the record of what he called them.

This record therefore does not infer either a limitation in God's knowledge or a detailed theory of divine foreknowledge from this phrase alone.

**Admitted:** The verse associates God's bringing with Adam's naming.

**Not admitted:** “God had to find out because He did not know.” That proposition is not stated.

## Relationship to Genesis 2:18 and 2:20

### Genesis 2:18 — purpose announced

God says that the man's being alone is not good and declares, “I will make him an help meet for him.” This is the announced purpose.

### Genesis 2:19 — creatures brought and named

God forms beasts and fowl, brings them to Adam, and Adam names the living creatures. This is the action recorded in the present verse.

### Genesis 2:20 — result stated

The next verse summarizes Adam's naming of cattle, fowl, and beasts, then states: “but for Adam there was not found an help meet for him.”

Verse 20 supplies an explicit result in relation to the help corresponding to Adam. This record does not move that conclusion backward into verse 19 or decide whether the animal-naming episode has additional functions beyond those stated.

**Admitted:** The narrative moves from God's announced purpose, to the bringing and naming of creatures, to the statement in verse 20 that no corresponding help was found for Adam among those named groups.

**Unresolved:** The full significance of this sequence and the precise narrative role of the naming episode.

## Related Scriptural context

### Genesis 1:24–25

God commands the earth to bring forth living creatures and makes the beasts of the earth according to their kinds. These verses provide the earlier Creation account of land animals. Genesis 2:19 gives a focused account involving the LORD God's forming of beasts and fowl from the ground and bringing them to Adam. The relationship between the two presentations should be examined across the complete passages rather than settled by one phrase in isolation.

### Genesis 1:26–28

Humanity is made in God's image, and God gives humanity dominion over the living creatures. This is relevant wider context for humanity's relation to other creatures. Genesis 2:19 itself, however, says that Adam names the creatures; it does not use the word “dominion” in this verse.

### Genesis 2:7

The LORD God forms the man from the dust of the ground. The shared formation verb and ground-related wording in Genesis 2:7 and 2:19 are relevant textual observations. They do not erase the distinct subjects and actions in the two verses.

### Genesis 2:18 and 2:20

These are the immediate purpose and result statements concerning a help corresponding to Adam. They frame the present naming episode.

No further cross-reference is needed to establish the direct wording of Genesis 2:19. Wider theological and scientific questions should be pursued only when the text or a subsequent question warrants them.
