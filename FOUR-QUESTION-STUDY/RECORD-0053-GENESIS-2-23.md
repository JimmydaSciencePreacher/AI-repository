# Study Record 0053 — Genesis 2:23 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:23 (KJV)**

> And Adam said, This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh: she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.

## Question under examination

What does Adam's “This is now” express? What do “bone of my bones” and “flesh of my flesh” state in this immediate context? What is the Hebrew wordplay between *ʾîš* (man) and *ʾiššâ* (woman)? What does Adam's declaration name, and what does it not establish about his role in the woman's making? How does his speech relate to the source statement in verse 22, and what must remain for the joining statement in verse 24?

The examination distinguishes God's making and bringing in verse 22 from Adam's speech in verse 23, the designation “Woman” from the personal name “Eve” given in Genesis 3:20, and the text's stated source relation from interpretations not established by the verse.

## KJV text and immediate context

**Genesis 2:18–24 (KJV)**

> 18 And the LORD God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him.  
> 19 And out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field, and every fowl of the air; and brought them unto Adam to see what he would call them: and whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof.  
> 20 And Adam gave names to all cattle, and to the fowl of the air, and to every beast of the field; but for Adam there was not found an help meet for him.  
> 21 And the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam, and he slept: and he took one of his ribs, and closed up the flesh instead thereof;  
> 22 And the rib, which the LORD God had taken from man, made he a woman, and brought her unto the man.  
> 23 And Adam said, This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh: she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.  
> 24 Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh.

Verse 18 records God's declared purpose. Verses 19–20 record the bringing and naming of creatures and the absence of a corresponding help for Adam. Verse 21 records God's taking from Adam. Verse 22 records God making and bringing the woman. Verse 23 records Adam's response to the woman brought to him. Verse 24 states the pattern of leaving, cleaving, and becoming one flesh.

The order is important: God makes and brings; Adam speaks; the narrator then states the one-flesh relation. This record examines Adam's speech and does not treat verse 24 as though it were part of his quoted words.

## Direct observations

1. Adam is the speaker.
2. Adam begins with “This is now” in the KJV.
3. He describes the woman in relation to himself with “bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh”.
4. He says she shall be called “Woman”.
5. He gives the reason: “because she was taken out of Man”.
6. The speech responds to the woman God has brought to him in verse 22.
7. Adam identifies a relation of shared bodily source or kinship; the verse does not describe the woman as an unrelated creature.
8. Adam's statement acknowledges that she was taken from man; it does not say Adam performed the taking or making.
9. The wordplay between “Man” and “Woman” is present in the Hebrew wording.
10. The verse does not give the woman's personal name “Eve”; that name appears in Genesis 3:20.
11. The verse does not itself state the “one flesh” formulation; that follows in verse 24.
12. The verse does not explicitly describe a marriage ceremony or provide a complete legal or social account of marriage.
13. The verse does not say Adam independently discovered or produced the woman; God has just brought her to him.
14. The verse does not explain Adam's mental process or provide a transcript of any prior conversation.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “And Adam said” — וַיֹּאמֶר הָאָדָם (*wayyōʾmer hāʾādām*)

The verb is from אָמַר (*ʾāmar*, “say”). The subject is “the man” (*hāʾādām*), rendered “Adam” in the KJV here. The verse now records the man's spoken response after God brings the woman to him.

**Direct finding:** Adam is the speaker and his words are a response within the narrated sequence.

**Not admitted:** Attributing the making or bringing to Adam, or treating his speech as the cause of the woman's existence.

### 2. “This is now” — זֹאת הַפַּעַם (*zōʾt happaʿam*)

The expression is literally along the lines of “this time” or “this one, at last”. The KJV renders it “This is now”. The demonstrative *zōʾt* (“this”, feminine) points to the woman, while *happaʿam* means “the time” or “this occasion”.

The expression is often understood against the preceding presentation of the animals and the statement that no corresponding help was found for Adam. Adam now identifies the woman as one who corresponds to him in a way the preceding creatures did not. The contrast is a contextual reading grounded in verses 19–22; the phrase by itself does not give a full account of Adam's inner thoughts.

**Direct finding:** Adam marks this presentation as distinct: “This is now” / “this one at last”.

**Contextual finding:** The expression naturally contrasts the woman with the creatures previously brought before him.

**Unresolved:** The full force of the expression's emotional or experiential nuance cannot be determined from this short phrase alone.

### 3. “Bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh” — עֶצֶם מֵעֲצָמַי וּבָשָׂר מִבְּשָׂרִי (*ʿeṣem mēʿaṣāmay ûbāśār mibbəśārî*)

The Hebrew pairs “bone” (*ʿeṣem*) with “flesh” (*bāśār*), and repeats the first-person relation: “of my bones” and “of my flesh”. The paired expression emphasizes close bodily kinship and shared source. It is consistent with the preceding account that God took from the man and made the woman from what He took.

The phrase should be read in its narrative setting. It is not merely a generic statement that all humans share flesh and bone; Adam says it specifically of this woman, immediately after the account of her being made from what was taken from him.

**Direct finding:** Adam declares a close bodily relation between himself and the woman, explicitly connecting his words to the source relation just narrated.

**Not admitted:** Claiming the phrase means the woman is a lesser copy, that the two are one physical person, or that it supplies anatomical details beyond the narrative.

### 4. “She shall be called Woman” — לְזֹאת יִקָּרֵא אִשָּׁה (*lĕzōʾt yiqqārēʾ ʾiššâ*)

The noun אִשָּׁה (*ʾiššâ*) means “woman” or “wife”, depending on context. The verb form from קָרָא (*qārāʾ*, “call/name”) is passive in form: “she/this one shall be called”. The KJV's “she shall be called Woman” communicates the designation.

This is a designation of the woman as “Woman” in relation to “Man” in the wordplay that follows. The verse does not provide her personal name as “Eve”. Genesis 3:20 later records Adam calling his wife's name Eve.

**Direct finding:** Adam declares that she shall be called *ʾiššâ* (“Woman”).

**Not admitted:** Treating “Woman” here as the personal proper name Eve, or concluding that the verse gives Adam creative authority over her existence.

### 5. “Because she was taken out of Man” — כִּי מֵאִישׁ לֻקֳחָה־זֹּאת (*kî mēʾîš luqăḥâ-zōʾt*)

The phrase begins with *kî* (“because/for”), giving the reason for the designation. *Mēʾîš* means “from man”. The verb *luqăḥâ* is a passive form of לָקַח (*lāqaḥ*, “take”): “was taken”. The demonstrative *zōʾt* (“this one”) refers to the woman.

The phrase explicitly links the designation to the taking from man already narrated in verses 21–22. Adam's speech recognizes the source relation; it does not claim that he took the material or made the woman.

**Direct finding:** Adam gives the reason for the designation: she was taken from man.

**Not admitted:** Reassigning the taking to Adam, or turning the passive statement into a claim about a process not described in the passage.

### 6. The wordplay between *ʾîš* and *ʾiššâ*

The Hebrew pairs אִישׁ (*ʾîš*, “man”) and אִשָּׁה (*ʾiššâ*, “woman”). The sound resemblance forms a wordplay in the narrative: the woman is designated in relation to the man from whom she was taken.

This is a literary and linguistic observation. The wordplay in the verse should not be made to carry a complete historical-linguistic theory of the origin of either Hebrew noun. Their precise etymological histories are a separate lexical question. The text's immediate point is the meaningful pairing of the terms in Adam's declaration.

**Direct finding:** The verse deliberately places *ʾîš* and *ʾiššâ* together in a wordplay that expresses their narrated relation.

**Unresolved:** The full historical etymology of the two nouns is not established by the wordplay alone.

## The sequence across Genesis 2:18–24

1. **Verse 18 — announced purpose:** God declares that He will make a help corresponding to the man.
2. **Verses 19–20 — comparison and absence:** Creatures are brought and named; no corresponding help is found for Adam.
3. **Verse 21 — taking:** God causes the deep sleep, takes from Adam, and closes the flesh.
4. **Verse 22 — making and bringing:** God makes the woman from what He took and brings her to the man.
5. **Verse 23 — recognition and declaration:** Adam speaks, identifies the bodily relation, and declares the designation “Woman”.
6. **Verse 24 — joining statement:** The text states that a man leaves father and mother, cleaves to his wife, and they become one flesh.

The text distinguishes God's action from Adam's response. Adam's speech recognizes the woman and her relation to him; it does not originate the woman or the preceding divine action.

**Admitted:** Adam's declaration is the narrated response to God's presentation, and the “Man/Woman” wordplay is tied to the stated source relation.

**Not admitted:** Claiming that Adam's words alone complete the explanation of marriage or the one-flesh relation. Verse 24 must be examined directly.

## Distinctions that must be preserved

### Recognition is not creation

Adam recognizes and speaks about the woman whom God has made and brought. His declaration does not make her exist.

### Designation is not the personal name Eve

“Woman” (*ʾiššâ*) is the designation in this verse. Genesis 3:20 later records the personal name Eve. These textual moments must not be collapsed.

### Shared source is not identity collapse

“Bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh” expresses close relation and shared source in this narrative. It does not say that the man and woman are one person. The one-flesh statement comes in verse 24 and must be interpreted in its own wording.

### Wordplay is not a complete etymological proof

The *ʾîš / ʾiššâ* pairing is a clear literary feature. It does not by itself settle the historical derivation of the Hebrew nouns.

### Adam's speech is not the joining statement

Verse 23 records Adam's declaration. Verse 24 gives the narrator's statement about leaving, cleaving, and one flesh. Their sequence matters.
