# Study Record 0054 — Genesis 2:24 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:24 (KJV)**

> Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh.

## Question under examination

What does “therefore” connect? Is verse 24 still Adam speaking, or is it the narrator's statement? What do the Hebrew verbs translated “leave” and “cleave” mean? What does “his wife” identify? What does “they shall be one flesh” state, and what does it not state? How does this verse move from the first man and woman to a continuing pattern for mankind? How should it be read alongside Genesis 1:27–28 without importing later conclusions before examining the text?

This record distinguishes the wording of Genesis 2:24 from later interpretation, the first couple's narrated origin from the general statement about a man and his wife, and the meanings of the Hebrew terms from any proposed GUA-W synthesis.

Verse 18 records God's stated purpose concerning the man's aloneness. Verses 19–20 record the creatures brought to Adam, his naming, and the absence of a corresponding help. Verses 21–22 record God's taking, making, and bringing. Verse 23 records Adam's declaration. Verse 24 states a general pattern concerning a man, his wife, and their becoming one flesh. Verse 25 returns to the condition of the first couple.

The word “therefore” makes verse 24 an inference or consequence connected to the preceding account. The precise scope of that connection must be stated carefully: at minimum it links the one-flesh pattern to the account of the woman's being taken from the man and Adam's recognition of her as bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh.

## Direct observations

1. The verse begins with “Therefore” (*ʿal-kēn*), a connective expression commonly rendered “therefore”, “for this reason”, or “on account of this”.
2. The subject is expressed generically: “a man” (*ʾîš*), not only Adam by personal name.
3. The man is said to leave “his father and his mother”.
4. He is said to cleave to “his wife”.
5. The result is expressed as “they shall be one flesh”.
6. The verse moves from the first couple's origin narrative to a statement about the relation of a man and his wife.
7. The verse does not state that a man literally abandons or ceases all responsibility toward his parents.
8. The verse does not describe the one-flesh relation as the erasure of two persons into one person.
9. The verse does not specify a biological mechanism, a legal ceremony, or every social custom associated with marriage.
10. The verse does not use the later technical vocabulary of modern biology or systems theory.
11. The verse's speaker is not explicitly identified by a new “Adam said” or “the LORD said” formula.
12. The verse is later quoted by Jesus in Matthew 19:5 and Mark 10:7–8, and by Paul in Ephesians 5:31. Those quotations are relevant canonical witnesses but do not remove the need to examine the Genesis wording first.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “Therefore” — עַל־כֵּן (*ʿal-kēn*)

The expression *ʿal-kēn* is a common Hebrew connective meaning “therefore”, “for this reason”, or “on account of this”. It marks the following statement as connected to what precedes.

In this immediate context, the preceding account includes God's making the woman from what was taken from the man and Adam's declaration, “bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh”. Verse 24 then states a pattern of leaving, cleaving, and one flesh.

**Direct finding:** Verse 24 is presented as consequentially related to the preceding narrative.

**Contextual finding:** The shared-source and kinship language in verse 23 provides an immediate narrative basis for the one-flesh statement in verse 24.

**Not admitted:** Claiming that *ʿal-kēn* by itself identifies every theological, biological, or social reason behind marriage.

### 2. “Shall a man leave” — יַעֲזָב (*yaʿăzōb*), from עָזַב (*ʿāzab*)

The verb *ʿāzab* commonly means “leave”, “forsake”, or “depart from”, with its precise force determined by context. Here it is used of a man leaving his father and mother.

The verse does not say that parents are to be dishonoured, hated, or denied all care. Its immediate contrast is the formation of a new marital bond: the man leaves the parental household or primary family attachment in order to cleave to his wife. The exact social arrangements are not specified in the verse.

**Direct finding:** The man is described as leaving his father and mother in the context of forming the marital bond.

**Not admitted:** Interpreting “leave” as a command to abandon, mistreat, or permanently sever all relationship with parents.

### 3. “And shall cleave” — וְדָבַק (*wĕdābaq*), from דָּבַק (*dābaq*)

The verb *dābaq* means “cling”, “stick”, “adhere”, or “cleave”. It can describe close attachment or holding fast. Here the man cleaves to his wife.

The verb expresses a strong bond. It is not merely a momentary meeting or physical proximity. The verse places this attachment in direct relation to leaving the parental household or primary attachment.

**Direct finding:** The man is to hold fast to his wife; the verb expresses attachment.

**Not admitted:** Reducing *dābaq* to a purely physical act or claiming the verb alone defines every dimension of marital commitment.

### 4. “Unto his wife” — אֶל־אִשְׁתּוֹ (*ʾel-ʾištô*)

The phrase means “to his wife”. The noun *ʾiššâ* can mean “woman” or “wife”, depending on context; here the possessive form and marital context identify her as his wife.

The verse describes a reciprocal marital relation in which the man cleaves to his wife and “they” become one flesh. The result is not stated as the man absorbing or possessing the woman as an object; the plural subject “they” is significant.

**Direct finding:** The man cleaves to his wife, and the resulting statement concerns both of them.

**Not admitted:** Treating the wife as an object without agency, or deriving a complete theory of authority and social roles from this phrase alone.

### 5. “And they shall be one flesh” — וְהָיוּ לְבָשָׂר אֶחָד (*wĕhāyû lĕbāśār ʾeḥād*)

The phrase is literally “and they shall become/be for one flesh” or idiomatically “they shall be one flesh”. *Bāśār* means “flesh”; *ʾeḥād* means “one”. The plural subject “they” is followed by the singular expression “one flesh”.

The wording expresses a profound unity of the man and his wife. The immediate context includes the woman's being made from the man and Adam's “bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh”. The verse now describes the marital bond as a new one-flesh relation.

The text does not say that the two persons cease to be distinct individuals. Nor does it reduce the expression to sexual union alone, although bodily union is naturally relevant to the phrase and to the marital context. Its full scope is not exhausted by a single modern category.

**Direct finding:** The verse describes the man and his wife together as “one flesh”.

**Contextual finding:** The one-flesh statement develops the bodily kinship and shared-source language of verse 23 into the enduring marital relation described in verse 24.

**Not admitted:** Claiming that “one flesh” means two persons become one individual, that it erases personal distinction, or that the verse gives a complete scientific mechanism.

**Unresolved:** The precise range of the expression across bodily, kinship, covenantal, and household dimensions requires comparison with its other biblical uses.

## Who is speaking in verse 24?

Genesis 2:23 explicitly begins “And Adam said”. Verse 24 does not repeat “And Adam said”, nor does it introduce a new formula such as “And the LORD said”. The verse is therefore commonly read as a narrator's explanatory or authoritative statement following Adam's speech. However, the Hebrew text does not mark modern quotation punctuation, and the precise boundary of Adam's speech is not stated by an explicit closing formula.

Later Scripture quotes the verse in ways that attribute the statement to the Creator. In Matthew 19:4–5, Jesus says that the Creator made them male and female and then quotes the leaving-and-cleaving statement with “and said”. Mark 10:6–8 gives a closely related form. These passages are important canonical evidence for hearing Genesis 2:24 as an authoritative statement grounded in the Creator's design.

For this record, the careful textual conclusion is:

- Genesis 2:23 explicitly identifies Adam as the speaker.
- Genesis 2:24 has no fresh speaker formula.
- The verse functions as a generalizing statement that draws out the implication of the preceding origin account.
- Jesus later treats the statement as authoritative in explaining the Creator's joining of man and woman.

**Unresolved at the level of Genesis's immediate quotation boundary:** Whether the verse should be represented typographically as the final words of Adam or as narrator commentary. The theological authority and function of the statement should not be made to depend on an unmarked punctuation choice.

## From the first couple to a continuing pattern

The movement in the text is notable:

1. God makes the first woman from what He took from the first man.
2. God brings her to the man.
3. Adam recognizes her as bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh.
4. The text states that “a man” leaves father and mother, cleaves to “his wife”, and “they” become one flesh.

The wording is no longer limited to Adam and the woman as named characters. It articulates a pattern for the man and his wife. This is a generalizing statement rooted in the first couple's narrated origin.

Genesis 1:27–28 states that God created mankind in His image, created them male and female, blessed them, and told them to be fruitful, multiply, replenish the earth, and have dominion. Genesis 2:24 adds a focused account of the marital relation and one-flesh bond. These passages can be read together without forcing either to replace the other: Genesis 1 gives the broad creation statement and blessing; Genesis 2 gives a detailed narrative of the first man and woman and the marital pattern drawn from their origin.

**Admitted:** Genesis 2:24 moves from the first couple's origin to a general statement about a man and his wife.

**Not admitted:** Claiming that this one verse independently answers every question about marriage, household organization, procreation, or social practice.

## Distinctions that must be preserved

### Leaving is not dishonouring

The verse identifies a changed primary attachment in marriage. It does not command contempt or abandonment of parents.

### Cleaving is not mere proximity

The verb conveys close attachment or holding fast. Its meaning should not be narrowed to physical nearness alone.

### One flesh is unity, not identity collapse

The plural “they” remain the persons in the sentence, while the predicate expresses their unity as one flesh. The text does not say that two persons become one individual or lose all distinction.

### Origin narrative is not a complete mechanism

The account gives the source relation and the actions attributed to God. It does not specify every biological or anatomical detail.

### A general pattern is not an exhaustive social code

The verse gives a foundational marital pattern. It does not spell out every legal, cultural, pastoral, or household implication.

### Textual punctuation is not the original speaker formula

Modern quotation marks and paragraphing are editorial aids. The Hebrew verse itself does not insert a new explicit speaker label at verse 24.
