# Study Record 0055 — Genesis 2:25 (KJV)

## Subject / Scripture

**Genesis 2:25 (KJV)**

> And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed.

## Question under examination

Who are “they”? What does “both” emphasize? What does the Hebrew word translated “naked” mean? What does “not ashamed” state in this context? How does verse 25 relate to the one-flesh statement in verse 24? What condition does the verse record before Genesis 3, and what must not be imported into it from the later account of the Fall?

This record examines the verse as the closing statement of Genesis 2. It distinguishes physical nakedness from shame, the condition described before the transgression from the changed awareness narrated in Genesis 3:7, and direct textual observations from theological or architectural synthesis.

## KJV text and immediate context

**Genesis 2:24–25 (KJV)**

> 24 Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh.  
> 25 And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed.

Genesis 2:24 states the one-flesh relation. Verse 25 then describes the man and his wife together: both are naked, and neither is ashamed. The verse closes the chapter's account before Genesis 3 narrates the serpent's temptation, the eating, and the changed response to nakedness.

The verse does not say that nakedness is itself evil, nor does it say that the couple had no awareness of their bodies. It states that they were naked and were not ashamed.

## Direct observations

1. The verse refers to two persons: “they”.
2. “Both” explicitly includes the man and his wife.
3. The verse identifies them as “the man and his wife”.
4. Their physical condition is described as naked.
5. Their response or condition with respect to shame is described negatively: “were not ashamed”.
6. The verse follows immediately after the one-flesh statement in verse 24.
7. The verse is the final statement of Genesis 2 and precedes the events narrated in Genesis 3.
8. The verse does not describe either person hiding, covering themselves, or accusing the other.
9. The verse does not state that nakedness is sinful.
10. The verse does not explain every possible meaning of shame or provide a psychological description of their inner experience.
11. The verse does not say they were unaware of being naked; it says they were not ashamed.
12. The verse does not itself state when or how shame first arose; Genesis 3:7–10 narrates the later change in their response.

## Examination of the Hebrew wording

### 1. “And they were both” — וַיִּהְיוּ שְׁנֵיהֶם (*wayyihyû šĕnêhem*)

The verb *wayyihyû* is from הָיָה (*hāyâ*, “be/become”), here rendered “were”. *Šĕnêhem* means “the two of them” or “both of them”.

The wording explicitly includes the pair. The statement is not about the man alone, nor the woman alone, but about both together.

**Direct finding:** The condition described applies to both the man and his wife.

**Not admitted:** Treating “both” as evidence that their individual identities disappear. The grammar identifies two persons.

### 2. “Naked” — עֲרוּמִּים (*ʿărummîm*)

The adjective *ʿărummîm* means “naked” or “unclothed”. It describes their physical condition in the scene.

An important nearby Hebrew wordplay occurs in Genesis 2:25–3:1. Genesis 2:25 uses *ʿărummîm* (“naked”), while Genesis 3:1 describes the serpent as *ʿārûm* (“subtil” in the KJV; crafty or shrewd). The words are similar in sound but differ in form and meaning. The proximity creates a literary wordplay often noted in Hebrew interpretation. It should not be confused as if “naked” and “crafty” were the same word or carried the same meaning.

**Direct finding:** The man and his wife are described as physically naked.

**Contextual observation:** The sound relationship between *ʿărummîm* in 2:25 and *ʿārûm* in 3:1 forms a literary transition into the serpent narrative.

**Not admitted:** Claiming that the wordplay proves the couple's nakedness was caused by, or morally equivalent to, the serpent's craftiness.

### 3. “And were not ashamed” — וְלֹא יִתְבֹּשָׁשׁוּ (*wĕlōʾ yitbōšāšû*)

The verb is related to בּוֹשׁ (*bôš*), “be ashamed”, “feel shame”, or “be put to shame”. The form in the verse is a reflexive/intensive stem, and the phrase means that they were not ashamed.

The statement describes the absence of shame in their naked condition. It does not explicitly explain whether this absence is due to innocence, trust, the absence of wrongdoing, the absence of hostile judgment, or some combination. Those may be theological interpretations, but the verse itself gives the condition rather than a full causal explanation.

**Direct finding:** They were naked and were not ashamed.

**Not admitted:** Replacing the verse's wording with a claim that they were incapable of shame, had no moral awareness, or had no awareness of their nakedness.

**Unresolved:** The precise experiential and theological dimensions of “not ashamed” require comparison with the later narrative and broader biblical uses of shame.

### 4. “The man and his wife” — הָאָדָם וְאִשְׁתּוֹ (*hāʾādām wĕʾištô*)

The verse identifies the pair as “the man and his wife”. The wording carries forward the marital relation stated in verse 24. The pair are not merely two unnamed naked individuals; they are the man and his wife within the account's one-flesh context.

**Direct finding:** The nakedness and absence of shame are described within the relationship of the man and his wife.

**Not admitted:** Reading the phrase as a complete account of every later social or legal aspect of marriage.

## Relationship to Genesis 2:24

Verse 24 states: “they shall be one flesh.” Verse 25 immediately describes “they” as the man and his wife, both naked and not ashamed.

The repetition of the pair links the marital unity of verse 24 with the condition of the first couple in verse 25. The text places bodily nakedness and absence of shame within the setting of their relationship.

**Admitted:** Verse 25 follows and complements the one-flesh statement by describing the condition of the man and his wife.

**Not admitted:** Claiming verse 25 is a technical definition of “one flesh”, or that one-flesh unity necessarily means the absence of all vulnerability, boundaries, or distinctions.

## Comparison with Genesis 3:7–10

**Genesis 3:7–10 (KJV)**

> 7 And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.  
> 8 And they heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day: and Adam and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden.  
> 9 And the LORD God called unto Adam, and said unto him, Where art thou?  
> 10 And he said, I heard thy voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.

The comparison shows a narrated change:

- Genesis 2:25: they are naked and are not ashamed.
- Genesis 3:7: their eyes are opened; they know they are naked; they make coverings.
- Genesis 3:8–10: they hide, and Adam says he was afraid because he was naked.

The text places this change after the eating described in Genesis 3:6. The sequence supports the observation that their relationship to nakedness, exposure, and shame changes within the narrative after the transgression.

Care is required with the wording. Genesis 3:7 says they “knew that they were naked”; it does not say they had never previously perceived their bodies. Genesis 2:25 says they were not ashamed; Genesis 3 narrates awareness, covering, fear, and hiding. The contrast is about the changed condition and response, not proof that the couple previously lacked ordinary bodily perception.

**Direct finding:** Genesis 2:25 records nakedness without shame; Genesis 3:7–10 records knowledge of nakedness, self-covering, fear, and hiding after the eating.

**Contextual finding:** The narrative presents a change in the couple's condition and response after the transgression.

**Not admitted:** Claiming the physical body or nakedness itself became evil, or that Genesis 3 describes a biological alteration of their bodies in these verses.

## Distinctions that must be preserved

### Nakedness is not itself declared sinful

Genesis 2:25 describes nakedness without condemnation. The verse does not identify the body as evil.

### Not ashamed is not the same as unaware

The verse says they were not ashamed. It does not say they lacked bodily awareness or could not perceive their condition.

### Shame and physical condition are distinct

Genesis 3 describes the couple as still bodily naked, while their response changes: they cover themselves and hide. The narrative distinguishes the physical condition from the response to it.

### The change follows the transgression in the narrative

Genesis 3:7–10 follows the eating in verse 6. The order is explicit. The precise theological account of shame should be developed from the complete passage, not assumed in advance.

### Two persons remain identifiable

The verse says “they were both” and identifies “the man and his wife”. Their unity in verse 24 does not erase their distinction.

### Literary wordplay is not identity of meaning

The similarity between *ʿărummîm* (“naked”) and *ʿārûm* (“crafty/subtil”) is a literary feature, not evidence that the words mean the same thing.
