# Guide 03 — Sources and Evidence

## Purpose

This guide describes how research records identify sources and handle evidence. Its aim is traceability: a reader should be able to tell where a statement came from and whether it is a source claim, an observation, an interpretation, or an inference.

## Source categories

A study may use several kinds of material:

- **Primary source:** the original text, publication, dataset, experimental report, record, or direct observation under examination.
- **Secondary source:** analysis or interpretation by another author.
- **Reference repository:** a GUA-W repository consulted for established project material, definitions, prior studies, or context.
- **Measurement or dataset:** recorded values and the method or instrument by which they were obtained.
- **AI-generated analysis:** summaries, comparisons, calculations, or hypotheses produced during the investigation. These are analytical outputs, not independent primary evidence.

Identify the category when it affects how a claim should be evaluated.

## Recording a source

For each important source, record as many of the following as are applicable:

- author, organization, or originating body;
- title;
- publication or release date;
- publisher, journal, repository, or host;
- stable URL, DOI, repository path, or other locator;
- version, edition, chapter, section, page, verse, or dataset identifier;
- date accessed when the source may change;
- the specific claim or material for which it is being used.

For Scripture, identify the translation and the passage. When wording matters, quote accurately and retain enough context to avoid changing the meaning.

## Evidence versus interpretation

Keep these levels distinct:

1. **Source statement:** what a source explicitly says.
2. **Observation:** what was directly observed or measured.
3. **Reported result:** what an author says their method or experiment found.
4. **Interpretation:** an explanation of the source or result.
5. **Inference:** a conclusion drawn by connecting evidence and reasoning.
6. **Hypothesis:** a proposal that remains to be tested or further examined.
7. **Analogy:** a comparison that may clarify a relationship but does not, by itself, establish a shared mechanism.

A source can support one level without supporting a stronger one. For example, a paper may report a correlation without demonstrating causation.

## Evaluating scientific evidence

When relevant, examine:

- the research question and method;
- sample, measurement, and controls;
- assumptions and operational definitions;
- uncertainty, error, and limitations;
- whether the result has been replicated or independently evaluated;
- the difference between data, model, and interpretation;
- the range within which the result applies.

Do not treat a model as a direct observation of everything it represents. Do not generalize beyond the source's scope.

## Evaluating biblical material

When Scripture is central, record the translation and context. Examine the passage in its literary and immediate context, attend to relevant wording and repeated terms, and distinguish the text itself from theological interpretation or a proposed architectural mapping.

A proposed connection should not be represented as the literal wording of a verse unless the verse actually says it.

## Conflicting sources

When sources disagree:

- describe the disagreement;
- compare their dates, methods, scope, and assumptions;
- identify whether one source directly addresses the other's claim;
- state what can and cannot be resolved from the available material.

Do not conceal a conflict by selecting only the source that agrees with a preferred interpretation.

## Citations and quotations

Cite claims close to where they are discussed. Use direct quotations sparingly and accurately. Mark paraphrases as paraphrases when ambiguity is possible. Preserve exact technical terms, units, and verse references where they matter.

## Evidence status

Useful labels include:

- **Established in source:** directly stated or demonstrated within the cited source's scope.
- **Corroborated:** supported by multiple relevant sources.
- **Contested:** materially disputed by credible sources or evidence.
- **Interpretation:** an explanatory reading of source material.
- **Working hypothesis:** a testable or investigable proposal not yet established.
- **Analogy:** a comparison, not evidence of identity or mechanism.
- **Unresolved:** insufficient basis to decide.

These labels describe the status of a claim in a particular study; they are not permanent rankings of truth.
