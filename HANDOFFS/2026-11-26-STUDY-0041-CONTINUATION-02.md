## Repository change

Added Section 25, “Asking, commanding, and acting: how delegated authority is exercised.”

The section examines:
- Asking in Jesus' name: John 14:12–14; John 15:7; 1 John 5:14–15.
- Commanding in particular narrated events: Acts 3:6 and Acts 16:18.
- The commission of the Twelve: Matthew 10:1–15 and Luke 9:1–6, retaining the immediate mission context and Matthew 10:5–6's stated scope.
- The sending of the Seventy (or Seventy-two, noting the textual variation): Luke 10:1–20.
- Prayer for boldness and subsequent speech: Acts 4:23–31.
- Ananias's specific assignment: Acts 9:10–18.
- Gifts distributed by the Spirit: 1 Corinthians 12:4–11; Hebrews 2:3–4.

## Principal findings

1. Asking, commanding, and acting under commission are distinguishable expressions in the texts, not competing sources of authority or interchangeable techniques.
2. Jesus calls, authorizes, instructs, and sends; the servant does not self-appoint or enlarge a commission into unrestricted authority.
3. The Twelve's mission in Matthew 10 has a stated immediate scope; it must not be silently universalized.
4. Luke 10's disciples are redirected from rejoicing in authority over spirits to rejoicing that their names are written in heaven.
5. Acts records particular events and assignments; those narratives should not be converted into a guarantee that every believer can command every desired outcome.
6. 1 Corinthians 12:11 locates the distribution of gifts in the Spirit's will; gifts are for the common good, not personal ownership or boasting.
7. The integrated synthesis recorded in the study is: “The disciple asks in dependence, acts in obedience, and speaks under the commission actually given. Christ remains Lord; the Spirit remains the giver; the vessel remains accountable.”
8. No GUA-W layer, operation, state, or definition was added or changed. The Triple Constraint assessment treats the passages as theological/textual claims and makes no unsupported scientific mechanism claim.
