## 3. Isaiah 46:9–10 — the next Isaiah passages identified by GUA-W

Study 0004 had already recorded Isaiah 46:9–10 as a GUA-W anchor concerning the LORD's declaration of the end from the beginning and the standing of His counsel.

The authoritative study **Human Will, Creaturely Capacity, and the Creator's Authority — Job 38–41, Isaiah 40–48, Colossians 2:8, and Romans 1** does not isolate Isaiah 46 from its wider Scriptural setting. Section 4 examines Isaiah 40–48 as a connected witness to the Creator/creature distinction.

### 3.1 The specific further Isaiah passages

Section 4.6 explicitly brings forward:

- **Isaiah 48:12–13** — the LORD identifies Himself as the first and the last; His hand laid the foundation of the earth and His right hand spread out the heavens.
- **Isaiah 48:17** — the LORD identifies Himself as Israel's Redeemer, the One who teaches them to profit and leads them in the way they should go.

These are added as further GUA-W-identified Scriptures in the Isaiah branch.

### 3.2 The wider Isaiah context retained by the source

The same source examines the wider unit **Isaiah 40–48**, with these particular passages:

- **Isaiah 40:12–14, 18–20, 21–31** — the incomparable Creator, the heavens, the created host, and the everlasting LORD.
- **Isaiah 41:1–7, 8–29** — the challenge to rival claims and the contrast between the LORD and powerless idols.
- **Isaiah 42:5, 8** — the LORD as Creator and the declaration that He will not give His glory to another or His praise to graven images.
- **Isaiah 43:10–13** — the LORD's uniqueness and Israel called as a witness.
- **Isaiah 44:6–20** — the first and the last, the challenge to rival claims, and the maker of an idol who mistakes a creaturely product for a god.
- **Isaiah 45:7–12, 18–23** — the LORD's sovereignty, the potter and clay, the Maker of the earth and Creator of man, and the call to look unto Him and be saved.
- **Isaiah 46:1–11** — the contrast between idols that must be carried and the LORD who carries His people, declares the end from the beginning, and whose counsel stands.
- **Isaiah 48:12–13, 17** — the first and the last, the Creator of heaven and earth, and Israel's Redeemer and Teacher.

The full Isaiah 40–48 unit is preserved as the context of this GUA-W investigation. The individual references above are not presented as a newly invented linear sequence.

### 3.3 Explicit canonical comparison from Isaiah 44

Section 4.4 of the same GUA-W study explicitly says Isaiah 44's critique of the creature being treated as the Creator closely parallels **Romans 1:23–25**.

Romans 1:18–32 is already an established GUA-W text-first investigation. Its relevant passage is retained as a cross-link, not duplicated as a new independent Scripture.

The Isaiah study also proceeds to examine **Colossians 2:6–15**, especially 2:8, and explicitly recalls:

- **Colossians 1:15–20** — Christ as the image of the invisible God and the One by whom and for whom all things were created, in whom all things consist.
- **Colossians 2:3** — in Christ are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge.

These are further canonical passages named within the same GUA-W investigation of the Creator/creature distinction and knowledge under Christ.

## 4. Revelation 4:11 — the companion creation-witness passage

The authoritative **Chapter 3 — Theology and the Differentiated Sciences**, section “Theology and the ultimate WHY,” explicitly names the following governing Scriptural witnesses:

- Genesis 1–2
- John 1:1–3
- Colossians 1:16–17
- Hebrews 1:1–3
- Romans 11:36
- **Psalm 19:1–4**
- **Revelation 4:11**

The chapter states that these passages must be read in their own contexts and together direct the inquiry to God as Creator, the Word in relation to Creation, and the purpose and end of what God has made.

Accordingly, **Psalm 19:1–4 is added to the Scripture Placement register as a companion canonical witness explicitly named in the same GUA-W governing list as Revelation 4:11.**

A precision is necessary: the source groups Psalm 19:1–4 and Revelation 4:11 among its governing witnesses. It does not state that Psalm 19 is a direct textual cross-reference from Revelation 4:11, or that Revelation 4:11 is a direct textual cross-reference from Psalm 19. This study records their shared source placement without inventing that stronger claim.

### 4.1 Psalm 19:1–4

The passage speaks of the heavens declaring the glory of God and the firmament showing His handiwork. Day and night communicate knowledge, and their testimony is described as extending through all the earth.

The passage is retained in its own poetic and theological context. It is not converted into a scientific mechanism or a claim that every person interprets the created order correctly.

### 4.2 Revelation 4:11

The passage declares that the Lord is worthy to receive glory, honour, and power, because He created all things and they exist by His will and for His pleasure.

The GUA-W source places this passage alongside Ephesians 1:9–11, Romans 11:36, Hebrews 1:1–3, and Colossians 1:16–17 as complementary witnesses to God's authorship, will, purpose, and sustaining relation to all that exists. These passages are related, but their wording and contexts are not interchangeable.

## 5. Additional cross-links preserved from the Isaiah investigation

The GUA-W Isaiah study explicitly places the following passages into its wider canonical examination:

- **Job 38–41** — the Creator's questions concerning the foundations, boundaries, governance, and creatures of Creation.
- **Isaiah 40–48** — the LORD's uniqueness, creative authority, purpose, and the Creator/creature distinction.
- **Colossians 2:6–15** — the warning against being taken captive by teaching not according to Christ.
- **Colossians 1:15–20** — Christ in relation to Creation and its continuance.
- **Colossians 2:3** — the treasures of wisdom and knowledge in Christ.
- **Romans 1:18–32** — truth suppressed and the Creator exchanged for the creature.

These are recorded as a canonical comparison group in the source. The source does not claim that Job, Isaiah, Colossians, and Romans are identical in genre, scope, or argument. Their particular contexts must remain visible.
