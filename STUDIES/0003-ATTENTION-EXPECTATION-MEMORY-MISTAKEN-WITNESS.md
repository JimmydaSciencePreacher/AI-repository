## 1. Question

How can attention, expectation, and memory influence what a Witness later reports—and how can a witness distinguish what was directly encountered from what was inferred, remembered, or supplied by expectation?

This study asks:

- Does attention affect what is encoded or later remembered?
- Can expectation influence recognition and recollection?
- Can a person remember an event or detail that did not occur?
- How can a witness confuse the source of a remembered item?
- What safeguards improve the traceability of testimony?
- How should these findings inform AI research records without treating human memory and computational storage as identical?

## 2. Scope and boundary

## 3. Governing context

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

God alone is Original Cause. The Witness is derivative. The witness's attention, memory, expectations, and report do not create or alter the Source.

The question here concerns the reliability and limits of a witness's account. A memory can be sincerely reported and still be incomplete, distorted, or assigned to the wrong source. Sincerity and accuracy are not identical. A faithful research method therefore needs to preserve the difference between an original source, a witness's encounter with it, a later recollection, and the account ultimately communicated.

## 5. Source observations

### 5.1 Scripture: a report must be heard and examined

**Proverbs 18:13** warns against answering before hearing. It identifies a failure of premature response: the answer is given before the matter has been adequately heard.

**Proverbs 18:17** says the first party's cause may seem right until the other party comes and examines him. The verse directly cautions that an initial account can appear convincing while further examination changes the assessment.

**Deuteronomy 19:15–21** sets out requirements and procedures for testimony in a particular legal context, including examination when a witness is suspected of false testimony. The passage does not prescribe a modern psychological experiment, but it clearly does not treat an accusation as established merely because someone has voiced it.

**Exodus 20:16** prohibits bearing false witness against one's neighbour. The command concerns truthful testimony; it should not be reduced to a technical theory of memory error. It does, however, establish that the truthfulness of a witness's report matters.

### 5.2 Scripture: investigation and source traceability

**Luke 1:1–4** describes accounts already delivered, eyewitnesses and ministers of the word, the writer's careful understanding of matters from the beginning, and an orderly written account. Luke states the purpose: that Theophilus may know the certainty of the things in which he had been instructed.

The prologue distinguishes prior accounts, eyewitness testimony, investigation, arrangement, and the reader's certainty. It gives a scriptural example of an account that identifies its relation to earlier testimony and describes a process of careful inquiry.

**1 John 1:1–3** grounds the apostolic proclamation in what was heard, seen, looked upon, and handled. The account explicitly relates testimony to encountered subject matter. It does not present the witness as the origin of the subject.

### 5.3 Science: memory is reconstructive and can produce false recognition

Schacter and Slotnick review false recognition: a person may claim to have encountered an object or event that was not previously encountered. Their review examines cognitive, neuropsychological, neuroimaging, and electrophysiological findings. The key point for this study is not that all memory is unreliable, but that true and false recognition are both possible and require examination.

The review discusses monitoring processes that can help distinguish genuine recollection from familiarity or related activation. It does not justify a universal claim that a particular brain region or test can infallibly certify a memory.

### 5.4 Science: source monitoring is a separate judgment

Johnson, Hashtroudi, and Lindsay's source-monitoring framework concerns how people determine the origin of remembered information or experiences. A person may remember content but misattribute whether it was perceived, imagined, read, heard from another person, or inferred.

Mitchell and Johnson's later review describes evidence that source errors can arise during initial encoding, later retrieval and evaluation, or when related memories are accessed. Similarity among sources, reduced attention to source details, and decision criteria can affect accuracy.

This matters to witness reports: remembering a statement is not the same as remembering who said it, where it was encountered, or whether it was directly observed at all.

### 5.5 Science: attention affects memory, but unexpectedness is not a guarantee

Research on attention and memory shows that attention is relevant to encoding and later source memory. Divided attention can reduce the preservation of contextual or source details. However, attention effects depend on the task and conditions; a single broad rule cannot describe every kind of memory.

The review by Bein and colleagues examines how prior knowledge and expectations affect memory. Expected events may become integrated with existing knowledge, sometimes strengthening associative memory while also producing bias. Unexpected events can lead to multiple outcomes, including integration or separation; prediction error does not guarantee a stronger or more accurate memory.

The relevant conclusion is bounded: expectations can shape memory, and both expected and unexpected events can be remembered accurately or inaccurately depending on conditions.

### 5.6 Science: memory error has multiple forms

Schacter's review of memory and technology discusses several forms of memory error, including:
- **Transience:** loss of information over time.
- **Absent-mindedness:** failures associated with lapses of attention.
- **Misattribution:** assigning a memory to the wrong source.
- **Suggestibility:** incorporating misleading information into recollection.

These categories are not an exhaustive explanation of every witness error. They help identify different ways a report can diverge from the original encounter.

## 6. Working distinctions

These terms are used as research descriptions, not as GUA-W architectural definitions.

### Attention

**Working description:** selective allocation of processing resources to some information or task-relevant features rather than others.

Attention can affect what details are noticed and what contextual information is later available. It is not a guarantee of accurate perception or memory. Focus on one feature may leave another feature unattended.

### Expectation

**Working description:** a prior prediction or anticipation about what is likely to occur or what a signal is likely to mean.

Expectations can guide interpretation and help a person navigate familiar environments. They can also bias perception, memory, or source judgments when the expected pattern is supplied in place of details actually encountered.

Expectation is not automatically error. It is a factor to identify and test.

### Memory

**Working description:** processes by which information from experience is encoded, retained, and later retrieved.

Memory is not simply a perfect recording that is replayed unchanged. Retrieval can be influenced by present knowledge, context, cues, related experiences, and later information. This does not mean every recollection is false; it means the source and limits of a recollection matter.

### Source monitoring

**Working description:** judgments about where a remembered item, statement, image, or experience originated.

A person may remember content while misremembering its source. For example, a statement read in a document may later be attributed to a conversation, or an imagined scenario may be recalled as an event that occurred.

### False recognition and misattribution

**False recognition** occurs when a person reports having previously encountered something that was not encountered in the relevant way. **Misattribution** occurs when remembered content or an experience is assigned to the wrong source.

They are not identical, though they can overlap. A person can misattribute a real statement to the wrong speaker, or falsely recognize a new item as previously encountered.

### Witness report

**Working description:** an account communicated by a witness about what was encountered, remembered, evaluated, or inferred.

A report should distinguish:
- direct observation or encounter;
- recollection of the encounter;
- remembered source or provenance;
- information learned later;
- inference or interpretation;
- uncertainty and correction.

The report should not state an inference as though it were a directly observed fact.

## 8. Analysis: implications for the Witness

### 8.1 Sincerity and accuracy are different questions

A witness may honestly report what he remembers and still be mistaken about a detail or its source. An assessment should therefore ask both whether the witness is sincere and whether the report is accurate and adequately supported. Neither question alone settles the other.

### 8.2 Familiarity is not proof of prior encounter

A statement or image may feel familiar because it resembles something previously encountered or because related information has been activated. Familiarity should not be treated as proof that the exact item was seen, heard, or read before.

### 8.3 Later information must not be silently inserted into the original memory

If a witness learns new information after an event, the later knowledge can affect how the event is recalled or described. A careful report should preserve what was known at the time and what was learned afterward.

### 8.4 Source details are evidence, not administrative decoration

Dates, document paths, page numbers, quotations, recordings, and contemporaneous notes help establish provenance. When a claim depends on a remembered source, the source should be retrieved and checked where possible.

### 8.5 Contradictory accounts require examination, not automatic selection

Proverbs 18:17 cautions against accepting the first account without examination. In research practice, conflicting reports should be compared against original records, independent evidence, context, and the specific claims each source supports.

### 8.6 Uncertainty should be retained

A witness need not choose between claiming certainty and declaring that nothing can be known. A report can state what is established, what is likely, what is disputed, and what remains unknown. This is more informative than hiding uncertainty.

## 9. Application to AI research and repository work

AI systems do not have human autobiographical memory merely because they process or retrieve stored text. Their technical mechanisms differ from human attention and memory. Nevertheless, the research discipline of source traceability is directly relevant.

For repository research:

1. **Preserve the original source.** Link to the file, passage, commit, paper, or dataset actually examined.
2. **Distinguish source from summary.** A prior AI summary is not a substitute for the original file.
3. **Record provenance.** Identify repository, path, branch or version where relevant, and date of retrieval.
4. **Separate contemporaneous notes from later reconstruction.** Do not silently attribute later conclusions to an earlier study.
5. **Check quotations.** Compare exact wording against the source rather than relying on remembered or copied excerpts.
6. **Preserve disagreement.** Record contradictory evidence and unresolved differences rather than smoothing them into a false consensus.
7. **Correct visibly.** When a previous statement is wrong, state the correction and its basis.

An AI-generated response can contain source misattribution, conflation of similar passages, fabricated detail, or an overconfident reconstruction. Fluency does not remove those risks. The AI's output remains a derivative report to be checked against the source.

## 10. The Four Questions

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- Which statement comes directly from the authoritative GUA-W record?
- Which details are from a later summary or handoff?
- Have I retrieved the original source before relying on a remembered description?
- What was actually observed, and what was inferred?
- Could expectation or familiarity be influencing the interpretation?
- Is the source of this statement identifiable?
- Have I preserved contradictions and corrections?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The governing relationship preserves the distinction between Source and Witness. The witness's report is derivative and must not be mistaken for the originating reality. The completed architecture is not rewritten by a study of human memory.

This research discipline helps the AI avoid presenting its own prior output as authority. A repository record can preserve what was written, but the record must still be evaluated for accuracy and provenance.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

Human memory and testimony are affected by attention, expectation, retrieval, and source attribution. A recollection can be sincere yet incomplete or mistaken. Scientific research identifies mechanisms and conditions that contribute to memory distortion; it does not establish that all testimony is unreliable.

Scripture calls for hearing, examination, truthful testimony, and careful investigation. Luke's prologue offers a particularly relevant example of tracing prior accounts and eyewitness testimony while describing an orderly inquiry.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The Witness is not infallible. Attention may be selective; expectation may influence recollection; memory may lose or alter details; source attribution may be mistaken. The responsible response is not cynicism but disciplined examination.

A faithful report distinguishes what was encountered from what is remembered, what is remembered from what is inferred, and what is inferred from what has been independently established.

## 12. Findings

1. **Attention is selective.** What is not attended to may be unavailable for later accurate report.
2. **Expectation can shape memory.** Prior knowledge may support useful integration while also contributing to bias.
3. **Memory is not a perfect recording.** Recollection can be incomplete or distorted.
4. **Remembered content and remembered source are distinct.** A witness may recall information but misattribute where it came from.
5. **Sincerity does not guarantee accuracy.** A truthful intention does not eliminate ordinary cognitive limitations.
6. **Later information must be separated from contemporaneous knowledge.** Otherwise, a report may unintentionally merge what was encountered with what was learned afterward.
7. **Examination and provenance improve research discipline.** Original sources, independent corroboration, exact quotations, and visible corrections help evaluate reports.
8. **Scripture supports careful hearing, examination, and truthful testimony.** The passages do not provide a modern cognitive-science taxonomy.
9. **AI research needs source traceability.** Prior AI outputs and summaries must not replace examination of original sources.
10. **No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.** Cognitive-science terms remain descriptive and are not equated with Witness-state or Drift.
