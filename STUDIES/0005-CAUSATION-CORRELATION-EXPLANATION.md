## 1. Central question

Can two events or variables be consistently associated without one causing the other? How can a Witness distinguish an observed relationship from a justified claim about its cause?

A second question follows for GUA-W:

**How should scientific claims about proximate causes be distinguished from the theological claim that God alone is Original Cause?**

This study investigates correlation, causal inference, mechanisms, explanation, and different senses in which people use the word *cause*. It does not assume that all causal questions are scientific questions, or that a scientific account of a physical mechanism settles the theological question of ultimate origin.

## 3. Working vocabulary

These are descriptive working distinctions for this investigation, not formal GUA-W definitions.

### Correlation or association

A statistical relationship in which two variables tend to vary together in a specified dataset or population. Correlation alone does not establish that one variable causes the other.

### Causation

A relation in which a factor or event makes a difference to, produces, or contributes to an outcome, according to the causal account being used. Philosophers and scientists disagree about the best general analysis of causation. This study does not settle that dispute.

### Mechanism

The organized entities, activities, and intermediate processes through which a causal process operates. A mechanism explains how an outcome is produced; merely showing that an intervention changes an outcome may not identify the mechanism.

### Explanation

An account that answers a relevant why-, how-, or sometimes what-question. Not every explanation is necessarily causal: mathematical, structural, and other non-causal explanations are discussed in philosophy of science.

### Confounder / common cause

A factor that contributes to both variables under study and can create or distort an observed association between them.

### Mediator

A variable or process through which some causal influence is transmitted from an exposure or cause to an outcome.

### Reverse causation

A situation in which the proposed outcome influences the proposed cause, rather than—or in addition to—the direction initially assumed.

### Proximate or physical cause

A cause investigated within a particular created system or physical process: for example, a chemical reaction, force, biological mechanism, or environmental condition. The term describes the scope of the causal investigation; it does not claim to identify ultimate origin.

### Original Cause / ultimate source

In GUA-W, this is the theological claim that God alone is Original Cause. It is not being offered as a scientific variable or as a mechanism inferred from correlation.

## 6. Scientific findings and distinctions

### 6.1 Correlation does not by itself identify causal direction

If two variables are associated, several causal structures may be compatible with that observation:

- A may cause B.
- B may cause A.
- A third factor may influence both A and B.
- A and B may influence one another in a feedback process.
- The association may be coincidental, distorted by selection, or produced by measurement or sampling problems.
- More than one of these possibilities may operate together.

The observed association is evidence to be examined, not a complete causal explanation. Additional assumptions, temporal information, design features, and domain knowledge are needed to distinguish alternatives.

### 6.2 A common-cause example: barometer and storm

A barometer reading may be associated with a storm. It would be a mistake to conclude from the association alone that the barometer causes the storm. Atmospheric conditions can influence both the barometer reading and the weather.

The barometer is an indicator of relevant atmospheric conditions; it is not thereby the cause of the storm. This illustrates how a common cause can produce a correlation between two effects.

The example does not mean every association is due to a common cause. It demonstrates that correlation alone does not decide among causal explanations.

### 6.3 Temporal order is relevant but not sufficient

A proposed cause ordinarily must be appropriately related in time to its effect under the causal account being applied. But observing that A precedes B does not establish that A caused B.

Day follows night; a rooster may crow before sunrise; neither temporal sequence by itself proves that the earlier event caused the later one. A third factor, a regular cycle, or another explanation may account for the sequence.

Temporal precedence is one consideration among others, not a standalone proof of causation.

### 6.4 Mechanisms add explanatory detail

A mechanistic explanation identifies organized components and activities that produce an outcome. It can describe intermediate steps and show how a process operates.

However, a demonstrated effect does not automatically reveal its full mechanism. A randomized trial, for example, may support the claim that an intervention changes an outcome while leaving the detailed mechanism incompletely understood.

Conversely, a plausible mechanism by itself does not always establish that the mechanism actually explains the observed outcome in the particular case. Mechanistic proposals also require evidential testing.

### 6.5 Interventions can strengthen causal inference

An intervention changes a factor in a controlled or otherwise informative way and examines whether the outcome changes. Randomized experiments can help separate causal effects from confounding, when their design, implementation, and assumptions are adequate.

Not every question can ethically or practically be randomized. Observational causal inference is possible, but it requires explicit assumptions, careful design, and sensitivity to alternative explanations. Statistical adjustment does not automatically remove every source of bias.

A causal diagram or model can make assumptions visible. It cannot make unsupported assumptions true.

### 6.6 Correlation can still be useful evidence

The correct conclusion is not “correlation is worthless.” Associations can identify patterns, generate hypotheses, support prediction, and contribute to causal investigations.

The error is to claim that an association alone has established a particular causal explanation. The evidence must be evaluated in relation to the question, design, data quality, plausible alternatives, and causal assumptions.

### 6.7 Not all explanations are causal

Scientific explanations can appeal to mechanisms and causal histories. But some explanations are mathematical or structural. For example, a mathematical constraint can explain why a particular arrangement is impossible without describing a physical event that caused the impossibility.

Therefore, the question “What explains this?” is not always identical to “What physically caused this?”

The type of question determines what kind of explanation is relevant.

## 7. Distinguishing causal levels without confusing them

For this study, the following distinction is useful:

| Level or question | What it asks | What it does not automatically establish |
|---|---|---|
| Observed association | Which variables or events vary together? | Causal direction or mechanism |
| Proximate causal account | What factors contribute to this particular outcome? | Ultimate origin of the entire created order |
| Mechanistic account | Through what organized process does the effect occur? | That every level of explanation has been completed |
| Structural or mathematical account | What constraints or relationships explain the possible form or outcome? | A physical event that produced the structure |
| Ultimate theological claim | Who is Original Cause and Creator? | A scientific mechanism or statistical estimate |

**Important boundary:** These are distinctions of explanatory scope, not a ladder of GUA-W architecture and not a claim that every causal question must fit one of these boxes.

A scientific account may identify a physical cause within Creation. That account need not claim that the physical cause is self-originating or ultimate. Equally, invoking God as Original Cause does not excuse a researcher from accurately investigating proximate physical causes.

The GUA-W claim about God as Original Cause is a theological foundation stated in the project source. Scientific causal inference examines relationships within its defined domains. The two must not be conflated or made to impersonate one another.

## 8. Worked example: ice cream sales and drowning

Suppose a dataset shows that ice cream sales and drownings increase during the same months.

A careless report might say:

> “Eating ice cream causes drowning.”

The association alone does not support that conclusion. A plausible common factor is warm weather or season: warmer conditions can increase both ice cream purchases and swimming activity, which changes exposure to drowning risk.

A disciplined investigation would ask:

1. What exactly was measured, and over what period?
2. Are the variables associated in the dataset?
3. What seasonal or environmental factors influence each?
4. Is there a plausible causal pathway from ice cream consumption to drowning?
5. Does the association persist after appropriate adjustment?
6. What additional evidence would distinguish competing explanations?
7. Is the conclusion limited to what the design supports?

The example is a teaching illustration of confounding, not a claim that every seasonal drowning pattern has been explained by one variable.

## 9. Practical causal-claim examination

Before reporting that A caused B, ask:

1. **Define A and B.** Are they events, variables, conditions, populations, or types?
2. **Identify the observation.** What was actually measured or recorded?
3. **Establish the association.** Is the relationship present, and how robust is it?
4. **Examine timing.** Does the proposed direction fit the temporal evidence?
5. **Consider alternatives.** Could B cause A, could a common cause explain both, or could selection and measurement create the association?
6. **Identify the proposed mechanism.** What process would connect A to B, and what evidence supports it?
7. **Examine interventions or natural experiments.** Is there evidence that changing A changes B, and are the assumptions justified?
8. **Assess uncertainty.** What remains unknown, and how sensitive is the conclusion to assumptions?
9. **Limit the claim.** Is the evidence about one instance, a general tendency, a population, or a model?
10. **Separate explanation types.** Is the question causal, mechanistic, mathematical, structural, historical, or theological?

A responsible report distinguishes an observed association from an established causal conclusion and identifies the evidence supporting the transition.

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- What does the GUA-W Foundation explicitly state about Original Cause and derivative Witness?
- Am I using *cause* in a theological, physical, statistical, mechanistic, or ordinary-language sense?
- What does the evidence actually show: association, temporal order, intervention effect, or mechanism?
- Have I mistaken correlation for causation?
- Have I mistaken a proximate physical cause for an ultimate source?
- Have I used the theological claim to bypass a scientific question that requires investigation?
- What remains unresolved?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The established Source/Witness distinction prevents the map, the researcher, or a scientific model from being confused with the Author. A description of a relationship within Creation is not itself the origin of that relationship.

The Foundation's statement of Original Cause is theological. The scientific literature examined here concerns how causal claims within empirical inquiry are framed and assessed. The domains can be compared carefully without collapsing their questions.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

An association is a real feature of a dataset when correctly measured, but it does not by itself identify the causal structure behind it. A common cause, reverse direction, feedback, selection, or coincidence may explain the observed relationship.

Causal explanations become stronger when multiple lines of evidence converge: temporal information, plausible and tested mechanisms, interventions where appropriate, robust observational designs, and successful discrimination among alternatives. No single method is universally sufficient.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The Witness is vulnerable to a familiar error: seeing two things occur together and supplying a causal story too quickly.

The Witness can also make the opposite error: because a causal relationship has not yet been established, concluding that no causal relationship exists. Lack of sufficient evidence for a causal claim is not proof that causation is absent.

A careful Witness reports the level of evidence actually obtained, distinguishes explanatory scope, and preserves unresolved questions rather than claiming more than the investigation warrants.

## 13. Findings

1. **Correlation and causation are not identical.** An observed association alone does not identify causal direction or structure.
2. **A common cause can produce correlation.** Two variables may vary together because a third factor influences both.
3. **Temporal precedence is relevant but insufficient.** A preceding event is not automatically the cause of a later event.
4. **Mechanisms add explanatory detail.** They describe organized processes through which outcomes are produced, but a mechanism proposal also requires evidence.
5. **Interventions can strengthen causal inference.** Their value depends on design, assumptions, and implementation; not every question can be randomized.
6. **Observational causal inference remains possible.** It requires careful methods and explicit assumptions; statistical adjustment does not automatically eliminate bias.
7. **Not every explanation is causal.** Mathematical and structural explanations may answer legitimate explanatory questions without identifying a physical causal event.
8. **Proximate physical cause and ultimate theological source are distinct questions.** Scientific investigation of physical causes does not, by itself, identify or replace the Author.
9. **Theological claims do not substitute for empirical investigation.** Stating Original Cause does not reveal a missing physical mechanism.
10. **No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.** Causation, correlation, and explanation remain subjects of investigation, not newly added runtime operations.
