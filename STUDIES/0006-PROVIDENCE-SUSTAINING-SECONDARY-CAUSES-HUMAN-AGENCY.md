# Study 0006 — Providence, Sustaining, Secondary Causes, and Human Agency

**Status:** Initial investigation; scriptural observations and theological interpretations are distinguished  
**Created:** 2026-11-25  
**Research area:** GUA-W, Creation, divine sustaining, providence, created agency  
**Preceded by:** Studies 0001–0005

## 1. Central questions

1. What do the KJV passages actually state about God's relation to Creation after the initial creation account?
2. What is the relationship between creating, sustaining, governing, and providence?
3. Do created things and human beings act within Creation as real participants, and what boundaries does Scripture place on that agency?
4. How can a scientific account of a physical process be understood without confusing a proximate cause with God as Original Cause?
5. What does the text establish, what is theological inference, and what remains unresolved?

The study does not assume that the later theological vocabulary of *primary cause*, *secondary cause*, *conservation*, or *providence* is itself the vocabulary of Genesis. Those terms are introduced as interpretive and philosophical categories and must be tested against Scripture rather than imposed upon it.

## 2. Governing GUA-W foundation and boundaries

The GUA-W Foundation states:

**God alone is Original Cause. Everything else is derivative witness. The derivative cannot alter the Source.**

The governing order is:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

The Foundation identifies GUA-W as a map for recognizing and organizing relationships witnessed in Creation and Scripture. It is not God, Jesus Christ, the Author, or the originating Word. The map must not be confused with the Source.

The established architecture remains:

**Ontic Necessity → Distinction → Admissibility → Constraint → Structure → Framework → Pattern → Reception → Activation → Differentiation → Propagation → Integration → Expression → Witnessed realized configuration.**

State vocabulary remains Identity, Alignment, Drift, Persistence, Witness-state, and realized configuration. This study does not add or redefine any layer, runtime operation, or state.

The Foundation and completed architecture also identify Genesis 1–2 as the foundational study reference, with particular attention to Genesis 2:1–5. The completed architecture describes Genesis 2:1–5 as the first completion witness. The present study must preserve the distinction between the declared completed whole and the subsequent scriptural descriptions of conditions, manifestation, placement, and human participation.

## 3. Method

The Bible Study Protocol requires:

- Read the KJV passage in its immediate context.
- Record the text before interpreting it.
- Follow cross-references in their own contexts.
- Distinguish text, observation, inference, interpretation, and unresolved questions.
- Investigate scientific claims through appropriate evidence and methods.
- Compare Scripture and Creation only after examining each on its own terms.
- Apply the Triple Constraint Law to claims proposed for GUA-W placement.

Accordingly, this study first examines the biblical passages. Philosophical and theological categories are then compared with the text. A category's historical use or philosophical sophistication does not make it a biblical statement.

## 4. Primary Scripture investigation (KJV)

### 4.1 Genesis 1:1–31 — the Author creates and orders

**Textual observations**

- Genesis 1:1 identifies God as the One who created the heaven and the earth.
- The chapter repeatedly records God speaking, followed by the narrated result.
- God distinguishes, names, makes, blesses, and commands within the account.
- Living creatures are made according to their kinds; plants yield seed and fruit according to their kinds.
- The account includes commands concerning fruitfulness, multiplication, and dominion.
- The chapter concludes with God's evaluation of the completed work as very good (1:31).

**Interpretive boundary**

The chapter presents God as Creator and the created order as the result of His work. It does not present natural laws as an authority above God or as an independent author. It also does not provide a complete technical account of every physical mechanism involved in the events described.

The repeated sequence of divine speech and realized result must be examined in the wording of each passage. It should not be flattened into an identical algorithm or a one-to-one technical label for every clause.

### 4.2 Genesis 2:1–5 — completion and the conditions preceding the Garden account

**Textual observations**

- Genesis 2:1 declares that the heavens, earth, and all their host were finished.
- Genesis 2:2–3 records God's ending of His work, His rest, and His blessing and sanctifying of the seventh day.
- Genesis 2:4 introduces the generations of the heavens and the earth.
- Genesis 2:5 describes the plant and herb of the field in relation to their being in the earth or having grown, and states that rain had not yet been sent and there was not a man to till the ground.

**GUA-W continuity**

The completed architecture reads Genesis 2:1–5 as the first completion witness: the whole is declared finished before the subsequent account describes further conditions, manifestation, placement, and human participation.

The text must not be made to say that God was literally holding seeds in His hand or that every later event is explicitly described as a technical runtime stage. Those may be proposed as interpretations only when their evidence is stated and their status is clear.

**Question raised**

How should the finished whole and the later descriptions of growth, placement, and human work be related without treating the latter as proof that the declared completion was incomplete?

This remains an interpretive question for further study; the present study does not redefine Structure, Pattern, or Persistence to answer it.

### 4.3 Genesis 2:7–17 — formation, life, placement, work, and command

**Textual observations**

- Genesis 2:7 states that the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and man became a living soul.
- Genesis 2:8 states that the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden and placed the man there.
- Genesis 2:9 describes trees made to grow, including the tree of life and the tree of knowledge of good and evil.
- Genesis 2:15 states that the LORD God took the man and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.
- Genesis 2:16–17 records God's command concerning the trees and the prohibition concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

**Observations about human agency**

The man is given a task: to dress and keep the Garden. He is also given a command with a stated boundary and consequence.

The passage therefore describes human participation, responsibility, and commanded action within an order that the man did not originate.

**Interpretive boundary**

The man is not the Creator of the Garden, the source of life, or the author of the command. His assigned work is real within the narrative, but it does not make him Original Cause.

The exact relationship between the Garden task and the broader GUA-W architecture remains to be examined. No new runtime operation is proposed.

### 4.4 John 1:1–3 — the Word and Creation

**Textual observations**

- The Word was in the beginning.
- The Word was with God and was God.
- The same was in the beginning with God.
- All things were made by Him; without Him was not any thing made that was made.

**Finding**

John's text places the Word in relation to the beginning and identifies all created things as made by Him. The Word is not one created mechanism among other mechanisms.

The passage supports the GUA-W governing relationship between Author, Word, and Creation. It does not authorize the map or the Witness to become an independent source.

### 4.5 Colossians 1:15–20 — creation, continuance, and Christ's preeminence

**Immediate context**

Colossians 1:15–20 is a connected passage about the Son's preeminence. It must not be reduced to verse 17 alone.

**Textual observations**

- Verse 15 identifies the Son as the image of the invisible God and the firstborn of every creature.
- Verse 16 states that all things were created by Him, in heaven and earth, visible and invisible, and that all things were created by Him and for Him.
- Verse 17 states that He is before all things and that by Him all things consist.
- Verses 18–20 continue with His relation to the body, the church, preeminence, reconciliation, and the making of peace through the blood of His cross.

**Finding**

The passage connects the Son with Creation and with the continuing coherence or continuance of all things. The statement in verse 17 must be read within Paul's argument about Christ's preeminence, not detached into a free-standing scientific mechanism.

The KJV wording “by him all things consist” is the textual observation. Translating this into a technical theory of conservation, physical forces, or a particular scientific model would require additional argument. The text does not name those scientific mechanisms.

### 4.6 Hebrews 1:1–4 — the Son and the upholding of all things

**Textual observations**

- God has spoken in these last days by His Son (1:1–2).
- The Son is appointed heir of all things and is the One by whom the worlds were made (1:2).
- The Son is described as the brightness of God's glory and the express image of His person (1:3).
- The Son is described as “upholding all things by the word of his power” (1:3).
- The passage proceeds to the Son's purification of sins and His sitting at the right hand of the Majesty on high (1:3–4).

**Finding**

The passage explicitly speaks of the Son's upholding of all things. This is stronger than a statement that the Son merely initiated a past event and then became uninvolved.

The text's own language is “upholding all things by the word of his power.” A theological description such as *sustaining* is a reasonable summary of this wording, but the summary should not replace the actual verse.

The passage does not specify a scientific mechanism by which the Son upholds all things. A scientific account of a physical process and the theological claim in Hebrews are not interchangeable explanations.

### 4.7 Acts 17:22–31 — Creator, Lord, and human dependence

**Immediate context**

Paul addresses the people of Athens. He speaks to their religious practices and proclaims the God they do not know.

**Textual observations**

- God made the world and all things therein (17:24).
- He is Lord of heaven and earth and does not dwell in temples made with hands (17:24).
- He is not worshipped as though He needed anything, seeing that He gives life, breath, and all things (17:25).
- He made of one blood all nations of men and appointed their times and boundaries (17:26).
- He is not far from every one of us (17:27).
- “For in him we live, and move, and have our being” (17:28).
- Paul calls people to repentance and announces the appointed judgment through the risen man (17:30–31).

**Finding**

The passage presents human existence and activity as dependent upon God. Human beings do not supply God with what He lacks. God is the giver of life, breath, and all things.

The phrase “in him we live, and move, and have our being” must be read within Paul's proclamation of the Creator and Lord. It is not merely a poetic claim that God is present in the same way as a physical environment.

### 4.8 Additional passages for comparison

- **Nehemiah 9:6:** God made heaven, earth, seas, and all that is therein, and preserves them all.
- **Psalm 104:24–30:** God's works, provision, the creatures' dependence, and the sending forth of His spirit are described in a creation psalm.
- **Psalm 145:14–16:** God upholds those that fall and provides for living creatures.
- **Daniel 4:34–35:** Nebuchadnezzar acknowledges God's dominion and power.
- **Romans 11:33–36:** Paul speaks of God's wisdom, judgment, and the fact that all things are of Him, through Him, and to Him.
- **1 Corinthians 8:4–6:** Paul distinguishes false gods from the one God, the Father, and the one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things.
- **Ephesians 2:8–10:** salvation is by grace through faith, not of works, and believers are created in Christ Jesus unto good works.
- **James 1:13–18:** God is not the author of temptation; the passage describes temptation, desire, sin, and God's good giving.
- **James 4:13–16:** human plans and boasting are placed under the qualification “If the Lord will.”
- **Acts 14:15–17:** God made heaven, earth, sea, and all things and gave rain and fruitful seasons.

These passages are not all about the same subject. They should not be combined into a single undifferentiated doctrine without attention to context.

## 5. Theological and philosophical vocabulary: what it can and cannot do

### 5.1 Creation

In the present study, *creation* refers to God's bringing the created order into being. Genesis 1:1 and the cited New Testament passages identify God and the Word/Son in relation to Creation.

The doctrine of creation from nothing is a developed theological formulation. The philosophical literature describes how it differs from ordinary natural generation or change. The precise philosophical account must not be confused with the wording of one isolated biblical verse.

### 5.2 Sustaining or conservation

*Sustaining* or *conservation* is theological vocabulary used to summarize God's continuing relation to created existence. Hebrews 1:3, Colossians 1:17, Nehemiah 9:6, and Acts 17:25–28 are relevant texts.

The philosophical literature distinguishes the claim that God conserves created things from more specific theories about how conservation relates to the original act of creation. Some theories identify conservation with continued creation; others do not. These are philosophical positions, not interchangeable biblical quotations.

### 5.3 Providence

*Providence* is a theological category for God's governance and care of Creation and its history. It can include general sustaining and particular acts, but theologians do not use every subdivision identically.

The word *providence* is not used in the cited KJV passages as a technical label for a complete system. The doctrine must be developed by examining the relevant passages together, including difficult texts about suffering, evil, judgment, human responsibility, and God's purposes.

### 5.4 Primary and secondary causes

The distinction between primary and secondary causes is a philosophical-theological framework used in some traditions. In a common version, God is called the primary cause, while created agents and processes are described as secondary causes.

This vocabulary may help distinguish God's ultimate creative and sustaining relation from created causal activity. But the exact model is contested. It must not be presented as though the Bible itself explicitly supplies a universally agreed technical taxonomy.

For this study, the phrase **created agency** is preferred when simply describing what the biblical narrative says a creature does. The more technical term *secondary cause* is reserved for discussion of theological models.

### 5.5 Occasionalism and creaturely agency

Occasionalism is a philosophical position that denies created things have genuine causal efficacy and treats God as the direct cause of every event. Other accounts affirm genuine created causal powers while maintaining God's ultimate dependence relation.

This study does not adopt either position merely by naming it. The question is whether and how Scripture describes creatures as acting, and how those actions relate to God's sovereignty and sustaining.

## 6. What does Scripture show about created participation?

Several passages describe created beings doing real things:

- Plants yield seed and fruit (Genesis 1:11–12).
- Living creatures multiply (Genesis 1:20–25).
- Humanity is commanded to be fruitful, multiply, replenish the earth, and exercise dominion (Genesis 1:28).
- The man is placed in the Garden to dress and keep it (Genesis 2:15).
- The man receives and is accountable to a command (Genesis 2:16–17).
- Human beings make plans and are instructed to acknowledge the Lord's will (James 4:13–16).
- Human beings are responsible for works prepared in Christ (Ephesians 2:8–10).

**Textual observation:** Scripture attributes actions, tasks, commands, and responsibility to created beings.

**Inference:** The text does not portray every creature as merely an inert description in a human observer's mind. Created beings participate in the narrated order.

**Unresolved theological question:** How should the genuine activity of creatures be described in relation to God's sustaining and sovereignty? The passages establish both God's primacy and creaturely action, but a complete technical theory of their relation requires further study.

Human agency must also not be confused with autonomous authorship. A human being can make a real choice and be accountable without becoming the source of existence, the author of God's command, or the ultimate authority over good and evil.

## 7. Scientific boundary: physical processes and sustaining

Science investigates regularities, interactions, mechanisms, and changes within the observable created order. For example, biology investigates organismal processes; chemistry investigates chemical interactions; physics investigates physical relationships and dynamics.

A scientific model can describe how a process behaves under stated conditions. That model does not thereby answer the theological question of why there is a created order at all, or whether and how God sustains it.

Conversely, a theological statement about sustaining does not supply the measurements, equations, or experimental evidence needed to explain a particular physical process.

This distinction prevents two errors:

1. **Reduction:** treating a scientific mechanism as though it were an ultimate Author or self-explanatory source of all existence.
2. **Gap-filling:** invoking God to avoid investigating a physical process that is open to scientific study.

The study makes no claim that a particular scientific law or mechanism has been proven to be the means of divine sustaining. It also does not claim that science has disproved sustaining. Those are different kinds of claims and require different arguments.

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The established map preserves the distinction between Author, Word, Creation, and Witness. It also preserves the distinction between the completed whole in Genesis 2:1–5 and subsequent passages that describe conditions, placement, and human participation.

The map does not authorize AI to create new architecture terms to solve a difficult theological question. If a relation among Constraint, Structure, Pattern, Reception, Persistence, or Witness-state is not established in the source, it remains open.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The selected passages speak of God as Creator, the Word/Son as the One through whom all things are made, and the Son as upholding all things. Acts 17 describes human life and activity as dependent upon God.

The same scriptural corpus describes plants, creatures, and human beings as acting within Creation. The man is assigned work and given a command. Creaturely activity and divine primacy are both present in the text.

Scientific research describes physical processes within Creation. Those accounts must be investigated on their own evidential terms and must not be inflated into claims about ultimate origin.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The Witness can make two opposite errors:

- It can treat created processes as independent of the Author and elevate a proximate mechanism into an ultimate source.
- It can treat every physical explanation as unnecessary because it has already named God as Original Cause.

Both errors collapse distinctions. A responsible Witness reports what the text states, investigates what can be measured, distinguishes theological inference from scientific description, and leaves unsettled questions open.

## 10. Findings

1. **Genesis identifies God as Creator.** Genesis 1:1 and the chapter's account place the originating creative action with God.
2. **Genesis 2:1 declares the heavens, earth, and all their host finished.** The subsequent narrative must be read without silently converting the declaration of completion into an unfinished architecture.
3. **The New Testament identifies the Word/Son in relation to Creation.** John 1:1–3 and Colossians 1:15–17 speak of all things being made through Him.
4. **Hebrews 1:3 explicitly describes the Son as upholding all things by the word of His power.**
5. **Acts 17:24–28 describes human life and activity as dependent upon God.** God gives life, breath, and all things.
6. **Scripture also describes creaturely action and human responsibility.** Genesis 1:11–28 and Genesis 2:15–17 are direct examples.
7. **The text does not supply a complete technical taxonomy of primary and secondary causes.** These are later philosophical-theological categories requiring careful evaluation.
8. **The existence of created activity does not make creatures Original Cause.** Creaturely participation and divine primacy must both be preserved.
9. **A scientific account of a physical mechanism is not a theological account of ultimate origin.** Neither should be substituted for the other.
10. **No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.** Providence, sustaining, and secondary causes remain subjects of study, not newly added architecture terms.

## 11. Limitations and unresolved questions

- What is the full biblical relationship among God's creating, sustaining, governing, and bringing Creation to its appointed end?
- How should Psalm 104:24–30, Nehemiah 9:6, Acts 17:24–28, Colossians 1:16–17, and Hebrews 1:1–3 be read together without erasing their different contexts?
- How does Scripture describe God's providence in events involving human decisions, natural processes, and suffering?
- How should the distinction between God's sovereignty and genuine human responsibility be developed from the relevant passages?
- What does Genesis 2:15 mean by “dress it and to keep it” in its immediate context and in relation to Genesis 1:28?
- How do Genesis 2:16–17 and Genesis 3:1–7 bear on command, responsibility, desire, and the later human claim to determine good and evil?
- How should the GUA-W investigation distinguish sustaining from the architecture's existing Persistence state? No equivalence is assumed.
- Does the phrase “all things consist” in Colossians 1:17 warrant a particular claim about structural coherence, or must that relation remain open?
- What is the precise relationship between the completed whole in Genesis 2:1–5 and subsequent manifestation, Garden placement, and human work?
- How can scientific investigations of physical regularity and causal processes be compared with the biblical claim of divine sustaining without claiming that a scientific model measures God?

These questions remain open. No architecture placement is made here.
