# Study 0008 — The Hebrew of Genesis 2:5–15: Work, Service, Keeping, Garden, and Ground

**Status:** Initial lexical and contextual investigation; Hebrew observations, translation, interpretation, and GUA-W comparison are distinguished  
**Created:** 2026-11-25  
**Research area:** Genesis 2:5–15; עבד (*‘abad*), שמר (*shamar*), גן (*gan*), אדמה (*’adamah*)

## 1. The puzzle carried forward

Genesis 2:5 says there was not yet a man “to till the ground.” Genesis 2:15 then says the LORD God placed the man in the Garden of Eden “to dress it and to keep it.”

The Hebrew text uses the same verb, עבד (*‘abad*), in both verses:

- Genesis 2:5 — to work/till the ground (*’adamah*).
- Genesis 2:15 — to work/serve the Garden (*gan*).

Genesis 3:23 uses that same verb again when the man is sent from Eden “to till the ground” from which he was taken.

The sequence is striking:

**No man to work the ground → man placed in the Garden to work and keep it → man sent out to work the ground.**

This is a textual pattern worth examining carefully. It is not, by itself, proof of a complete GUA-W mechanism or of a particular theological theory.

A second clue is the pairing in Genesis 2:15 of עבד (*‘abad*) and שמר (*shamar*). These two verbs also occur together in passages describing the duties of the Levites in relation to the tabernacle and its service. That parallel has led some interpreters to understand Eden in sanctuary or priestly terms. This proposal deserves examination, but it must not be presented as an uncontested fact or as the only meaning of Genesis 2:15.

## 4. Primary text: Genesis 2:5–15 (KJV)

### 4.1 Genesis 2:5 — no man to work the ground

The verse describes conditions before the LORD God had caused it to rain upon the earth and before there was a man to till the ground.

**Textual observations**

- The verse uses אֲדָמָה (*’adamah*) for the ground.
- The phrase “to till” uses the infinitive of עבד (*‘abad*).
- The verse connects the absence of a man with the absence of this human work upon the ground.
- It does not say that no vegetation existed anywhere. Genesis 1:11–12 has already described seed-bearing plants and fruit trees.
- It does not use the Hebrew noun for seed in this clause.

**Important distinction**

The KJV's “till” in Genesis 2:5 is not a different Hebrew root from “dress” in Genesis 2:15. Both translate forms of עבד (*‘abad*). The object and context differ: ground in 2:5, the Garden in 2:15.

This repeated verb makes the relationship between the two verses more visible than the English wording alone may suggest.

### 4.2 Genesis 2:6 — watering the ground

The verse describes a mist going up from the earth and watering the whole face of the ground.

**Textual observation**

The Hebrew noun translated “mist” is אֵד (*’ed*). The precise physical referent has been debated. The verse says what it does in the narrative: it waters the ground. It does not call this rain.

**Boundary**

The lexical and contextual observation should not be converted into a confident modern meteorological identification without further evidence. The study does not claim to know the exact physical process described by *’ed*.

### 4.3 Genesis 2:7 — man and ground

Genesis 2:7 says that the LORD God formed the man of the dust of the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life.

**Textual observations**

- אָדָם (*’adam*) is the word translated “man.”
- אֲדָמָה (*’adamah*) is the word translated “ground.”
- The verse explicitly relates the man's formation to the ground.
- The LORD God is the acting subject: He forms and breathes.

The sound and word relationship between *’adam* and *’adamah* is a prominent feature of the Hebrew narrative. It is appropriate to note the wordplay, while avoiding an unsupported claim that the two words are identical in meaning or that the wordplay alone establishes a full doctrine of human nature.

### 4.4 Genesis 2:8–9 — the Garden and its growth

The LORD God plants a garden in Eden and places there the man whom He had formed. The LORD God also causes trees to grow from the ground.

**Textual observations**

- גַּן (*gan*) is the noun translated “garden.”
- Eden is the location named in the passage.
- God is the subject of planting and causing growth.
- The Garden contains trees described as pleasant to the sight and good for food, including the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

The Garden is not introduced as a human construction. The text attributes its planting to the LORD God.

### 4.5 Genesis 2:15 — the man's assigned task

The KJV reads:

**“And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.”**

The two infinitive forms are:

- לְעָבְדָהּ — *le‘ovdah*: to work/serve/cultivate it.
- וּלְשָׁמְרָהּ — *uleshomrah*: and to keep/guard/watch over it.

The suffix on each verb is feminine singular, referring grammatically to the Garden (*gan*, treated as feminine in this construction). The man is placed there for the purpose expressed by the two infinitives.

**Textual observation**

The verse explicitly gives the man's placement a stated purpose: he is placed in the Garden to work/serve it and to keep/guard it.

**Interpretive boundary**

The verse does not by itself specify every dimension of this responsibility. It clearly includes work upon or service to the Garden and keeping or guarding it. Whether the wording also carries a broader sanctuary, priestly, covenantal, or representative dimension must be evaluated through the wider biblical context.

## 5. Hebrew lexical investigation

### 5.1 עבד — *‘abad* (Strong's H5647)

**Basic lexical range:** work, serve, labour, till/cultivate; in other contexts, serve as a servant or perform service. The word can describe ordinary work, agricultural labour, service to another, or service in a religious setting. Context determines the sense.

**Occurrences central to this study**

| Passage | Object/context | KJV rendering | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 2:5 | The ground (*’adamah*) | “to till” | Human work upon the ground is absent in the described condition. |
| Genesis 2:15 | The Garden (*gan*) | “to dress” | The man is placed in the Garden for work/service. |
| Genesis 3:23 | The ground (*’adamah*) | “to till” | After expulsion, the man is sent out to work the ground. |
| Genesis 4:2 | The ground | “tiller of the ground” | Cain is described as a worker of the ground. |

**Finding**

The same Hebrew root is used for the human work described before the Garden placement, the assigned work within the Garden, and the work of the ground after expulsion.

This is a meaningful lexical connection. It shows that the English translations “till” and “dress” conceal a repeated Hebrew verb.

It does not mean that every occurrence of *‘abad* has the same nuance. “Serve,” “work,” and “cultivate” are related senses, but the object and context guide the translation.

### 5.2 שמר — *shamar* (Strong's H8104)

**Basic lexical range:** keep, guard, watch, preserve, observe, take heed, have charge of. The root can describe physical guarding, careful attention, preserving something, or observing a command or obligation.

**Occurrences central to this study**

| Passage | Context | KJV rendering | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 2:15 | The Garden | “to keep it” | The man is assigned responsibility for the Garden. |
| Genesis 3:24 | The way to the tree of life | “to keep the way” | Cherubims guard the way after the man's expulsion. |
| Genesis 4:9 | Abel | “keeper” | Cain's question uses the related participle: “Am I my brother's keeper?” |
| Genesis 17:9–10 | God's covenant | “keep” | The verb describes keeping a covenant. |
| Genesis 30:31 | A flock | “keep” | The verb describes tending/keeping animals. |

**Finding**

In Genesis 2:15, “keep” should not be reduced to a vague idea of maintenance. The lexical range includes guarding, watching, protecting, and having charge of something. The immediate Garden context supports a responsibility that includes careful keeping.

Genesis 3:24 is especially important: the same root appears when the way to the tree of life is guarded. The narrative moves from the man's charge to keep the Garden to the cherubims' guarding of the way after expulsion.

That sequence is textually significant, but it does not mean the man's earlier task and the cherubims' later task are identical in every respect.

### 5.3 גן — *gan* (Strong's H1588)

**Basic lexical range:** garden; an enclosed or cultivated garden space.

The term is used for the Garden in Eden and elsewhere for cultivated gardens, royal gardens, and figurative garden imagery.

**Genesis context**

- Genesis 2:8: God plants a garden in Eden.
- Genesis 2:9: trees grow in the garden.
- Genesis 2:15: the man is placed in the Garden and assigned work and keeping.
- Genesis 3:23–24: the man is sent out, and the way to the tree of life is guarded.

**Finding**

The word identifies a particular garden space. It should not automatically be expanded into “the whole earth,” “the whole Creation,” or “the temple” without additional argument. The broader significance of Eden must be established from the wider text, not smuggled into the lexical definition of *gan*.

### 5.4 אדמה — *’adamah* (Strong's H127)

**Basic lexical range:** ground, soil, land; often the ground as tilled or yielding produce.

**Genesis context**

- Genesis 2:5: ground to be worked.
- Genesis 2:6: the face of the ground is watered.
- Genesis 2:7: man is formed from dust of the ground.
- Genesis 2:9: trees grow from the ground.
- Genesis 3:17–19: the ground is cursed in relation to man's labour and food.
- Genesis 3:23: man is sent out to till the ground.

**Finding**

The ground is a repeated and important element in the narrative. It is related to plant growth, human formation, human work, and the post-Fall toil described in Genesis 3.

The narrative's wordplay between *’adam* and *’adamah* contributes to the literary relationship between humanity and the ground. It does not reduce humanity to soil: Genesis 2:7 also records God's breath of life.

## 6. The paired verbs: עבד and שמר

Genesis 2:15 places the two verbs together: *‘abad* and *shamar*.

A noteworthy comparison occurs in the descriptions of Levitical duties:

- Numbers 3:7–8 describes the Levites keeping the charge of the tabernacle and performing service.
- Numbers 8:26 describes service and keeping charge in relation to the congregation and tabernacle duties.
- Numbers 18:5–6 describes the charge of the sanctuary and the service associated with it.

These passages use the same two roots in combination in the context of Levitical service and guarding/keeping.

**What this comparison establishes**

1. The pairing of work/service and keeping/guarding is not limited to Genesis 2:15.
2. Elsewhere in the Torah, the pair can describe duties associated with the tabernacle and its service.
3. This provides a legitimate canonical comparison when investigating whether Eden is presented with sanctuary-like features.

**What it does not establish by itself**

1. It does not prove that Adam held the later Levitical office.
2. It does not mean Genesis 2:15 is directly describing the tabernacle, which is introduced later in Scripture.
3. It does not prove that agricultural cultivation is absent from the verse.
4. It does not settle the historical or theological debate about whether Eden should be called a temple or sanctuary.

The responsible conclusion is that a sanctuary or priestly reading has a textual basis worth examining, especially because the paired verbs occur in later sanctuary-service passages. It remains an interpretation that must be tested against the full Genesis narrative and the later canonical texts.

## 7. The narrative sequence: Genesis 2:5, 2:15, and 3:23–24

The repeated Hebrew roots reveal a pattern:

| Narrative location | Hebrew expression | What the text says |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 2:5 | *‘abad* + *’adamah* | No man yet to work the ground. |
| Genesis 2:15 | *‘abad* + *shamar* + *gan* | Man placed in the Garden to work/serve and keep/guard it. |
| Genesis 3:23 | *‘abad* + *’adamah* | Man sent out to work the ground. |
| Genesis 3:24 | *shamar* + “the way” | Cherubims guard the way to the tree of life. |

**Observation**

The same work verb connects the ground before the Garden assignment, the Garden assignment itself, and the ground after expulsion. The keeping verb connects the man's assigned responsibility with the later guarding of the way to the tree of life.

**Possible literary implication**

Genesis 2:15 is not an incidental note about employment. It stands at a hinge in the narrative: the man is placed in a prepared Garden, given a task, and given a command. After disobedience, he is expelled and the ground becomes the place of painful labour.

This is a strong narrative observation. The exact theological meaning of the sequence requires further study.

## 8. Work before the Fall and toil after the Fall

Genesis 2:15 occurs before Genesis 3. Therefore, human work is present before the Fall.

Genesis 3:17–19 describes a changed condition: the ground is cursed for man's sake, and man eats by sorrow and the sweat of his face until he returns to the ground.

**Textual distinction**

- Genesis 2:15: assigned work in the Garden before the transgression.
- Genesis 3:17–19: painful toil and resistance associated with the ground after the transgression.
- Genesis 3:23: the man is sent from Eden to work the ground.

**Finding**

The text does not present work itself as originating with the Fall. The Fall changes the conditions and burden of human labour. This distinction matters when interpreting purpose, service, stewardship, and human participation.

## 9. Scientific comparison: cultivation and care

This study's central claims are linguistic and textual, not claims about a specific modern agricultural method. Still, limited scientific comparison can clarify what cultivation and care may involve as physical activities.

Agricultural and horticultural work can include:

- preparing and managing soil;
- regulating water availability and drainage;
- supporting plant establishment and growth;
- protecting plants from damaging organisms or conditions;
- monitoring growth and responding to changing conditions.

These are examples of modern cultivation and stewardship activities, not a reconstruction of the exact tasks Adam performed. Genesis 2:15 does not list tools, techniques, irrigation systems, crop species, or a work schedule.

Scientific evidence can explain soil, plant, water, and ecological processes. It cannot decide whether the Garden has a sanctuary dimension or establish the theological meaning of *‘abad* and *shamar*.

## 12. Findings

1. Genesis 2:5 and Genesis 2:15 use the same Hebrew root, עבד (*‘abad*), although the KJV translates it “till” and “dress.”
2. Genesis 3:23 uses the same root again when the man is sent out to till the ground.
3. The repeated root establishes a textual connection among ground-work before the Garden placement, assigned work in the Garden, and ground-work after expulsion.
4. Genesis 2:15 pairs עבד (*‘abad*) with שמר (*shamar*), expressing work/service and keeping/guarding.
5. The paired roots also appear in Numbers 3:7–8, 8:26, and 18:5–6 in descriptions of Levitical service and sanctuary charge.
6. The canonical parallel makes a sanctuary or priestly reading of Eden worth investigating, but does not by itself prove that Adam held a later Levitical office or settle every feature of an Eden-temple interpretation.
7. גַּן (*gan*) means garden or cultivated/enclosed garden space in the lexical evidence examined. The word alone does not establish that Eden means the whole earth or that *gan* is a technical synonym for temple.
8. אֲדָמָה (*’adamah*) refers to ground, soil, or land; אָדָם (*’adam*) refers to man/humanity. Their word relationship is a literary feature of Genesis 2.
9. Human work is present before the Fall. Genesis 3 describes painful toil and changed conditions, not the first appearance of work.
10. The lexical evidence supports a meaningful narrative pattern. It does not establish a new GUA-W layer or settle the open question concerning Purpose and Constraint → Structure.
11. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
