# Study 0009 — Eden, Sanctuary, and the Assigned Human Vocation

**Status:** Comparative canonical investigation; textual observations, intertextual arguments, theological interpretations, and unresolved questions are distinguished  
**Created:** 2026-11-25  
**Research area:** Genesis 1–3; Eden, sanctuary imagery, human vocation, guarding, cherubim, and GUA-W  
**Preceded by:** Studies 0001–0008

## 1. Central question

Does Genesis 1–3 present Eden with sanctuary or temple-like features, and if so, what does that reveal about the man's assigned vocation in Genesis 2:15?

Study 0008 identified a significant lexical pattern:

- Genesis 2:5 — *‘abad* (עבד), work/till the ground.
- Genesis 2:15 — *‘abad* (עבד) and *shamar* (שמר), work/serve and keep/guard the Garden.
- Genesis 3:23 — *‘abad* (עבד), work/till the ground after expulsion.
- Genesis 3:24 — *shamar* (שמר), guard the way to the tree of life.

The paired verbs in Genesis 2:15 also occur in passages about Levitical service and sanctuary responsibility. This raises a question: is the Garden merely a cultivated setting, or does the narrative also portray it as a sacred dwelling in which the human vocation has a guarding and service dimension?

The study must not assume its answer. It examines the evidence for and against the sanctuary interpretation.

## 3. Method

The study follows the KJV Bible Study Protocol and recursive Four-Question Study Method.

It distinguishes:

- **Text:** what the KJV explicitly states.
- **Lexical observation:** what the Hebrew wording and forms show.
- **Intertextual observation:** a repeated word, phrase, motif, or pattern in another passage.
- **Inference:** a conclusion reasoned from one or more observations.
- **Interpretation:** an explanation of the narrative's meaning.
- **Scholarly position:** an argument made by a commentator or researcher.
- **Unresolved:** a question the available evidence does not settle.

A lexical parallel is not automatically proof of identical function. A later sanctuary text can illuminate Genesis, but it must not overwrite Genesis' own immediate setting.

## 4. Genesis 1–3: evidence to examine

### 4.1 Genesis 1:26–28 — image, dominion, fruitfulness

**Textual observations**

- God creates man in His image and likeness (1:26–27).
- God blesses them and commands them to be fruitful, multiply, replenish the earth, and subdue it (1:28).
- Dominion is assigned over the creatures named in the verse.

**Interpretive questions**

- What is the relationship between the image of God and the assigned human vocation?
- Does dominion describe responsible rule under the Creator, rather than independent ownership or authorship?
- How does Genesis 1:28 relate to the more localized task in Genesis 2:15?

**Boundary**

The passage explicitly gives a commission. It does not call Adam a priest or identify the whole earth as a temple. Those conclusions require further canonical argument.

### 4.2 Genesis 2:8–17 — Garden, placement, work, and command

**Textual observations**

- The LORD God plants the Garden in Eden (2:8).
- God causes trees to grow there (2:9).
- God places the man in the Garden (2:8, 15).
- The man is assigned to work/serve and keep/guard the Garden (2:15).
- God gives the man a command and prohibition (2:16–17).

The Garden is God's planting, not man's construction. The man receives a place, a task, provision, and a command from the Creator.

### 4.3 Genesis 3:8 — God's presence in the Garden

The KJV says the man and woman heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the Garden in the cool of the day.

**Observation**

The narrative depicts God's presence and interaction in the Garden. This contributes to the possibility that Eden is more than an ordinary agricultural location.

**Boundary**

The verse does not use the noun “temple” or “sanctuary.” The description of divine presence is evidence relevant to a sanctuary reading, not a direct lexical identification.

### 4.4 Genesis 3:22–24 — expulsion and guarded access

**Textual observations**

- God sends the man out of the Garden to till the ground from which he was taken (3:23).
- God places cherubims east of the Garden and a flaming sword to keep/guard the way to the tree of life (3:24).
- The verb translated “keep” in 3:24 is the same root *shamar* found in 2:15.

**Narrative observation**

The man who had been placed in the Garden with a charge to keep it is expelled. The way to the tree of life is now guarded by cherubims.

This creates a meaningful narrative contrast. It does not establish that Adam failed a formal priestly office unless that further interpretation is argued.

## 5. The *‘abad*–*shamar* pairing and the sanctuary comparison

### 5.1 Genesis 2:15

The two Hebrew verbs are:

- עבד (*‘abad*) — work, serve, labour, till/cultivate.
- שמר (*shamar*) — keep, guard, watch, preserve, observe.

The verse places them together in the man's assigned work within the Garden.

### 5.2 Numbers 3:7–8; 8:26; 18:5–6

These passages describe Levitical duties involving service and keeping charge in relation to the tabernacle and its responsibilities.

**Intertextual observation**

The combination of service/work and guarding/keeping occurs in a sanctuary-service context elsewhere in the Pentateuch. This makes the comparison relevant.

**What the comparison can support**

- The man's task can be read as having more significance than undifferentiated manual labour.
- A sanctuary or priestly dimension is a plausible interpretation worth examining.
- The Garden may be understood as sacred space in a wider canonical reading.

**What the comparison cannot prove alone**

- Adam was a Levitical priest in the later institutional sense.
- The Garden was identical in every respect to the Mosaic tabernacle.
- The agricultural sense of *‘abad* is absent.
- Every occurrence of the two common verbs carries a technical priestly meaning.

The two verbs are not rare words in Hebrew. The significance rests not merely on each verb appearing somewhere in Scripture, but on their combination in relevant contexts and the wider narrative features.

### 5.3 A caution from the scholarly debate

Some scholarship argues that Genesis 2:15 gives Adam a priest-like vocation and that Eden functions as a sanctuary. Other scholarship warns that the verbal parallel with Numbers is insufficient to establish Adam's priestly status, since the contexts differ and the verbs have broad ordinary uses.

Both arguments must be represented fairly.

The study's position is therefore:

**The sanctuary interpretation has cumulative textual support and deserves serious consideration, but the lexical pairing alone does not settle it.**

## 6. Other possible sanctuary features

The sanctuary interpretation is cumulative. Several additional features have been discussed in scholarly literature.

### 6.1 Cherubims

Genesis 3:24 places cherubims at the guarded way to the tree of life. Cherubim also appear in later tabernacle and temple descriptions, including Exodus 25:18–22, Exodus 26:1, 31–33, and 1 Kings 6:23–29.

This is a canonical motif worth comparing. It does not mean every cherubim scene has an identical function.

### 6.2 Eastward orientation and guarded access

Genesis 3:24 places the cherubims east of the Garden. Later sanctuary arrangements include ordered access and guarded sacred space. The comparison is relevant but should not be overstated as a complete architectural identity.

### 6.3 Tree and life imagery

The tree of life appears in Genesis 2–3 and again in Revelation 2:7 and 22:1–2, 14. The later imagery connects life, God's presence, and restored access.

This canonical trajectory may illuminate the Garden's theological significance. It does not by itself provide a blueprint of Eden's physical architecture.

### 6.4 Divine dwelling and presence

Genesis 3:8 depicts the LORD God in the Garden. Later Scripture develops the theme of God's dwelling among His people in tabernacle, temple, Christ, and the final dwelling of God with humanity.

The canonical theme is significant, but each passage must retain its own historical and literary context.

## 7. Counterarguments and limits

A responsible study must examine objections rather than collecting only confirming parallels.

1. **Common vocabulary:** *‘abad* and *shamar* are common Hebrew verbs. Their occurrence together does not automatically create a technical office.
2. **Different contexts:** Genesis describes a Garden and human work; Numbers describes the duties of Levites in a tabernacle system.
3. **No explicit title:** Genesis 2:15 does not call Adam a priest, nor does it use the later institutional title for priest.
4. **Agricultural sense remains:** The immediate context of Genesis 2:5–9 includes ground, plant growth, water, and cultivation. A sanctuary interpretation should not erase this setting.
5. **Cumulative argument required:** The temple reading depends on several features taken together—divine presence, guarded access, cherubim, the paired verbs, and later canonical patterns. Each link must be evaluated separately.
6. **Scholarly disagreement:** Published research offers both arguments for Eden sanctuary imagery and cautions against treating the lexical parallel as conclusive.

The correct outcome is not to dismiss the sanctuary reading or to declare it proved by one word pair. It is to evaluate the cumulative case.

## 8. GUA-W comparison — without architecture alteration

The study can identify a set of textual relations without assigning them new technical labels:

- **Author:** God plants the Garden, places the man, gives provision, and issues the command.
- **Created setting:** the Garden and its trees are presented as God's work.
- **Assigned human activity:** the man is placed there to work/serve and keep/guard.
- **Boundary:** the command concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.
- **Consequence:** disobedience, expulsion, and guarded access to the tree of life.

These are narrative observations, not a new GUA-W sequence.

The map's governing distinction remains: the man is not the Author of the Garden, the source of life, or the author of the command. His assigned activity is real but derivative and bounded.

The question about Purpose and Constraint → Structure remains open. This study does not add Purpose as a formal layer or claim that sanctuary imagery resolves the architecture question.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

Genesis describes a Garden planted by God, a man placed there with a two-part assignment, divine presence, a command, expulsion, and cherubims guarding the way to the tree of life.

The *‘abad*–*shamar* pairing has a relevant parallel in Levitical sanctuary service. Cherubims and guarded access also recur in later sanctuary descriptions. Taken together, these details support a serious sanctuary reading, though the exact status and extent of that interpretation remain debated.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The Witness can miss meaningful patterns when it studies each verse in isolation. It can also overreach by turning a pattern into proof before evaluating context and counterarguments.

Faithful witnessing requires cumulative reasoning, fair treatment of disagreement, and clear distinctions among text, parallel, inference, and interpretation.

## 11. Findings

1. Genesis 2:15 assigns the man work/service and keeping/guarding within the Garden.
2. The paired Hebrew verbs have a meaningful canonical parallel in passages describing Levitical duties associated with the tabernacle.
3. Genesis 3:24 uses *shamar* for the guarding of the way to the tree of life, creating a narrative connection with the man's earlier charge.
4. Cherubims appear both at Eden's guarded access and in later sanctuary/temple descriptions.
5. Genesis depicts divine presence in the Garden and a command given by the Creator.
6. Taken cumulatively, these features support a serious interpretation of Eden as sacred space with sanctuary or temple-like features.
7. The evidence does not establish that Adam held the later institutional Levitical office, nor that Eden was identical in every respect to the Mosaic tabernacle.
8. The agricultural and cultivation dimension of Genesis 2:5–15 must be retained; sanctuary interpretation does not erase the literal Garden and ground.
9. The sanctuary interpretation remains a well-supported but debated theological reading, not a direct statement that Genesis calls Eden a temple.
10. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
