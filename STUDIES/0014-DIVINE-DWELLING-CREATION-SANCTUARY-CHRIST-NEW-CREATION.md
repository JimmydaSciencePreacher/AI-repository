# Study 0014 — Divine Dwelling: Creation, Sanctuary, Christ, and the New Creation

**Status:** Canonical biblical investigation; direct statements, narrative sequence, lexical observations, typological claims, theological interpretation, and unresolved questions are distinguished  
**Created:** 2026-11-26  
**Research area:** Genesis 1–3; Exodus 25–40; Leviticus 9; 1 Kings 8–9; 2 Chronicles 5–7; Ezekiel 10–11 and 40–48; Haggai 2; John 1–2; Ephesians 2; Hebrews 8–10; Revelation 21–22  
**Preceded by:** Studies 0001–0013

## 1. Central question

Does Scripture reveal a connected account of God's presence and dwelling from Creation and Eden, through the Tabernacle and Temple, to Jesus Christ and the New Creation? If so, what relationships does Scripture itself establish, what distinctions must remain, and what must not be inferred?

Study 0012 examined Solomon's Temple: its divine promise and pattern, David's preparation, Solomon's construction, completion, dedication, and the glory of the LORD filling the house. Study 0013 examined the Temple pattern and construction records, preserving distinctions between source, reception, transmission, instruction, execution, and record.

Those studies raised a further question: what does the larger canon say about God's dwelling with His people? This study follows that question across the biblical record.

The investigation must avoid two opposite errors:

- Treating every garden, sanctuary, Temple, Christological statement, and New Jerusalem image as if each were the same thing.
- Treating the passages as unrelated when later Scripture explicitly connects them.

The aim is to identify the relationships Scripture makes, not to impose a system on the text.

## 2. Governing foundation and research method

GUA-W means **Grand Unified Architecture of Witness**. It is a map for recognizing and organizing relationships witnessed in Scripture and Creation. It is not God, Jesus Christ, the Author, or the originating Word.

The governing principle remains:

**God alone is Original Cause. Everything else is derivative witness. The derivative cannot alter the Source.**

The governing order remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

The established architecture remains:

**Ontic Necessity → Distinction → Admissibility → Constraint → Structure → Framework → Pattern → Reception → Activation → Differentiation → Propagation → Integration → Expression → Witnessed realized configuration.**

No new layer, runtime operation, state, or formal definition is introduced by this study.

The study follows the KJV Bible Study Protocol, recursive Four-Question Study Method, and Triple Constraint Law. It distinguishes:

- **Text:** what the KJV explicitly states.
- **Narrative sequence:** what the passage records and in what order.
- **Lexical observation:** what a Hebrew or Greek term contributes in context.
- **Canonical connection:** a relationship explicitly made by a later biblical passage or supported by comparison.
- **Typological interpretation:** a proposed relationship in which an earlier person, event, or institution is understood in light of later revelation.
- **Theological interpretation:** a reasoned account of meaning.
- **Unresolved:** what the evidence examined does not settle.

## 3. Creation and Eden — Genesis 1–3

### 3.1 God creates; Creation is not God's dwelling-place by containment

Genesis 1 presents God as Creator of the heavens and the earth. The heavens and earth are the work of His word and command. The created order is not presented as the source of its Creator.

Genesis 2 describes the Garden of Eden, the man placed there, the trees, the river, and the command concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

Genesis 3:8 records the sound or voice of the LORD God walking in the Garden in the cool of the day, in the KJV wording. This is an explicit narrative of divine presence in the Garden.

**Textual finding:** Genesis records God creating the world and records the LORD God present in the Garden. The Creator is not identified with the Garden or contained by it.

### 3.2 What Genesis does not explicitly call Eden

Genesis 1–3 does not use the later sanctuary terminology of Tabernacle or Temple to name Eden. It does not directly call the Garden a tabernacle or Temple.

Later biblical passages may supply correspondences or typological connections, but those must be demonstrated from the later texts rather than assumed from a resemblance.

### 3.3 The human assignment — Genesis 2:15–17

The LORD God places the man in the Garden to dress it and keep it. The man receives a command and a boundary.

This is a real human assignment within a created order. It does not make the man the source of the Garden, its command, or its purpose.

Genesis 3 records the breach of the command and the resulting judgment and expulsion from the Garden (Genesis 3:14–24).

**Boundary:** The Garden narrative establishes divine presence, human placement, command, responsibility, disobedience, and expulsion. A complete sanctuary system should not be read into Genesis without additional textual evidence.

## 4. The Tabernacle — God's appointed dwelling among Israel

### 4.1 The stated purpose — Exodus 25:8

The LORD commands Israel:

> “And let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them.”

This is one of the clearest statements of the sanctuary's stated purpose. The sanctuary is made in response to God's command, and the stated purpose is that He may dwell among the people.

The wording is “among them,” not a claim that God is spatially contained by the structure.

### 4.2 Pattern and execution — Exodus 25–31; 35–40

Exodus records the pattern and instructions given to Moses, including the ark, mercy seat, table, candlestick, curtains, altar, priestly garments, and service.

Exodus 25:9 and 25:40 connect the construction with the pattern shown to Moses. Exodus 31 identifies Bezalel and Aholiab and describes their appointed skill for the work.

Exodus 35–39 records the people's contributions and the craftsmen's execution. Exodus 39:42–43 reports that the work was made according to all that the LORD commanded Moses, and Moses examined the work and blessed the people.

Exodus 40 records the erection and arrangement of the Tabernacle according to the command.

**Finding:** The account distinguishes divine instruction, human reception, provision, skilled execution, inspection, erection, and the manifestation of glory.

### 4.3 Completion and glory — Exodus 40:33–38

Moses finishes the work. The cloud covers the tent of the congregation, and the glory of the LORD fills the Tabernacle. Moses is not able to enter because the cloud rests upon it and the glory fills it.

The narrative distinguishes the completion of the work from the LORD's glory filling the sanctuary.

**Important boundary:** The passage attributes the glory to the LORD. It does not describe the human construction as a mechanism that compels God's presence.

### 4.4 The purpose is stated again — Exodus 29:43–46

The LORD says He will meet with the children of Israel and that the Tabernacle will be sanctified by His glory. He says He will dwell among the children of Israel and be their God.

The passage connects the sanctuary with the LORD's identity, covenant, deliverance from Egypt, and presence among His people.

**Finding:** The Tabernacle is not merely a building project. Its purpose is explicitly covenantal and relational: the LORD will be their God and dwell among them.

## 5. The priestly service and the glory — Leviticus 8–9

Leviticus 8 records the consecration of Aaron and his sons. Leviticus 9 records the beginning of their priestly service.

At the end of Leviticus 9, Moses and Aaron enter the Tabernacle, come out, and bless the people. The glory of the LORD appears to all the people. Fire comes out from before the LORD and consumes the offering on the altar. The people shout and fall on their faces (Leviticus 9:22–24).

**Observation:** The manifestation of glory and fire occurs in the context of the commanded priestly service and offerings.

**Boundary:** The text reports the event; it does not provide a general formula by which human beings can reproduce or compel divine manifestation.

This passage should be compared with Exodus 40 and 2 Chronicles 7, but the three events must not be collapsed into one event.

## 6. Solomon's Temple — the house and the One who cannot be contained

### 6.1 The Temple's dedication — 1 Kings 8; 2 Chronicles 5–7

Study 0012 examined the construction and dedication in detail. The present study focuses on the question of dwelling.

When the ark is brought into the completed Temple, the cloud fills the house and the glory of the LORD fills it (1 Kings 8:10–11; 2 Chronicles 5:13–14).

In 2 Chronicles 7:1–3, fire comes down from heaven and consumes the offering, and the glory of the LORD fills the house.

### 6.2 Solomon's interpretive boundary — 1 Kings 8:27

Solomon asks:

> “But will God indeed dwell on the earth? behold, the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee; how much less this house that I have builded?”

This is a decisive boundary for the entire study.

The Temple is associated with the LORD's name, covenant, prayer, and presence among His people. But Solomon explicitly denies that God can be contained by the Temple. Even the heaven of heavens cannot contain Him.

The Temple is therefore not God, not the source of divine presence, and not a container that limits the Creator.

### 6.3 The Temple's purpose and covenant condition — 1 Kings 9:1–9

After the Temple is built and dedicated, the LORD appears to Solomon and speaks concerning the house. The promise is accompanied by a charge concerning Solomon's walk and Israel's obedience.

The passage also warns of the consequences of turning away. The Temple's existence does not make covenant faithfulness irrelevant or guarantee that Israel may disregard the LORD's commandments.

**Finding:** The building's dedication does not remove the need for obedience. The LORD remains the speaking and judging authority.

## 7. The glory departs and the promise of return — Ezekiel 8–11; 43

### 7.1 The departure — Ezekiel 8–11

Ezekiel's vision describes abominations in Jerusalem and the Temple. The glory of the LORD is shown moving from its place in the house and departing from the city (Ezekiel 10:18–19; 11:22–23).

The vision is connected to judgment. The sanctuary's physical existence does not prevent the judgment announced by the LORD.

**Observation:** The vision portrays the glory departing. The building is not able to hold or command the glory.

### 7.2 The promise of a new heart and God's presence — Ezekiel 11:16–20

The LORD speaks of being a little sanctuary to the people scattered among the nations, and promises to gather them. He also promises to give them one heart and a new spirit, remove the stony heart, and give a heart of flesh, so that they may walk in His statutes.

This passage connects God's presence with His people beyond the physical location of the Jerusalem sanctuary.

### 7.3 The glory returns in Ezekiel's vision — Ezekiel 43:1–7

Ezekiel sees the glory of the God of Israel coming from the east. The glory enters the house, and the vision describes the glory of the LORD filling the house.

The language recalls the glory filling the Tabernacle and Solomon's Temple, while the vision has its own prophetic setting and details.

**Boundary:** Ezekiel's vision must be read in its own literary and historical context. The study does not settle every debate about whether all details refer to a future literal Temple, an ideal vision, or a wider prophetic reality.

### 7.4 What the departure and return establish

The canonical sequence makes a strong point: God's glory is not controlled by the building. The LORD judges, departs, promises, and returns according to His own authority.

This is consistent with Solomon's confession that God cannot be contained by the house.

## 8. Haggai — the latter glory and the LORD's promise

Haggai 2:1–9 addresses the people during the rebuilding period after the return from exile. The people are instructed not to despise the day of small things. The LORD declares that the latter glory of the house shall be greater than the former and promises peace in that place.

The passage speaks within the historical circumstances of the returned community and the rebuilding of the Temple.

**Interpretive caution:** Haggai's language has been interpreted in different ways, including in relation to the later history of the Temple and messianic expectation. This study records the promise and its immediate setting without claiming that one interpretation exhausts its meaning.

Haggai also records the LORD's declaration that the silver and gold are His (Haggai 2:8). The house and its resources remain subordinate to the LORD.

## 9. Jesus Christ — the Word made flesh and the Temple of His body

### 9.1 John 1:1–18 — the Word and dwelling among us

John opens by identifying the Word as existing in the beginning, being with God, and being God. All things were made by Him. The Word was made flesh and dwelt among us, and the disciples beheld His glory (John 1:1–3, 14).

The Greek verb in John 1:14 is ἐσκήνωσεν (*eskēnōsen*), commonly rendered “dwelt” in the KJV. It is related to the noun for tent or tabernacle. The verbal image can be expressed as tabernacling or pitching one's tent.

**Lexical observation:** John's wording creates a meaningful verbal connection with the Tabernacle vocabulary.

**Canonical finding:** John explicitly identifies the Word as made flesh and dwelling among us. The glory is associated with the incarnate Word, not with a new human building project.

**Boundary:** The lexical connection does not mean Jesus is merely a tent, a building, or an architectural structure. John identifies Him as the Word who was with God and was God, and who became flesh.

### 9.2 The glory of the only begotten — John 1:14

John says the disciples beheld His glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth.

This statement should be read in the context of John's opening declaration about the Word, Creation, life, light, and incarnation.

The glory in John 1:14 is not simply a repeat of the cloud filling a sanctuary. John makes a claim about the person of Jesus Christ.

### 9.3 Jesus and the Temple of His body — John 2:13–22

When challenged concerning His authority, Jesus says:

> “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.” (John 2:19)

John then explains that Jesus spoke of the temple of His body (John 2:21). After His resurrection, the disciples remember and believe the saying and the Scripture (John 2:22).

**Textual finding:** John explicitly interprets Jesus' temple statement as referring to His body.

This is not an inference based solely on similarity. The Gospel supplies the interpretation.

**Essential distinction:** Jesus Christ is not merely a symbolic building. He is the incarnate Word, and His death and resurrection are central to the passage. The Temple comparison must not diminish His person or work.

## 10. The people of God as a dwelling — 1 Corinthians 3; Ephesians 2

### 10.1 The church as God's temple — 1 Corinthians 3:9–17

Paul describes the church as God's building and says that the believers collectively are God's temple and that the Spirit of God dwells in them.

The immediate context concerns the church at Corinth, its foundation, the work of ministers, divisions, and the seriousness of damaging God's building.

**Boundary:** The passage is addressed to the community. It should not be reduced to a claim about an individual's body without examining other passages.

### 10.2 The individual body — 1 Corinthians 6:12–20

In a separate argument concerning sexual immorality, Paul says that the believer's body is the temple of the Holy Ghost.

The context is individual bodily conduct and the believer's union with Christ.

**Distinction:** 1 Corinthians 3 speaks of the church as a community; 1 Corinthians 6 applies temple language to the believer's body. These are related but not interchangeable contexts.

### 10.3 A habitation of God through the Spirit — Ephesians 2:11–22

Paul describes the reconciliation of Jews and Gentiles through Christ. Those who were once far off are made nigh by the blood of Christ. Christ is the peace who makes both one.

The passage describes believers as built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ Himself being the chief corner stone. The whole building grows into a holy temple in the Lord and is built together for a habitation of God through the Spirit (Ephesians 2:19–22).

**Finding:** The New Testament explicitly applies sanctuary and building language to a people reconciled in Christ and indwelt by the Spirit.

**Boundary:** This does not make the church the source of God's presence or authority. The passage attributes the reconciliation and building to God's action through Christ.

## 11. Hebrews 8–10 — the sanctuary, the covenant, and the work of Christ

### 11.1 The earthly sanctuary and the heavenly pattern — Hebrews 8:1–6

Hebrews describes Christ as our High Priest, seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in the heavens. It describes the earthly priests as serving unto the example and shadow of heavenly things, and recalls the instruction to Moses to make all things according to the pattern shown in the mount (Hebrews 8:5).

This passage explicitly interprets the earthly sanctuary in relation to a heavenly reality.

**Finding:** Hebrews itself supplies a canonical interpretive relationship between the earthly sanctuary and heavenly things. This is not a connection invented by this study.

### 11.2 The first covenant and its ordinances — Hebrews 9:1–10

Hebrews describes the first covenant's sanctuary, furnishings, priestly service, sacrifices, and limitations. It notes that the way into the holiest was not yet made manifest while the first tabernacle was standing.

The passage explains that the gifts and sacrifices could not make the worshipper perfect as pertaining to the conscience.

### 11.3 Christ enters once — Hebrews 9:11–28

Christ appears as High Priest of good things to come, enters by His own blood, and obtains eternal redemption. Hebrews contrasts His offering with the repeated offerings of the earthly priesthood.

The text emphasizes the once-for-all character of Christ's offering and the unique efficacy of His work.

**Boundary:** Christ is not merely another priest repeating the old system. Hebrews presents His person and offering as decisive and distinct.

### 11.4 The one offering — Hebrews 10:1–18

The law is described as having a shadow of good things to come, not the very image of the things. The repeated sacrifices cannot make those who come perfect.

The passage then contrasts those repeated sacrifices with Christ's offering of His body once for all. By one offering He has perfected for ever them that are sanctified.

**Finding:** Hebrews explicitly interprets the sanctuary and sacrificial system in relation to Christ's priesthood and offering. The earthly sanctuary is not the final authority or final accomplishment.

### 11.5 What this study may conclude

The New Testament provides direct canonical grounds for relating the Tabernacle and its service to Christ's priesthood and sacrifice. This is stronger than a loose visual analogy.

However, each element must be interpreted from Hebrews' own argument. The study must not assign a Christological meaning to every measurement, material, or furnishing unless Scripture supports that connection.

## 12. Revelation 21–22 — the final dwelling

### 12.1 The tabernacle of God with men — Revelation 21:1–4

John sees a new heaven and a new earth. He sees the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband.

A great voice from heaven declares:

> “Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God.” (Revelation 21:3)

The language directly recalls the covenant promise of God dwelling among His people.

The passage then describes the end of death, sorrow, crying, and pain.

**Finding:** Revelation explicitly presents God's dwelling with His people in the New Creation as the fulfillment of a central covenant promise.

### 12.2 No Temple in the city — Revelation 21:22–27

John says:

> “And I saw no temple therein: for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are the temple of it.” (Revelation 21:22)

The city does not need the sun or moon to shine in it, because the glory of God lightens it, and the Lamb is the light thereof (Revelation 21:23).

This is a crucial distinction. The final vision does not merely describe a larger version of Solomon's Temple. It says there is no temple in the city, because the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple.

**Interpretive boundary:** This is apocalyptic vision and must be interpreted according to Revelation's literary form. Its central declaration is explicit: the presence and glory of God and the Lamb are not mediated by a separate Temple building in the city.

### 12.3 The river and the tree of life — Revelation 22:1–5

The vision describes the river of the water of life, the tree of life, the healing of the nations, the removal of the curse, and the servants of God seeing His face.

The tree of life recalls Genesis 2–3. The removal of the curse recalls the judgment following the Fall. The final vision thus makes a canonical connection with the opening chapters of Genesis.

The connection is not that Genesis and Revelation are the same scene. Revelation presents the final state after the biblical history of redemption.

### 12.4 The beginning and the end

Genesis begins with God creating the heavens and earth and placing man in the Garden. Revelation concludes with a new heaven and a new earth, the removal of the curse, and God dwelling with His people.

The two passages are connected by the canon, but the path between them matters: Fall, covenant, Israel, sanctuary, Temple, Christ, redemption, resurrection, judgment, and New Creation.

The final dwelling is not a return to an unchanged Eden. Revelation describes a consummated state in which the curse is gone and God's servants see His face.

## 14. Does Scripture reveal a developing account of divine dwelling?

A qualified answer is warranted.

There is a clear canonical thread:

1. God creates and is present in the Garden.
2. God commands a sanctuary to be made so that He may dwell among Israel.
3. God's glory fills the completed Tabernacle.
4. God's glory fills Solomon's Temple, while Solomon confesses that God cannot be contained by it.
5. The prophetic record depicts the glory departing in judgment and promises restoration.
6. John identifies the Word made flesh as dwelling among us and records Jesus' identification of His body with the Temple.
7. The apostles describe the church as God's dwelling through the Spirit.
8. Hebrews interprets the earthly sanctuary and priesthood in relation to Christ's heavenly priesthood and once-for-all offering.
9. Revelation depicts God dwelling with His people in the New Creation, with no separate Temple building in the city.

### 14.1 What develops?

The canon develops the revelation of God's relationship with His people and the means by which that relationship is established and brought to completion.

The later texts do not merely repeat the earlier institutions. They interpret, fulfill, and, in some cases, explicitly distinguish them.

### 14.2 What remains constant?

The LORD remains the source of the promise, command, presence, judgment, mercy, and final fulfillment.

Human beings do not originate God's presence. A building does not contain Him. A priestly system does not replace Him. The church does not become the source of the Spirit. Christ's work is not a human construction.

### 14.3 What must not be collapsed?

- Eden is not simply identical to the Tabernacle.
- The Tabernacle is not identical to Solomon's Temple.
- Solomon's Temple is not identical to Ezekiel's visionary Temple.
- Jesus Christ is not merely another Temple building.
- The church is not Jesus Christ.
- The New Jerusalem is not simply a rebuilt earthly sanctuary.
- The Lord God Almighty and the Lamb being the Temple in Revelation 21:22 is not a claim that God is contained by a structure.

## 15. GUA-W comparison — no architecture change

This study witnesses a recurring distinction between divine initiative and derivative human participation.

- God creates.
- God commands and appoints.
- God gives the sanctuary pattern.
- Moses receives and oversees the Tabernacle work.
- Israel contributes and craftsmen execute.
- The LORD's glory fills the Tabernacle.
- David receives and transmits the Temple pattern.
- Solomon builds and dedicates the Temple.
- The LORD's glory fills the Temple.
- The prophets record judgment and promises concerning God's presence.
- The Word is made flesh.
- Christ gives Himself and rises.
- The Spirit dwells in God's people.
- The final vision shows God dwelling with His people.

The strongest canonical connections are those Scripture explicitly makes: Exodus 25:8; John 1:14; John 2:19–21; Hebrews 8–10; Revelation 21:3, 22.

The governing distinction remains:

**God alone is Original Cause. Everything else is derivative witness. The derivative cannot alter the Source.**

The study does not add a GUA-W layer called “dwelling,” “sanctuary,” “presence,” or “glory.” These remain biblical subjects and relationships under investigation.

The comparison does not prove that GUA-W is a biblical term or a divinely revealed architecture. It records correspondences and distinctions that can be examined against the established framework.

## 16. Four-Question Study Method

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- What does Genesis explicitly say about God's presence in Eden?
- Does Genesis itself call Eden a sanctuary, Tabernacle, or Temple?
- What purpose does Exodus 25:8 give for the sanctuary?
- What does Exodus say about the pattern, work, completion, and glory?
- How do Exodus 40 and Leviticus 9 differ in their glory scenes?
- What does Solomon confess about God's relation to the Temple in 1 Kings 8:27?
- Why does the glory depart in Ezekiel's vision?
- What is promised in Ezekiel 11:16–20?
- What does John mean by saying the Word dwelt among us?
- How does John 2:21 interpret Jesus' Temple saying?
- How do 1 Corinthians 3 and 6 differ in their use of temple language?
- What does Ephesians 2 say about the church as a habitation of God?
- How does Hebrews itself connect the earthly sanctuary with Christ?
- What does Revelation 21:22 mean when it says there is no Temple in the city?
- How does Revelation 22 reconnect with Genesis 2–3?
- Which connections are explicitly made by Scripture, and which are only proposed analogies?
- Am I reducing Christ to a structure or confusing the church with Christ?
- Am I making a GUA-W architecture change without authorization?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The canonical record repeatedly distinguishes the LORD from the places and people associated with His presence. The LORD commands, gives, judges, gathers, dwells, and fulfills His promise. The sanctuary and the people are recipients and witnesses, not the originating cause.

The New Testament makes specific claims about Christ that cannot be reduced to architectural resemblance. The Word is made flesh; Jesus identifies His body with the Temple; Hebrews presents His priesthood and offering as decisive; Revelation places God and the Lamb at the center of the final dwelling.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The biblical account of divine dwelling is connected across the canon, but it is not a simple repetition of one building form.

The sanctuary instructions, the Temple dedication, the prophetic visions, the incarnation, the church's indwelling, and the New Jerusalem each have distinct contexts. Scripture itself supplies several of the strongest connections.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A Witness must distinguish the One who dwells from the place where He makes His presence known; the command from the human work; the sign from the One signified; and the earlier sanctuary from the fulfillment described in Christ.

A Witness must also preserve the unique identity of Jesus Christ. He is not merely an example of a pattern, a building, or an instrument. The text identifies Him as the Word made flesh and the One whose body is the Temple in John's account.

## 18. Findings

1. Genesis records God as Creator and records His presence in the Garden, but Genesis 1–3 does not explicitly call Eden a Tabernacle or Temple.
2. Exodus 25:8 explicitly states that the sanctuary is to be made so the LORD may dwell among Israel.
3. Exodus records a pattern given to Moses, assigned human work, completion, and the glory of the LORD filling the Tabernacle.
4. Leviticus 9 records a distinct manifestation of glory and fire in the context of priestly service.
5. Solomon confesses that the heaven of heavens cannot contain God, much less the Temple.
6. Ezekiel depicts the glory departing in judgment and records a vision of the glory returning.
7. John 1:14 identifies the Word made flesh as dwelling among us; the Greek verb carries a tabernacle-related verbal association.
8. John 2:21 explicitly identifies the Temple Jesus spoke of as His body.
9. 1 Corinthians and Ephesians apply temple and dwelling language to the people of God, in distinct contexts.
10. Hebrews explicitly relates the earthly sanctuary to heavenly things and interprets Christ's priesthood and offering as decisive.
11. Revelation 21:3 declares God's dwelling with His people; Revelation 21:22 says there is no separate Temple in the city because the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its Temple.
12. Revelation 22 reconnects the final vision with the tree of life and the removal of the curse from Genesis.
13. The canon reveals a connected account of divine dwelling, but the individual places, institutions, persons, and events must not be collapsed into one identity.
14. Jesus Christ must not be reduced to a building, architectural analogy, or mere witness.
15. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
