# Study 0018 — The Spirit, the Word, and the Breath of Life

**Status:** Biblical investigation; Hebrew and Greek observations, direct textual statements, canonical synthesis, theological interpretation, and unresolved questions distinguished  
**Created:** 2026-11-26  
**Research area:** Genesis 1:1–3; Genesis 2:7; Psalm 33:6; Job 33:4; Psalm 104:27–30; John 1:1–4; John 3:1–8; Hebrews 1:1–3  
**Preceded by:** Study 0017 — The Word, the Life, and the Light

## 1. Central question

What is the biblical relationship between:

- the **Spirit of God** in Genesis 1:2;
- the **Word** through whom all things were made in John 1:1–3; and
- the **breath of life** by which man became a living soul in Genesis 2:7?

Are these expressions identical, related, or distinct? Which relationships does Scripture explicitly establish, and which are theological synthesis?

A particularly important comparison is Psalm 33:6:

> “By the word of the LORD were the heavens made; and all the host of them by the breath of his mouth.”

This verse places “the word of the LORD” and “the breath of his mouth” in one statement about the making of the heavens and their host. It deserves close examination. It does not automatically prove that every biblical use of “breath,” “spirit,” and “Word” names the same referent or describes the same operation.

## 2. Scope and method

This study examines the KJV passages in their immediate contexts and compares their Hebrew and Greek wording where relevant. It does not attempt to settle every doctrine concerning the Trinity, the human soul, the origin of life, or the scientific mechanisms of biological respiration.

The investigation distinguishes:

1. **Source statement:** what the biblical text explicitly says.
2. **Lexical observation:** what the relevant Hebrew or Greek term can mean and how it is used.
3. **Canonical comparison:** how separate passages illuminate one another.
4. **Theological synthesis:** a conclusion drawn from multiple passages.
5. **Analogy:** a comparison that may clarify a relation but does not establish identity or mechanism.
6. **Unresolved question:** what the available passages do not settle.

The study follows the repository's Research Method, Sources and Evidence, and Study Format guides. The KJV is the principal English text. Lexical references are aids to examining the source wording, not authorities that replace the biblical context.

## 3. Governing GUA-W foundation

GUA-W means **Grand Unified Architecture of Witness**. It is a map for recognizing and organizing relationships witnessed in Scripture and Creation. It is not God, Jesus Christ, the Author, or the originating Word.

The governing principle remains:

**God alone is Original Cause. Everything else is derivative witness. The derivative cannot alter the Source.**

The governing relationship remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

John 1:1 must govern how this relationship is expressed: “the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” The ordering must not imply that the Word is a creature or outside God's divine identity.

The established architecture remains:

No new layer, runtime operation, state, or formal definition is introduced by this study. Spirit, Word, breath, life, and living soul remain biblical subjects and relationships under investigation, not new GUA-W components.

## 4. Genesis 1:1–3 — God, the Spirit, and the spoken command

### 4.1 Genesis 1:1

> “In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.”

**Direct observation:** God is identified as Creator. Heaven and earth are the created objects named in the verse.

The verse does not explain the physical mechanism of creation. It establishes the Creator–creation distinction.

### 4.2 Genesis 1:2

The KJV describes the earth as without form and void, darkness upon the face of the deep, and “the Spirit of God” moving upon the face of the waters.

The Hebrew expression translated “the Spirit of God” is רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים (*ruach Elohim*).

The noun רוּחַ (*ruach*) has a range that includes wind, breath, and spirit. Context is necessary to determine the sense in a particular passage. In Genesis 1:2, the KJV renders the phrase “the Spirit of God.” The study retains that translation and records the lexical range without replacing the verse's wording with an alternative.

The verb מְרַחֶפֶת (*meraḥephet*, from רחף *raḥaph*) is rendered “moved” in the KJV. The form describes movement over or upon the waters; Deuteronomy 32:11 uses the related image of an eagle fluttering over its young. That parallel can illuminate the image of movement, but it does not by itself establish a complete physical description of what occurred in Genesis 1:2.

**Direct observations:**

- The Spirit of God is named.
- The Spirit is described as moving upon the face of the waters.
- The verse does not call the Spirit a created thing.
- The verse does not explicitly explain the relation between the Spirit's movement and the command in verse 3.

### 4.3 Genesis 1:3

> “And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.”

The text distinguishes God's speaking from the resulting light. God speaks; light comes to be.

The verse does not use the Hebrew noun *ruach* or the Greek noun *logos*. It says God said. The comparison with John 1 and Psalm 33 is a canonical comparison, not a claim that the words in the different passages are lexically identical.

### 4.4 What Genesis 1:1–3 establishes — and what it does not

It establishes God as Creator, names the Spirit of God in the scene, and records God's spoken command followed by the appearance of light.

It does not, in these three verses alone, provide a full systematic account of the relation among Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Later canonical passages must be read in their own contexts and brought into comparison without forcing every later term into Genesis' wording.

## 5. Genesis 2:7 — formed man, breath of life, living soul

The KJV reads:

> “And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.”

### 5.1 The actions recorded

The verse identifies the LORD God as the acting subject:

1. God formed man of the dust of the ground.
2. God breathed into man's nostrils the breath of life.
3. Man became a living soul.

The verse does not describe man as self-originating. His formation and the life-giving action are attributed to God.

### 5.2 Hebrew terms

| KJV wording | Hebrew | Transliteration | Lexical observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| man | אָדָם | *'adam* | Man, mankind; in this context the man formed. |
| ground | אֲדָמָה | *'adamah* | Ground, land, soil. |
| breathed | נָפַח | *naphach* | To breathe, blow, or puff. |
| breath | נְשָׁמָה | *neshamah* | Breath; often associated with the breath of life. |
| life | חַיִּים | *chayyim* | Life; the form in this expression is plural in form. |
| living soul | נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה | *nephesh chayyah* | Living being / living creature / living soul, according to context. |

### 5.3 An essential lexical distinction

Genesis 2:7 uses נְשָׁמָה (*neshamah*) in “breath of life.” Genesis 1:2 uses רוּחַ (*ruach*) in “Spirit of God.”

These are not the same Hebrew word.

Both words can overlap in semantic territory associated with breath or life in some contexts, but lexical overlap does not make them interchangeable in every verse. Genesis 2:7 does not say, in its Hebrew wording, “God breathed the *ruach* of life.” It uses *neshamah*.

This distinction is central to the investigation. It prevents an attractive word association from becoming an inaccurate quotation.

### 5.4 What does “man became a living soul” say?

The Hebrew expression is *nephesh chayyah*. The same expression is used in Genesis 1 for living creatures (Genesis 1:20, 21, 24), and appears again in Genesis 2:19 in reference to the animals.

This does not mean that man and animals are identical in every respect. It does mean that the phrase *nephesh chayyah* itself cannot be treated as a term that, by its lexical meaning alone, distinguishes an immortal human soul from animal life.

Genesis 2:7 states that the formed man became a living being when God breathed into him the breath of life. Wider biblical teaching about human personhood, death, resurrection, and life with God must be considered from the relevant passages rather than loaded into this one expression.

### 5.5 What the verse does not say

Genesis 2:7 does not explicitly say:

- that the breath of life is the Holy Spirit;
- that the man's *neshamah* is identical to the Spirit of God in Genesis 1:2;
- that the human soul is a detachable substance;
- that the physical mechanics of respiration are fully described;
- that the word *nephesh* by itself settles every question about consciousness or immortality.

Those may be questions for broader biblical theology, but they are not direct statements of this verse.

## 6. Psalm 33:6 — the Word and the breath of His mouth

The KJV reads:

> “By the word of the LORD were the heavens made; and all the host of them by the breath of his mouth.”

This is a particularly important text because it places two expressions together:

- “the word of the LORD”;
- “the breath of his mouth.”

### 6.1 Hebrew wording

- דְּבַר (*devar*) — word, speech, matter, or utterance; here in the construct phrase “word of the LORD.”
- רוּחַ (*ruach*) — breath, wind, or spirit; here rendered “breath.”
- פִּיו (*piv*) — his mouth.

The verse uses *ruach* for “breath,” while Genesis 2:7 uses *neshamah* for the breath breathed into man's nostrils. The difference in wording should be retained.

### 6.2 Immediate context: Psalm 33:4–9

Psalm 33 speaks of the word of the LORD as right and His works as done in truth. It describes His lovingkindness, the heavens, the waters, the earth, and the nations. Verse 9 says:

> “For he spake, and it was done; he commanded, and it stood fast.”

The immediate context therefore connects the LORD's word, His breath, His making of the heavens and their host, and His command.

### 6.3 What Psalm 33:6 supports

The verse supports a close relationship between divine speech and the breath of God's mouth in the description of Creation. It is legitimate to say that the psalm presents the heavens and their host as made by the LORD's word and breath.

### 6.4 What Psalm 33:6 does not settle by itself

The verse does not explicitly say that:

- the Hebrew noun *ruach* here is a direct title for the Holy Spirit;
- the word *devar* and the word *ruach* are identical in meaning;
- every occurrence of *ruach* and *neshamah* refers to the same thing;
- the breath given to Adam in Genesis 2:7 is the same specific event as the breath of God's mouth in Psalm 33:6;
- the verse supplies a physical mechanism for cosmological creation.

The poetic parallelism may express closely related aspects of God's effective utterance. Whether it should be read as strict synonymy, complementary description, or another form of Hebrew poetic parallelism requires care. The safest conclusion is the one the verse clearly supports: the LORD's word and the breath of His mouth are both named in the making of the heavens and their host.

## 7. Job 33:4 — Spirit of God and breath of the Almighty

The KJV reads:

> “The Spirit of God hath made me, and the breath of the Almighty hath given me life.”

This verse brings *ruach* and *neshamah* into one statement.

### 7.1 Hebrew terms

- רוּחַ אֵל (*ruach El*) — Spirit of God.
- נִשְׁמַת שַׁדַּי (*nishmat Shaddai*) — breath of the Almighty.
- חַיָּתְנִי (*chayyathni*) — hath given me life / made me live.

### 7.2 Direct observation

Elihu says that the Spirit of God made him and the breath of the Almighty gave him life. The verse places the two expressions in parallel in a statement about his creaturely life.

This is stronger evidence for a close conceptual relationship between God's Spirit and life-giving breath than Genesis 2:7 alone provides. It still does not make the two Hebrew nouns lexically identical.

### 7.3 Contextual boundary

Job 33 is part of Elihu's speech. The statement is Scripture, but its speaker and rhetorical context should be noted. The verse is a direct statement by Elihu about his own creaturely dependence on God. It can be compared with Genesis 2:7 and Psalm 104, but it should not be detached from its context and made to answer every question about the Spirit.

## 8. Psalm 104:27–30 — breath, death, and renewal

Psalm 104 describes God's provision for creatures. They wait upon Him for food; He gives and they gather; He opens His hand and they are filled with good.

Verses 29–30 describe what happens when God hides His face, takes away their breath, and sends forth His spirit.

The KJV says:

> “Thou hidest thy face, they are troubled: thou takest away their breath, they die, and return to their dust.
>
> Thou sendest forth thy spirit, they are created: and thou renewest the face of the earth.”

### 8.1 Direct observations

- Creatures depend on God's provision.
- When their breath is taken away, they die and return to dust.
- God sends forth His spirit, and creatures are created.
- God renews the face of the earth.

### 8.2 Hebrew distinction

Verse 29 uses רוּחָם (*ruacham*, their breath/spirit) in the phrase rendered “their breath.” Verse 30 uses רוּחֲךָ (*ruachak*, thy spirit) in “thy spirit.”

The same Hebrew root appears in both verses. The KJV renders the first as “breath” and the second as “spirit,” showing how context guides translation.

### 8.3 What this adds

Psalm 104 places creaturely life and death under God's sustaining authority. It also associates God's sending forth His spirit with creation and renewal.

This passage strengthens the canonical relationship among God's Spirit, creaturely life, and renewal. It does not, by itself, define the precise mechanism or settle every question about the relation of the Holy Spirit to ordinary creaturely respiration.

## 9. John 1:1–4 — the Word and life

John 1:1 says the Word was in the beginning, was with God, and was God. John 1:3 says all things that were made were made by Him. John 1:4 says:

> “In him was life; and the life was the light of men.”

### Direct findings carried forward from Study 0017

- The Word was in the beginning.
- The Word was with God.
- The Word was God.
- All things that were made were made by Him.
- Life was in Him.
- That life was the light of men.

John does not use the Genesis 2:7 noun *neshamah* in these verses. He uses ζωή (*zōē*), translated “life.” The Greek term has its own range and Johannine usage. It must not be treated as a Greek translation of one specific Hebrew word in Genesis 2:7 without a textual basis.

### Comparison

Genesis 2:7 describes man's life as given through God's breathing into the formed man. John 1:4 locates life in the Word, through whom all created things were made.

A canonical synthesis can recognize that creaturely life is dependent upon God and that John identifies life as being in the Word. But these are not identical sentences, and the distinction between the passages must remain visible.

## 10. John 3:1–8 — born of the Spirit

Jesus tells Nicodemus that a man must be born again / born from above to see the kingdom of God. In John 3:5–8, Jesus speaks of being born of water and of the Spirit, and contrasts that which is born of flesh with that which is born of the Spirit.

John 3:8 uses the Greek word πνεῦμα (*pneuma*), which can mean wind or spirit. Jesus uses the wind's sound and movement as an illustration of the Spirit's work.

### 10.1 Direct findings

- Jesus distinguishes birth of the flesh from birth of the Spirit.
- Being born of the Spirit is necessary in the context of entering or seeing the kingdom of God.
- The wind analogy illustrates that the Spirit's work is not controlled or comprehended by human command.
- Jesus tells Nicodemus that he must not marvel at this teaching.

### 10.2 The wordplay and its limits

The Greek *pneuma* can mean wind or spirit, and John 3:8 uses the ambiguity in the illustration. This is a feature of the Greek text.

It is not proof that Genesis 2:7's Hebrew *neshamah* and Genesis 1:2's Hebrew *ruach* are interchangeable in every context. The language and argument of John 3 must be respected.

### 10.3 Genesis 2:7 and John 3

Genesis 2:7 concerns the formation of man and the beginning of his creaturely life. John 3 concerns the new birth of which Jesus speaks to Nicodemus. Both passages describe life in relation to God's action, but the immediate subject differs.

It would be inaccurate to say that Genesis 2:7 is simply describing the new birth of John 3. The comparison may be theologically fruitful, but it is a comparison between distinct contexts.

## 11. Hebrews 1:1–3 — the Son and the sustaining of all things

Hebrews 1:1–3 says God has spoken by His Son, by whom He made the worlds. The Son is described as the brightness of God's glory and the express image of His person, upholding all things by the word of His power.

### Direct findings

- God speaks by the Son.
- God made the worlds by the Son.
- The Son upholds all things by the word of His power.

This reinforces the relationship between the Son, Creation, and its continuance established in Study 0017.

### Boundary

Hebrews 1:3's phrase “the word of his power” should not be casually equated with every use of “Word” in John 1 or with the breath terms in Genesis and Job. The passages can be compared, but their grammatical constructions and immediate arguments differ.

### Conclusions supported by this comparison

1. Scripture repeatedly attributes creaturely life, creation, and renewal to God's action.
2. Genesis 1:2 names the Spirit of God; Genesis 2:7 uses *neshamah* for the breath of life.
3. Psalm 33:6 places the LORD's word and the breath of His mouth together in the making of the heavens and their host.
4. Job 33:4 places the Spirit of God and the breath of the Almighty together in a statement about creaturely life.
5. Psalm 104:29–30 connects creaturely breath, death, God's spirit, creation, and renewal.
6. John 1 identifies the Word as God, as the One through whom all created things were made, and as the One in whom life is.
7. John 3 distinguishes natural birth from birth of the Spirit.
8. Hebrews 1 identifies the Son as the One through whom God made the worlds and who upholds all things.

### Conclusions not established by this comparison alone

- That *ruach*, *neshamah*, *nephesh*, *Logos*, *zōē*, and *pneuma* are interchangeable terms.
- That the breath breathed into Adam in Genesis 2:7 is explicitly named as the Holy Spirit in that verse.
- That every reference to breath or spirit in Scripture is a reference to the third Person of the Trinity.
- That Genesis 2:7 and John 3 describe the same event or kind of birth.
- That the poetic parallelism in Psalm 33:6 is a technical explanation of the physical mechanism of creation.

## 13. The central question revisited

**What is the biblical relationship between the Spirit of God, the Word, and the breath of life?**

The evidence supports a careful answer:

- The Word is identified in John 1:1 as God, and in John 1:3 as the One through whom all created things were made.
- The Spirit of God is named in Genesis 1:2 as moving upon the waters.
- The breath of life in Genesis 2:7 is the *neshamah* God breathes into the formed man, who thereby becomes a living soul.
- Psalm 33:6 relates the LORD's word and the breath of His mouth in the making of the heavens and their host.
- Job 33:4 relates the Spirit of God and the breath of the Almighty in a statement about creaturely life.
- Psalm 104 relates the breath of creatures, their death, God's spirit, and renewal.
- John 3 describes birth of the Spirit as distinct from birth of the flesh.

The passages establish meaningful relationships among God's creative action, His Word, His Spirit, and the life of creatures. They do not license collapsing every use of “word,” “spirit,” “breath,” and “life” into one undifferentiated term.

A theological synthesis may say that all creaturely life is dependent upon God and that the Word/Son and Spirit are not independent created powers. But any more exact account of the relation among the Persons of the Godhead requires the wider canon and careful doctrinal treatment. This study does not claim to settle that doctrine.

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The biblical text repeatedly distinguishes the Creator from created life and attributes the origin and continuance of creaturely life to God. It also distinguishes words with overlapping semantic ranges rather than allowing similarity to erase their contextual differences.

The Word, Spirit, and breath are related in the canon, but their relationship must be described from the actual passages rather than from an assumed architectural equivalence.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

Genesis records the Spirit of God moving upon the waters, God's command that light be, and God's breathing into the formed man so that man becomes a living soul. Psalms and Job speak of God's word, breath, Spirit, creation, and the continuance of life. John identifies the Word as God and the One through whom all created things were made, and teaches the new birth of the Spirit.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A living creature does not originate its own existence in these passages. The human witness is a creature whose formation and life are attributed to God. A witness can recognize and testify to the Creator's work, but cannot claim the Creator's authorship.

This observation does not settle every question about human agency, consciousness, or the soul. Those require their own investigations.

## 16. Findings

1. Genesis 1:2 uses *ruach Elohim*, rendered “the Spirit of God.”
2. Genesis 1:3 records God speaking and light coming to be.
3. Genesis 2:7 uses *neshamah* for “breath of life,” not *ruach*.
4. Genesis 2:7 says God formed man from the dust, breathed into him, and man became a living soul (*nephesh chayyah*).
5. The expression *nephesh chayyah* is also used for other living creatures; the phrase alone does not settle all questions about the distinctiveness or destiny of human personhood.
6. Psalm 33:6 places the word of the LORD and the breath of His mouth together in the making of the heavens and their host.
7. Job 33:4 places the Spirit of God and the breath of the Almighty in parallel in a statement about creaturely life.
8. Psalm 104:29–30 relates the breath of creatures, death, God's spirit, creation, and renewal.
9. John 1:1–4 identifies the Word as God, the One through whom all things were made, and the One in whom life is.
10. John 3:5–8 distinguishes birth of the flesh from birth of the Spirit.
11. The biblical terms *ruach*, *neshamah*, *nephesh*, *Logos*, *zōē*, and *pneuma* must not be treated as interchangeable merely because their semantic fields can overlap.
12. The canon supports meaningful relationships among the Word, Spirit, divine speech, breath, creation, and life, but the precise theological synthesis requires care.
13. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
