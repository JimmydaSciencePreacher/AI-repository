## 1. Central question

Why does Ezekiel 37 describe bones coming together and bodies receiving sinews, flesh, and skin before breath enters them? What does the passage establish about formation, breath, life, and God's Spirit?

The striking sequence is:

**Bones → sinews → flesh → skin → breath → life**

But a sequence in a prophetic vision must first be interpreted according to the explanation God gives in the passage. We must not turn its imagery into a universal biological formula or a new GUA-W runtime sequence without evidence.

## 2. Scope and method

The primary text is Ezekiel 37:1–14 in the KJV. The investigation examines the vision, the two prophetic commands, the result, and God's explicit interpretation in verses 11–14. It compares the Hebrew term *ruach* with the *neshamah* of Genesis 2:7 and reviews relevant canonical passages.

This study distinguishes:
- what Ezekiel sees;
- what God commands Ezekiel to say;
- what happens in the vision;
- what God says the vision means;
- what may be compared with other Scripture;
- what the passage does not establish.

The repository's Research Method, Sources and Evidence, and Study Format guides govern this record. The KJV is the principal English text.

## 4. The setting — Ezekiel 37:1–3

### 4.1 The hand of the LORD and the Spirit

Ezekiel says:

> “The hand of the LORD was upon me, and carried me out in the spirit of the LORD, and set me down in the midst of the valley which was full of bones,” (Ezekiel 37:1, KJV)

The vision is presented as an experience initiated by the LORD. Ezekiel is carried and set down; he is not the originator of the vision.

The expression “in the spirit of the LORD” is part of the prophet's description of how he is brought into the vision. The passage does not invite us to treat the valley as an ordinary field observation or a laboratory demonstration.

### 4.2 What Ezekiel sees

The valley is full of bones. They are “very many” and “very dry” (v. 2). The description emphasizes extensive death and the absence of life.

God asks:

> “Son of man, can these bones live?” (v. 3)

Ezekiel answers:

> “O Lord GOD, thou knowest.” (v. 3)

Ezekiel does not claim the power or knowledge to make the bones live. He directs the answer to the Lord GOD.

**Observation:** The question establishes the impossibility, from the visible condition of the bones, of treating life as already present. The answer leaves the matter with God.

## 5. First command — prophecy to the bones (verses 4–8)

### 5.1 Speak the word given

God commands Ezekiel:

> “Prophesy upon these bones, and say unto them, O ye dry bones, hear the word of the LORD.” (v. 4)

The prophet is instructed to speak a message he has received. The word belongs to the LORD; Ezekiel is the commissioned speaker.

The message declares that God will cause breath to enter the bones and that they shall live (v. 5). God also says He will lay sinews upon them, bring up flesh, cover them with skin, and put breath in them (v. 6).

The repeated “I will” statements identify God as the acting source of the promised restoration.

### 5.2 Ezekiel obeys

Ezekiel prophesies as commanded (v. 7). He then hears a noise and a shaking, and the bones come together, “bone to his bone.”

The text next describes sinews, flesh, and skin appearing upon them (v. 8). Yet it concludes:

> “but there was no breath in them.” (v. 8)

This is the key observation: the bodies have been assembled and covered, but the vision explicitly says there is no breath in them.

### 5.3 What this first stage establishes

Within the vision:
1. The LORD gives the command and the promised outcome.
2. Ezekiel speaks the message he is given.
3. The bones come together.
4. Sinews, flesh, and skin appear.
5. The bodies remain without breath.

The vision distinguishes the appearance of bodily structure from the arrival of breath and life. It does not say that the bodies are independently alive or that the material formation itself produces life.

## 6. Second command — prophecy to the breath (verses 9–10)

### 6.1 A new prophetic address

God commands Ezekiel:

> “Prophesy unto the wind, prophesy, son of man, and say to the wind, Thus saith the Lord GOD; Come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe upon these slain, that they may live.” (v. 9)

The KJV uses “wind” and “breath” in this verse. The Hebrew word behind both is רוּחַ (*ruach*), whose contextual range includes wind, breath, and spirit.

The phrase “four winds” uses the same Hebrew noun as the address “O breath.” This is a significant feature of the Hebrew wording and the prophetic imagery. The text portrays breath as coming from the four winds at God's command.

### 6.2 Ezekiel obeys; breath enters

Ezekiel prophesies as commanded (v. 10):

> “and the breath came into them, and they lived, and stood up upon their feet, an exceeding great army.”

The vision now records the result: breath enters, they live, and they stand.

The text deliberately distinguishes the breathless bodies of verse 8 from the living multitude of verse 10.

### 6.3 The sequence in the vision

| Order | What the passage records | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bones lie scattered and very dry. | Vision description |
| 2 | The LORD commands Ezekiel to prophesy to the bones. | Divine command |
| 3 | Bones come together. | Vision event |
| 4 | Sinews, flesh, and skin appear. | Vision event |
| 5 | There is no breath in them. | Explicit qualification |
| 6 | The LORD commands Ezekiel to prophesy to the breath/wind. | Divine command |
| 7 | Breath comes into them. | Vision event |
| 8 | They live and stand as a great army. | Vision outcome |

This order is explicit in the vision. Its meaning must now be governed by God's own interpretation.

## 7. God's interpretation — the whole house of Israel (verses 11–14)

This section is decisive. The vision is not left for the reader to decode by imagination.

### 7.1 The bones are identified

God says:

> “Son of man, these bones are the whole house of Israel.” (v. 11)

The people speak of their bones being dried, their hope being lost, and themselves being cut off. The vision's primary referent is therefore the condition and restoration of the whole house of Israel.

This identification prevents us from treating the scene primarily as a general anatomy lesson or a direct description of the creation of Adam.

### 7.2 The graves and restoration

God commands Ezekiel to prophesy that He will open their graves, bring them into the land of Israel, and cause them to know that He is the LORD (vv. 12–13).

The language of graves intensifies the image of national death and restoration. The passage speaks of God's power to restore a people who regard their hope as gone.

### 7.3 “I shall put my spirit in you”

Verse 14 states:

> “And shall put my spirit in you, and ye shall live, and I shall place you in your own land: then shall ye know that I the LORD have spoken it, and performed it, saith the LORD.”

The LORD identifies Himself as the One who puts His spirit in them, gives life, places them in their land, and brings them to know that He has spoken and performed it.

The final interpretation is not merely “bodies need air.” It is a prophetic promise of restoration to Israel, accomplished by the LORD.

The passage's imagery includes breath and life, but its explicit interpretation concerns the restoration of God's people.

## 8. Hebrew observation — *ruach* in Ezekiel 37

The Hebrew noun רוּחַ (*ruach*) appears repeatedly in this passage. Depending on context, it is rendered “spirit,” “wind,” or “breath.”

Important occurrences include:
- Verse 1: “the spirit of the LORD” in the prophet's description of the vision.
- Verse 5: “breath” promised to enter the slain.
- Verse 6: “breath” to be put in them.
- Verse 8: “no breath” in the assembled bodies.
- Verse 9: “wind” and “breath” in the command concerning the four winds.
- Verse 10: “the breath came into them.”
- Verse 14: “my spirit” in the LORD's interpretation.

This repeated use is important. The KJV's different English renderings reflect the context of the same Hebrew term; they do not mean the English words are interchangeable in every setting.

### Compare Genesis 2:7

Genesis 2:7 uses נְשָׁמָה (*neshamah*) in “the breath of life.” Ezekiel 37 uses *ruach* throughout the vision's breath/wind/spirit language.

Therefore:
- Genesis 2:7: *neshamah* — breath of life.
- Ezekiel 37: *ruach* — rendered spirit, wind, or breath according to context.

The two passages can be compared as biblical testimony that life is dependent upon God's action. But we must not rewrite Genesis 2:7 as though it used the same Hebrew noun as Ezekiel 37.

### Does *ruach* always mean the Holy Spirit?

No. Its range includes wind and breath as well as spirit. The context determines the appropriate sense. In Ezekiel 37, the wordplay and the LORD's explanation in verse 14 make the spiritual dimension of the vision unmistakable. Yet each occurrence must still be read in its own clause and context.

## 9. Comparison with other Scripture

### 9.1 Genesis 2:7 — formation and the breath of life

Genesis 2:7 describes the LORD God forming man from the dust, breathing into his nostrils the breath of life, and man becoming a living soul.

Both passages distinguish God's action in relation to bodily formation and life. But the settings differ:
- Genesis 2:7 describes the formation of the first man.
- Ezekiel 37 is a prophetic vision whose meaning God explicitly identifies as the restoration of Israel.

The sequence in Ezekiel may illuminate the distinction between formed bodies and life, but it must not be used to claim that Genesis 2:7 contains every stage shown in Ezekiel's vision.

### 9.2 Job 33:4 — Spirit and breath

Job 33:4 says:

> “The Spirit of God hath made me, and the breath of the Almighty hath given me life.”

This passage, like Ezekiel 37, relates *ruach* and breath to God's life-giving work. It reinforces the biblical theme of creaturely dependence upon God.

It does not turn every use of breath into a technical description of the same event.

### 9.3 Psalm 104:29–30 — breath, death, and renewal

Psalm 104 describes creatures dying when their breath is taken away and being created when God sends forth His spirit.

This is a canonical parallel to Ezekiel's vision: creaturely life is dependent upon God, and renewal is attributed to Him. But Psalm 104 speaks in the context of God's care for His creatures and the renewal of the earth; Ezekiel 37 explicitly interprets its vision as Israel's restoration.

### 9.4 John 3:5–8 — birth of the Spirit

Jesus distinguishes birth of the flesh from birth of the Spirit. John 3:8 uses the Greek word πνεῦμα (*pneuma*) with the image of wind.

This is a meaningful comparison with Ezekiel's *ruach* imagery, especially because Ezekiel 37 uses wind/breath language in a vision of restoration. However, John 3 addresses the new birth and Jesus' teaching to Nicodemus. Ezekiel 37:11–14 identifies the vision with the restoration of Israel.

A canonical connection may be explored; an assertion that both passages describe precisely the same event would exceed what these texts explicitly say.

## 10. The Word, prophecy, and the Witness

Ezekiel 37 repeatedly shows that the prophet does not originate the message or the power of restoration.

The sequence of agency is clear:
- The LORD gives the vision.
- The LORD gives the command.
- Ezekiel receives and speaks the message.
- The LORD brings about the events represented in the vision.
- The LORD explains what the vision means.
- The restored people are to know that He is the LORD.

Ezekiel is a witness and commissioned messenger, not the source of life or restoration.

This is consistent with the governing GUA-W distinction: **Witness ≠ Author.** The prophet's testimony does not make him the originator of what he witnesses.

The passage uses the phrase “word of the LORD” in verse 4. Ezekiel is told to speak that word to the bones. This should be distinguished from making an unsupported claim that every use of “word of the LORD” is a direct lexical occurrence of John's Greek *Logos*. John 1 must be read in its own context.

## 11. The central question revisited

**Why do the bodies receive structure before breath enters them?**

The direct answer is that this is the order God gives Ezekiel in the vision. The bones come together; sinews, flesh, and skin appear; there is still no breath; then Ezekiel prophesies to the breath, which enters them; they live and stand.

**What does this sequence mean?**

God Himself interprets the vision: the bones are the whole house of Israel, and the promised action is the LORD's restoration of His people. The imagery portrays a people whose hope is gone as dead and dry, whom God promises to restore.

**Does the passage establish that structure alone produces life?**

No. It explicitly says the assembled bodies have no breath. Life follows the LORD's commanded arrival of breath.

**Does the passage establish a universal sequence for all biological life?**

No. It is a prophetic vision with an explicit national-restoration interpretation. It can provide a meaningful biblical comparison with Genesis 2:7, but it is not a scientific account of embryology, anatomy, or respiration.
