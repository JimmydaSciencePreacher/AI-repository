## 1. Central question

What is the relationship between the new heart, the new spirit, and God's Spirit in Ezekiel 36:24–28?

The KJV distinguishes these expressions in the passage:

- “a new heart” (v. 26);
- “a new spirit” (v. 26);
- “my spirit” (v. 27).

The Hebrew noun translated “spirit” is *ruach* in both verses 26 and 27. Does that mean the expressions refer to precisely the same thing? What does the grammar and context establish? How do cleansing, removal of the stony heart, the new heart, the new spirit, and God's indwelling Spirit relate?

This study must not collapse four different expressions—heart, new spirit, God's Spirit, and living soul—into one category merely because they are related to life and inward transformation.

## 2. Scope and method

The primary passage is Ezekiel 36:16–38, with special attention to verses 24–28. Ezekiel 37:1–14 is included because it immediately follows and uses *ruach* in the vision of the dry bones. Deuteronomy 30, Jeremiah 31, John 3, and Romans 8 provide canonical comparison.

The study distinguishes:
1. what Ezekiel explicitly says;
2. the Hebrew wording and grammatical forms;
3. the historical and literary context;
4. canonical connections;
5. theological synthesis;
6. questions not settled by these passages.

The KJV is the principal English text. Lexical observations clarify the Hebrew wording but do not replace the passage's context.

## 4. The context — why God promises restoration (Ezekiel 36:16–23)

### 4.1 Israel's conduct and the profaning of God's name

Ezekiel 36:16–21 recounts Israel's uncleanness and the judgment that followed. The LORD says that the house of Israel defiled the land through their way and deeds. Their scattering among the nations is part of the judgment described.

When they came among the nations, God's holy name was profaned because the nations said, “These are the people of the LORD, and are gone forth out of his land” (v. 20).

The passage's concern is not merely Israel's internal condition. God's holy name among the nations is central.

### 4.2 The stated reason for God's action

The LORD says:

> “I do not this for your sakes, O house of Israel, but for mine holy name's sake” (Ezekiel 36:22, KJV).

He declares that He will sanctify His great name, which has been profaned among the nations, and that the nations shall know that He is the LORD when He is sanctified in Israel before their eyes (v. 23).

**Direct finding:** The promised restoration originates in God's own purpose concerning His holy name. The text explicitly denies that the action is grounded in Israel's merit.

This context must govern our reading of the cleansing and inward renewal in verses 24–28.

## 5. The sequence in Ezekiel 36:24–28

### 5.1 Gathering — verse 24

> “For I will take you from among the heathen, and gather you out of all countries, and will bring you into your own land.”

The LORD is the subject of the repeated action. He takes, gathers, and brings.

The restoration has a people, a location, and a covenant context. It is not only a private inward experience.

### 5.2 Cleansing — verse 25

> “Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean: from all your filthiness, and from all your idols, will I cleanse you.”

The LORD promises cleansing from filthiness and idols. The verse uses purification imagery and identifies God as the One who cleanses.

The water language should first be read in the prophetic context of cleansing from impurity and idolatry. Later Christian interpretation may connect this promise with other passages, but no single later sacramental or ritual mechanism should be read into this verse without argument.

### 5.3 A new heart and a new spirit — verse 26

> “A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh.”

The verse contains parallel but distinguishable expressions:
- a new heart;
- a new spirit;
- a stony heart removed;
- a heart of flesh given.

God is the giver and actor. The people do not manufacture their own inward renewal.

The contrast between “stony heart” and “heart of flesh” is an image of changed inward responsiveness. It should not be reduced to a claim about literal cardiac tissue. The context is moral and covenantal: cleansing from idolatry and renewed obedience to the LORD.

### 5.4 God's Spirit — verse 27

> “And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them.”

The LORD now says “my spirit.” The result named is that He will cause the people to walk in His statutes and keep and do His judgments.

This is a critical distinction in the wording:
- Verse 26: “a new spirit” — Hebrew *ruach chadashah*.
- Verse 27: “my spirit” — Hebrew *ruchi* (“my spirit”).

The same Hebrew noun *ruach* occurs in both expressions. The difference is not that two unrelated Hebrew roots are used. The difference lies in the modifiers, grammatical form, and the movement of the passage's argument.

Verse 26 describes the promised new inward condition; verse 27 explicitly identifies the LORD's own Spirit as placed within the people and connects this gift with their enabled covenant obedience.

The text supports distinguishing the expressions in their roles and referents in context. It does not require us to posit two independent spiritual substances or to define the metaphysics of the human spirit from these two verses alone.

### 5.5 Land and covenant — verse 28

> “And ye shall dwell in the land that I gave to your fathers; and ye shall be my people, and I will be your God.”

The passage returns to the land and covenant relationship. The promise includes:
- dwelling in the land given to the fathers;
- being God's people;
- the LORD being their God.

This confirms that Ezekiel 36:24–28 is a corporate restoration promise with inward renewal at its heart. It should not be detached from the surrounding promise concerning Israel, the land, God's name, and covenant identity.

## 6. Hebrew observations — heart and spirit

### 6.1 Heart — לֵב / לֵבָב (*lev / levav*)

The Hebrew words *lev* and *levav* refer to the heart and, in biblical usage, the inner person: thought, understanding, intention, desire, will, and moral orientation. The precise sense depends on context.

In Ezekiel 36:26, “heart” is *lev* (לֵב). The contrast is between the stony heart and the heart of flesh. The image concerns the inward disposition and responsiveness of the people.

It is not a claim that the physical organ is replaced.

### 6.2 New — חָדָשׁ (*chadash*)

The word translated “new” in “a new heart” and “a new spirit” is from the Hebrew *chadash*, meaning new or renewed according to context.

The text promises a changed inward condition. The exact scope of “new” should be interpreted from the covenantal restoration context, not turned into an abstract technical definition.

### 6.3 Spirit — רוּחַ (*ruach*)

The Hebrew noun *ruach* has a contextual range that includes wind, breath, and spirit.

In Ezekiel 36:26 the phrase is *ruach chadashah* — “a new spirit.”
In Ezekiel 36:27 the phrase is *ruchi* — “my spirit.”

The same noun is used, but the expressions are not identical:
- “new” qualifies the spirit in verse 26;
- “my” identifies the Spirit as belonging to the LORD in verse 27.

The context also distinguishes their place in the promise: verse 26 describes the inward renewal given to the people; verse 27 names the LORD's Spirit and the resulting obedience.

### 6.4 Flesh — בָּשָׂר (*basar*)

“Flesh” in “an heart of flesh” is *basar*. In this contrast it is the opposite of stone: living, responsive, and no longer hardened. The phrase is metaphorical in context, not an instruction about bodily tissue.

### 6.5 What lexical evidence does not settle

A lexical definition alone cannot answer every doctrinal question:
- whether “new spirit” in verse 26 is best described as the renewed human spirit, a new disposition, or a phrase with a broader prophetic sense;
- the full relation between the human spirit and the Holy Spirit;
- how this promise is fulfilled across different stages of biblical history.

The passage's grammar and context support a distinction between the expressions, but a complete doctrinal account requires wider canonical study.

## 7. The new heart is connected to obedience, not self-authorship

Ezekiel 36:27 does not stop at the gift of God's Spirit. It states the outcome:

> “and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them.”

The LORD is the acting subject: “I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk.”

This is not a description of people independently creating a new moral standard. The statutes and judgments are God's; the promised inward renewal results in obedience to what He has already commanded.

The passage therefore connects:
- divine cleansing;
- inward renewal;
- God's Spirit within;
- enabled obedience;
- restored covenant life.

The order and relationship are stated by the text. We should not turn them into a claim that every person's experience must unfold in an identical chronological order without examining other passages.

## 8. Canonical comparison

### 8.1 Deuteronomy 30:1–10 — the LORD turns the heart

Deuteronomy 30 describes Israel's return to the LORD after blessing and curse, scattering, and repentance. In verses 6–8, the LORD promises to circumcise the heart of Israel and their seed to love the LORD, and the people will return and obey.

This is a significant antecedent to Ezekiel's promise. The inward transformation is attributed to God's action and is directed toward love of God and obedience.

It supports the connection between a changed heart and a changed direction of life. It does not make “heart” and “Spirit” identical terms.

### 8.2 Jeremiah 31:31–34 — the new covenant and the law within

Jeremiah promises a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah. The LORD says He will put His law in their inward parts and write it in their hearts; He will be their God, and they shall be His people. He also promises knowledge of the LORD and forgiveness of iniquity.

This parallels Ezekiel 36:
- God's initiative;
- covenant restoration;
- inward transformation;
- God's people knowing Him;
- forgiveness and changed relationship.

Jeremiah's passage emphasizes the law written in the heart; Ezekiel emphasizes cleansing, the new heart, the new spirit, and the LORD's Spirit within. These passages are closely related but should not be quoted as if they were the same wording.

### 8.3 Ezekiel 37:1–14 — breath and the Spirit in the restoration vision

Study 0019 examined the dry-bones vision. God explicitly identifies the bones as the whole house of Israel (37:11), and promises to put His Spirit in them (37:14).

Ezekiel 36 and 37 share the restoration theme and the promise of God's Spirit. Ezekiel 36 describes cleansing and inward renewal; Ezekiel 37 dramatizes the restoration of a people portrayed as dead and without hope.

The two chapters illuminate each other. Yet the dry-bones vision must retain its own stated interpretation, and the terms in each passage must be examined in context.

### 8.4 John 3:1–8 — born of the Spirit

Jesus tells Nicodemus that a man must be born again / born from above. He distinguishes that which is born of the flesh from that which is born of the Spirit.

This is a strong canonical connection to Ezekiel's promise of inward renewal by God's Spirit. Jesus' rebuke in John 3:10—“Art thou a master of Israel, and knowest not these things?”—is especially relevant to the question of what a teacher in Israel should have understood.

However, John 3 is a New Testament teaching about the new birth. It should not be reduced to a mere repetition of Ezekiel 36, nor should Ezekiel's national and covenant setting be erased.

### 8.5 Romans 8:1–17 — the Spirit and a new manner of life

Romans 8 contrasts life according to the flesh with life according to the Spirit. It describes the Spirit of God dwelling in believers, the Spirit of Christ, life, mortification of sinful deeds, and adoption.

This passage provides New Testament teaching about the Spirit's indwelling and its effect on the believer's life. It is a substantial canonical comparison with Ezekiel 36:27, where God's Spirit within is connected with walking in His statutes.

The comparison should preserve each passage's own argument. Ezekiel addresses Israel's promised restoration; Romans addresses life in Christ and the Spirit in Paul's argument.

## 10. The central question revisited

**What is the relationship between the new heart, the new spirit, and God's Spirit?**

Ezekiel 36:26–27 presents a connected promise:
1. God gives a new heart.
2. God puts a new spirit within the people.
3. God removes the stony heart and gives a heart of flesh.
4. God puts His Spirit within them.
5. God causes them to walk in His statutes and keep His judgments.

The same Hebrew noun *ruach* appears in “a new spirit” and “my spirit.” The difference in expression and context matters. Verse 26 speaks of the new inward condition given to the people; verse 27 specifically speaks of the LORD's own Spirit and the obedience He brings about.

This supports a distinction between the renewed inward disposition and God's Spirit. It does not, from these verses alone, answer every metaphysical question about the human spirit or provide a complete systematic doctrine of the Spirit's indwelling.

**What is the relationship to the living soul in Genesis 2:7?**

Genesis 2:7 describes the beginning of man's creaturely life. Ezekiel 36 describes promised covenant cleansing and inward renewal. The passages can be compared because both attribute life and renewal to God's action, but they are not describing the same event or using the same Hebrew expressions.
