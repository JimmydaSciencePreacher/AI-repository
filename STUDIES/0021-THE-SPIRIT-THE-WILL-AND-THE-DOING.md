## 1. Central question

What does Scripture teach about God's work in a person in relation to willing and doing?

Philippians 2:13 states:

> “For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure.” (KJV)

The question is not whether human beings will and act; Scripture plainly addresses human willing and acting. The question is how human willing and doing relate to God's prior and active work.

We will examine the immediate context of Philippians 2:12–13, then compare Ezekiel 36:26–27, John 6:35–45, Romans 8:1–17, and Romans 9:14–24.

We must avoid two opposite errors:
- making the human will an independent original cause outside God's action;
- speaking as though the biblical commands to believe, obey, work, and persevere are meaningless or unreal.

## 4. Philippians 2:12–13 — the command and its ground

### 4.1 Read both verses together

Paul writes:

> “Wherefore, my beloved, as ye have always obeyed, not as in my presence only, but now much more in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling.” (Philippians 2:12, KJV)

Then:

> “For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure.” (Philippians 2:13, KJV)

Verse 12 contains a real imperative addressed to the believers: “work out your own salvation.” Verse 13 begins with “For,” giving the reason or ground for the exhortation: God is at work in them, both in willing and in doing.

The verses must not be separated. Verse 12 must not be used to erase verse 13; verse 13 must not be used to erase verse 12.

### 4.2 “Work out” is not “work to originate”

The Greek verb in verse 12 is *katergazomai* (κατεργάζομαι), commonly carrying the sense of working out, bringing about, accomplishing, or producing an outcome. The immediate context does not say “work to purchase” or “author your salvation.” Paul addresses people who have already received the gospel and calls them to obedient conduct.

The distinction between “work out” and “work for” is a useful explanatory distinction, but it should not be treated as a complete doctrine based on an English preposition alone. The argument of verses 12–13 and the wider letter must govern.

### 4.3 “God which worketh in you”

The Greek verb in verse 13 is *energeō* (ἐνεργέω), to work or be at work. God is the grammatical subject. The phrase “in you” addresses the believers, and the plural context of the letter means the church community is in view, not merely an isolated individual's private experience.

Paul attributes both willing and doing to God's work:
- “to will” — *to thelein* (τὸ θέλειν);
- “to do” — *to energein* (τὸ ἐνεργεῖν).

The wording places the willing and the doing within the scope of God's work in the believers. It does not portray God as merely observing a self-originating human decision and then assisting afterward.

### 4.4 “Of his good pleasure”

The final phrase is *hyper tēs eudokias* (ὑπὲρ τῆς εὐδοκίας), rendered “of his good pleasure.” The purpose and pleasure named are God's, not an autonomous human standard that directs God.

The verse therefore grounds the believers' obedience in God's active work and purpose.

### 4.5 The immediate context — Christ's humility

Philippians 2:1–11 calls the church to unity, humility, and regard for others, presenting the mind of Christ and His humiliation and exaltation. Verses 12–13 continue that practical exhortation.

The “doing” in view must be read within this context of humble, obedient, Christ-shaped life. Verse 14 immediately continues: “Do all things without murmurings and disputings.”

**Direct finding:** Paul commands the believers to work out their salvation in lived obedience and grounds this command in God's work within them, which extends to both willing and doing.

## 5. Ezekiel 36:26–27 — the promised inward renewal

Study 0020 examined God's promise:

> “A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you...” (Ezekiel 36:26)

And:

> “And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes...” (Ezekiel 36:27)

The passage identifies God as the giver of the new heart and new spirit, and the One who puts His Spirit within the people and causes them to walk in His statutes.

The immediate setting is Israel's promised restoration for God's holy name. It is not a detached philosophical statement about the mechanics of every human decision.

### 5.1 Relationship to Philippians 2

Both passages attribute inward enabling and resulting obedience to God's action:
- Ezekiel: God puts His Spirit within and causes His people to walk in His statutes.
- Philippians: God works in believers both to will and to do.

This is a strong canonical correspondence. It supports the conclusion that Scripture does not portray obedient willing and action as independent of God's work.

The passages are not identical in setting: Ezekiel speaks prophetically to Israel's restoration; Philippians addresses a New Testament church. The comparison is theological and canonical, not an assertion that both texts describe exactly the same historical event.

## 6. John 6:35–45 — coming to Christ and the Father's drawing

### 6.1 Human coming is real

Jesus says:

> “All that the Father giveth me shall come to me...” (John 6:37, KJV)

The statement includes a real human response: they “shall come.” The verse also identifies the Father's giving as prior in the sentence's stated order.

Jesus continues:

> “and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out.” (v. 37)

The one who comes is truly received by Christ. Divine initiative and human coming are both present in the same statement.

### 6.2 The Father's will and the Son's preservation

John 6:38–40 identifies the Son as having come down from heaven not to do His own will, but the will of the Father who sent Him. The Father's will is that the Son lose none of those given to Him, but raise them up at the last day.

The passage places the believer's coming and final preservation within the Father's will and the Son's mission.

### 6.3 “No man can come” and “draw him”

Jesus says:

> “No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him: and I will raise him up at the last day.” (John 6:44, KJV)

Verse 45 continues with the promise that those who have heard and learned of the Father come to Christ.

The statement is stronger than a claim that God merely makes coming possible while leaving the decisive source wholly independent. Jesus says no one can come unless the Father draws him.

The verse does not, by itself, settle every later theological dispute about the precise mode, scope, or timing of drawing. Those questions require examination of the wider Johannine context and the whole canon.

### 6.4 What this passage establishes

- Coming to Christ is a real response.
- The Father gives people to the Son.
- No one can come unless the Father draws.
- Those who come are received by Christ.
- The Son accomplishes the Father's will and raises His people at the last day.

The passage therefore preserves both the reality of coming and the priority and necessity of the Father's action.

## 7. Romans 8:1–17 — the Spirit, mind, and action

### 7.1 Two orientations

Romans 8 contrasts walking after the flesh with walking after the Spirit (vv. 1–4). Verses 5–8 describe the mind set on the things of the flesh and the mind set on the things of the Spirit.

Paul states that the carnal mind is enmity against God and is not subject to the law of God; indeed, it cannot be (v. 7).

This is not a neutral picture of an autonomous will standing outside all moral orientation. The mind is described in relation to flesh or Spirit.

### 7.2 The Spirit dwells in believers

Verses 9–11 speak of the Spirit of God dwelling in believers, and of the Spirit of Christ. Paul distinguishes those who have the Spirit of Christ from those who do not belong to Him.

Verse 11 connects the indwelling Spirit with the future quickening of mortal bodies.

### 7.3 The Spirit and putting to death the deeds of the body

Paul writes:

> “For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live.” (Romans 8:13, KJV)

Verse 14 continues:

> “For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God.”

The believer acts—mortifying the deeds of the body—but does so “through the Spirit.” The Spirit's leading and the believer's action are not presented as competing causes in the text.

### 7.4 What Romans 8 adds

Romans 8 contributes a fuller account of life in the Spirit:
- the Spirit dwells in believers;
- the mind's orientation is contrasted;
- believers put to death the deeds of the body through the Spirit;
- those led by the Spirit are sons of God;
- the Spirit bears witness with their spirit (v. 16).

This is a canonical connection to Ezekiel 36:27 and Philippians 2:13. It does not mean every use of “spirit” in these passages has exactly the same grammatical referent or function.

## 8. Romans 9:14–24 — mercy, the potter, and the creature

### 8.1 The question Paul addresses

Romans 9:14 raises the question: “Is there unrighteousness with God?” Paul answers, “God forbid.”

He then cites God's declaration to Moses:

> “I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion.” (v. 15)

Verse 16 states:

> “So then it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy.”

This passage explicitly denies that God's mercy originates in human willing or exertion.

### 8.2 The potter and the clay

Romans 9:20–21 challenges the creature's presumption to answer back to God and uses the potter/clay image. The image emphasizes the Creator's authority and the creature's dependence.

Verses 22–24 discuss vessels of wrath and vessels of mercy, including those called by God, “not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles.”

The passage must be read in Paul's argument about God's promise, Israel, the Gentiles, mercy, and calling. It is not responsible to reduce the entire chapter to a slogan about individual psychology while ignoring Romans 9:1–13 and 9:25–33.

### 8.3 Relationship to willing and doing

Romans 9:16 strongly supports the priority of divine mercy over human willing and running as the originating ground of salvation. Philippians 2:13 addresses God's work in believers' willing and doing. John 6 addresses the Father's giving and drawing and the person's coming to Christ.

Together these passages give substantial canonical support for God's initiative and sustaining action. They do not make human response unreal; they place it within God's mercy, call, and work.

## 10. The central question revisited

**Does God change the heart, give His Spirit, and then produce willingness and doing?**

The examined passages support a strong connection:
- Ezekiel 36 promises a new heart, a new spirit, and the LORD's Spirit within, with the LORD causing obedience.
- Philippians 2:12–13 commands believers to work out their salvation and grounds their willing and doing in God's work within them.
- John 6:37–45 describes people coming to Christ while identifying the Father's giving and drawing as necessary and prior.
- Romans 8 describes Spirit-indwelt believers acting through the Spirit.
- Romans 9:16 denies that God's mercy originates in human willing or running.

A careful synthesis is therefore:

**God's initiative and work are foundational; human willing and doing are real responses that Scripture places within God's action, mercy, and enabling.**

This formulation is a canonical synthesis, not a direct quotation from any single verse. The passages do not all address the same question in the same way, and the synthesis must preserve their distinct contexts.

## 13. Findings

1. Philippians 2:12 gives believers a real command to work out their salvation with fear and trembling.
2. Philippians 2:13 grounds that command in God's work within believers, explicitly including both willing and doing.
3. The immediate context concerns Christlike humility, unity, obedience, and the conduct of the church.
4. Ezekiel 36:26–27 attributes the new heart, new spirit, indwelling of God's Spirit, and resulting obedience to God's action.
5. John 6:37–45 describes real coming to Christ while identifying the Father's giving and drawing as necessary.
6. Romans 8:1–17 describes the Spirit's indwelling and believers' action through the Spirit.
7. Romans 9:16 explicitly denies that God's mercy originates in human willing or running.
8. These passages preserve both divine initiative and real human response, without presenting them as equal original causes.
9. The statement that God's work is foundational and human willing and doing are real responses is a canonical synthesis, not a verbatim statement from one verse.
10. The passages have distinct contexts and should not be flattened into a single undifferentiated formula.
11. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
