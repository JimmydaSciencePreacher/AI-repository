## 1. Central question

What is the order and relationship in Ephesians 2:1–10 between man's condition in trespasses and sins, God's making alive, salvation by grace through faith, and the believer's walk in good works?

The passage moves from “dead in trespasses and sins” (v. 1) to “hath quickened us together with Christ” (v. 5), then to being raised and seated with Christ (v. 6), salvation by grace through faith (vv. 8–9), and being created in Christ Jesus unto good works (v. 10).

The central question is whether the text presents God's action as foundational to the subsequent walk, and how this relates to Genesis 2:7 without confusing creation, new creation, and spiritual renewal.

## 4. The passage in its immediate context

Ephesians 1:15–23 has just described God's power toward those who believe, the raising of Christ from the dead, and Christ's exaltation above all principality and power. Ephesians 2:1–10 then describes the believers' former condition and God's action in bringing them into life with Christ.

The passage is therefore connected to the preceding account of God's power in Christ. It is not an isolated philosophical statement about human ability.

Ephesians 2:11–22 continues by addressing Gentiles who were formerly alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers from the covenants of promise, and who are brought near by the blood of Christ. The wider chapter includes both salvation and the formation of one reconciled people in Christ.

## 5. Ephesians 2:1–3 — the former condition

### 5.1 “Dead in trespasses and sins” — verse 1

> “And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins;”

The KJV includes “hath he quickened” in verse 1. In many modern critical Greek editions, the verb “made alive” is supplied from verse 5; the Greek sentence begins by describing the readers as dead in trespasses and sins and continues into the explanation of God's action. The KJV's rendering makes explicit the verb understood from the later clause.

The condition is described as death “in trespasses and sins.” The text is not describing biological death: the people were physically alive and walking (vv. 2–3). “Dead” describes their condition in relation to trespasses and sins and their alienation from the life God gives.

This distinction matters. We must not turn a spiritual description into a claim that the people had no physical, mental, or volitional activity. Verses 2–3 explicitly describe their former walk and conduct.

### 5.2 A former walk — verse 2

> “Wherein in time past ye walked according to the course of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that now worketh in the children of disobedience:”

Paul describes a former manner of life:
- walking according to the course of this world;
- according to the prince of the power of the air;
- among the children of disobedience.

The language is striking: those described as dead are also described as walking. Therefore “dead” cannot mean inactive in every ordinary sense. It describes a spiritual and moral condition, while “walked” describes actual conduct.

### 5.3 Desires and conduct — verse 3

> “Among whom also we all had our conversation in times past in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind; and were by nature the children of wrath, even as others.”

Paul includes himself and his readers in the former condition: “we all.” Their conduct involved desires of the flesh and of the mind.

This passage does not portray fallen humanity as lacking desires, thoughts, choices, or actions. It describes those activities as taking place within a condition characterized by trespasses, sins, disobedience, and wrath.

**Direct finding:** Spiritual death in this passage does not mean the absence of all human activity. The people were active, but their former walk was characterized by the conditions Paul names.

## 6. Ephesians 2:4–7 — “But God”

### 6.1 The decisive transition — verse 4

> “But God, who is rich in mercy, for his great love wherewith he loved us,”

The transition is explicit: “But God.”

The text moves from the former condition to God's action. The stated grounds are God's mercy and great love. Paul does not say that the people first made themselves alive and thereby caused God to become merciful.

### 6.2 Quickened together with Christ — verse 5

> “Even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ, (by grace ye are saved;)”

The timing phrase is important: “even when we were dead in sins.”

God's making alive is described as occurring when the people were in the condition just described. The verse attributes the action to God and joins it to Christ: “quickened us together with Christ.”

The Greek verb is *synezōopoiēsen* (συνεζωοποίησεν), a form meaning made alive together with. It is a compound verb joining the idea of being made alive with the idea of togetherness. The phrase “with Christ” is essential; this is not an independent human self-revival.

The parenthetical phrase “by grace ye are saved” identifies salvation as gracious.

### 6.3 Raised and seated together — verse 6

> “And hath raised us up together, and made us sit together in heavenly places in Christ Jesus:”

Paul continues the sequence:
- made alive together with Christ;
- raised up together;
- made to sit together in heavenly places in Christ Jesus.

The repeated “together” language emphasizes union with Christ. The passage describes a status and participation grounded in Christ, not a self-produced ascent by the believers.

The statements are expressed as accomplished divine action. Their full relation to present experience and future consummation should be studied in the wider Pauline context; the wording should not be weakened or forced into a simplistic timeline.

### 6.4 The purpose of the display — verse 7

> “That in the ages to come he might shew the exceeding riches of his grace in his kindness toward us through Christ Jesus.”

God's action has a stated purpose: the display of the exceeding riches of His grace in His kindness toward believers through Christ Jesus.

The emphasis remains on God's grace and kindness. The saved people are recipients through whom the riches of grace are displayed; they are not the source of that grace.

## 7. Ephesians 2:8–9 — grace, faith, and works

### 7.1 Salvation by grace through faith

> “For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God:”

The statement identifies:
- grace as the basis or character of salvation;
- faith as the means expressed by “through”;
- salvation as “not of yourselves”;
- the gift as “of God.”

The passage denies that salvation originates in the people themselves.

### 7.2 “And that not of yourselves”

The Greek demonstrative translated “that” is *touto* (τοῦτο), neuter singular. The nouns “grace” (*charis*) and “faith” (*pistis*) are feminine. This grammatical observation is sometimes used to argue that “that” cannot refer to faith alone. However, grammatical gender agreement does not mechanically settle every question about a demonstrative's antecedent, especially when it summarizes a preceding idea or clause.

A careful conclusion is that the whole salvation-by-grace-through-faith statement is described as not originating from the readers and as God's gift. This verse should not be made to prove, by gender agreement alone, a detailed account of exactly how the gift of faith is given. That question requires wider textual examination.

### 7.3 “Not of works” — verse 9

> “Not of works, lest any man should boast.”

The text explicitly excludes works as the ground on which anyone may boast of salvation. Human achievement is not the source of the salvation described.

This does not mean works are absent from the believer's life. Verse 10 immediately addresses good works, but gives them a different place in the argument.

## 8. Ephesians 2:10 — God's workmanship and prepared works

### 8.1 “For we are his workmanship”

> “For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them.”

The Greek noun translated “workmanship” is *poiēma* (ποίημα). It refers to something made or produced, a work or workmanship. The word itself does not mean a complete technical blueprint, nor does its etymology establish a full architecture. Its meaning here is governed by the sentence: “we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus.”

The possessive “his” is significant in the English rendering: the workmanship belongs to God. The verb “created” (*ktisthentes*, κτισθέντες) is a passive participle: the people are described as having been created. They are not the creators of this new identity in Christ.

### 8.2 “Created in Christ Jesus”

The phrase identifies the sphere and union in which this new creation occurs: “in Christ Jesus.” The verse does not say that people independently create themselves into Christ.

The creation language is a strong statement of divine initiative. It must be read with the preceding statements that God made them alive, raised them, and seated them with Christ.

### 8.3 “Unto good works”

The purpose or result is “unto good works.” Good works are not the source or price of salvation in this passage. They are the intended outcome of God's workmanship in Christ.

Verse 10 therefore prevents a reading of verses 8–9 in which grace means that conduct and obedience no longer matter. Paul rejects works as the ground for boasting in salvation, then states that those saved are God's workmanship, created in Christ for good works.

### 8.4 “Which God hath before ordained”

The Greek expression is *hois proētoimasen ho theos* (οἷς προητοίμασεν ὁ θεός): “which God prepared beforehand.”

The verb *proētoimasen* (προητοίμασεν) means prepared beforehand. God is the grammatical subject. The works are described as prepared by God beforehand.

The verse does not specify every detail of the preparation, nor does it provide a schedule or list of each good work. It does establish that the good works are not an autonomous human invention that gives salvation its origin. They are within God's prior preparation and purpose.

### 8.5 “That we should walk in them”

The final phrase returns to the language of walking. Compare:
- verse 2: they formerly “walked” according to the course of this world;
- verse 10: they are to “walk” in the good works God prepared beforehand.

The same broad image of a manner of life is used with a changed direction and purpose. The passage moves from a former walk in trespasses and sins to a walk in good works prepared by God.

**Direct finding:** Ephesians 2:10 presents good works as the intended walk of those who are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus. The works are prepared beforehand by God; the believers are to walk in them.

## 9. The sequence in Ephesians 2:1–10

The passage's argument can be summarized without turning it into a mechanical formula:

1. **Former condition:** dead in trespasses and sins (vv. 1, 5).
2. **Former walk:** according to the world, disobedience, and the desires of flesh and mind (vv. 2–3).
3. **Divine initiative:** “But God,” rich in mercy and great love (v. 4).
4. **Quickening:** God makes alive together with Christ (v. 5).
5. **Union with Christ:** raised and seated together with Him (v. 6).
6. **Purpose:** the display of God's exceeding grace (v. 7).
7. **Salvation:** by grace through faith, not from the recipients or their works (vv. 8–9).
8. **New creation/workmanship:** created in Christ Jesus (v. 10).
9. **Prepared purpose and walk:** good works prepared beforehand by God, in which believers are to walk (v. 10).

This is the passage's rhetorical and theological movement. It should not be made into an exhaustive chronology of every aspect of salvation without examining other passages.

## 10. Comparison with Genesis 2:7

Genesis 2:7 (KJV):

> “And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.”

Genesis 2:7 describes the creation of the first man:
- the LORD God formed man from the dust;
- God breathed into his nostrils the breath of life;
- man became a living soul.

Ephesians 2:1–10 describes people dead in trespasses and sins being made alive together with Christ, raised with Him, and created in Christ Jesus unto good works.

### 10.1 A valid point of comparison

Both passages make God's action foundational:
- Genesis 2:7 attributes man's formation and life to the LORD God.
- Ephesians 2 attributes quickening and new creation in Christ to God.

Both resist the idea that the creature is the original source of its own life.

### 10.2 The necessary boundary

The passages do not describe the same event:
- Genesis 2:7 concerns the creation and animation of the first man.
- Ephesians 2 concerns salvation and new life in Christ for people formerly dead in trespasses and sins.

Genesis 2:7 uses *neshamah* (“breath”) and *nephesh chayyah* (“living soul/being”). Ephesians 2 uses Greek terms for being made alive together with Christ and being created in Christ Jesus. It does not use Genesis 2:7's Hebrew terms.

Therefore, “quickening” in Ephesians should not be equated lexically with God's breathing into Adam. A theological analogy may be considered, but it must remain an analogy and not be mistaken for the direct meaning of either text.

### 10.3 Does Ephesians 2 establish a second Genesis 2:7?

No. Ephesians 2 does not say that the believer undergoes a second physical formation from dust or receives the same breath described in Genesis 2:7. It speaks of being made alive with Christ and created in Christ Jesus.

The wider canon may develop creation and new-creation themes, but any such connection must be established from the relevant passages rather than assumed from similar English ideas of life.

## 11. Canonical comparisons

### 11.1 Ezekiel 36:24–28 — cleansing and God's Spirit

Ezekiel 36 promises cleansing, a new heart, a new spirit, and the LORD's Spirit within, with the LORD causing His people to walk in His statutes.

The correspondence with Ephesians 2 is significant:
- divine initiative;
- inward renewal;
- a changed walk;
- God's action as the ground of obedience.

The contexts differ. Ezekiel addresses Israel's promised restoration for God's holy name; Ephesians addresses salvation in Christ and the formation of a reconciled people. The texts should illuminate rather than erase each other's setting.

### 11.2 John 5:24–29 — passing from death unto life

Jesus speaks of the one who hears His word and believes the Father having everlasting life, not coming into condemnation, and having passed from death unto life (v. 24). He then speaks of the hour when the dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God, and they that hear shall live (v. 25), as well as the future resurrection of those in the graves (vv. 28–29).

This passage gives a canonical connection between death and life through the Son's word and authority. It also contains both present spiritual life and future bodily resurrection. Those dimensions must not be collapsed.

### 11.3 Romans 6:1–14 — raised to walk in newness of life

Romans 6 connects union with Christ's death and resurrection to walking in newness of life (v. 4). Paul commands believers not to let sin reign in their mortal body and to yield themselves unto God (vv. 12–13).

This provides a close comparison to Ephesians 2: the new life in Christ has an ethical expression. The believer's walk changes because of what God has done in Christ.

### 11.4 Philippians 2:12–13 — God's work and the believer's walk

Study 0021 examined Paul's command to work out salvation and his statement that God works in believers both to will and to do.

Ephesians 2 complements this:
- God makes alive and creates in Christ;
- God prepares the good works beforehand;
- believers are to walk in those works.

The two passages together preserve divine initiative and actual human obedience.

### 11.5 Titus 3:3–7 — saved according to mercy

Titus 3 describes the former life of foolishness and disobedience, then says that the kindness and love of God appeared, and that salvation is “not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy” (vv. 4–5). It speaks of the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Ghost, justification by grace, and hope of eternal life.

This is a strong parallel to Ephesians 2: former conduct, God's kindness and mercy, salvation not grounded in human works, and renewal by the Holy Ghost. The exact meanings of “washing” and “regeneration” require their own contextual study; they should not be made identical to every image in Ephesians 2 without examination.

## 12. The central question revisited

**Does God's work of making alive precede the believer's walk in the works God has prepared?**

Ephesians 2 presents the following argument:
- the people were dead in trespasses and sins;
- God, rich in mercy and great love, made them alive together with Christ;
- salvation is by grace through faith, not from themselves or their works;
- they are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus;
- the good works are prepared beforehand by God;
- the believers are to walk in those works.

The passage therefore places God's action and preparation as foundational to the believer's new walk. The walk is real and commanded, but it is not presented as the source of the life or salvation that makes it possible.

A concise synthesis:

**God makes alive in Christ; God creates in Christ unto good works; God prepares the works; the believer walks in them.**

This is a summary of the passage's sequence, not a new formal law and not a complete account of every question about the order of salvation.

## 15. Findings

1. Ephesians 2:1–3 describes the readers' former condition as dead in trespasses and sins while also describing their active former walk and conduct.
2. “Dead” in this context is a spiritual and moral description, not a claim of biological inactivity.
3. “But God” in verse 4 marks the transition to God's action, grounded in His mercy and great love.
4. God makes believers alive together with Christ while they are described as dead in sins (v. 5).
5. Paul describes believers as raised and seated together with Christ (v. 6).
6. The purpose includes the display of God's exceeding grace and kindness (v. 7).
7. Salvation is by grace through faith, not from the recipients or their works, and excludes boasting (vv. 8–9).
8. Ephesians 2:8's demonstrative “that” should not be used to settle the gift-of-faith question by grammatical gender alone.
9. Believers are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works (v. 10).
10. God prepared the good works beforehand; believers are to walk in them.
11. The passage's movement from former walk (v. 2) to intended walk (v. 10) is significant.
12. Genesis 2:7 and Ephesians 2 may be compared in their attribution of life to God's action, but they concern distinct events and use different languages and contexts.
13. Ephesians 2:10 does not make *poiēma* a technical term for GUA-W architecture.
14. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
