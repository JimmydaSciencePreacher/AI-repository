## 1. Question

How should the description “dead in trespasses and sins” in Ephesians 2:1–5 be understood in relation to the Bible's other uses of death and life?

This investigation asks:

- Does Scripture use death only for the end of biological life?
- What does Genesis 2:17–3:24 contribute to the biblical relationship between sin and death?
- How does Romans 5 describe death entering and reigning through Adam?
- How does Ephesians 2 itself explain the condition it calls “dead”?
- How do John 5 and Romans 6 distinguish present life in Christ from bodily resurrection and from being “dead to sin”?
- Which other passages use death figuratively, morally, relationally, or eschatologically?
- What can be concluded without forcing every use of “death” into one identical meaning?

## 5. Source observations

### 5.1 Genesis: the warning of death and the later narrative

Genesis 2:16–17 records God's command concerning the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and the warning:

> “for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.” (Genesis 2:17, KJV)

Genesis 3 records the serpent's contradiction of that warning, the woman's and man's disobedience, God's judgment, the expulsion from the Garden, and the prevention of access to the tree of life (Genesis 3:22–24). Genesis 5:5 later records Adam's death at the age of nine hundred and thirty years.

**What the text explicitly records:** the warning precedes the transgression; death is announced as its consequence; the transgression is followed by judgment and expulsion; Adam's bodily death is subsequently recorded.

**What the text does not explicitly narrate:** Genesis 3 does not say that Adam's body ceased functioning on the calendar day he ate. Nor does Genesis 2:17 itself provide a technical explanation separating spiritual death from physical death.

The relationship between the day of disobedience, the announced death, the immediate judgment, the loss of access to the tree of life, and eventual bodily death requires interpretation. The narrative supplies the events; the exact theological account of how these dimensions relate must be argued from the wider canon.

### 5.2 Romans: death through Adam and life through Christ

Romans 5:12–21 places sin and death in a comparison between Adam and Christ. Paul writes that by one man sin entered the world, and death by sin; death passed upon all men (v. 12). He describes death reigning from Adam to Moses (v. 14), contrasts the offence of one with the gift through Jesus Christ (vv. 15–17), and contrasts condemnation with justification and life (vv. 18–21).

Death in this argument is not limited to the moment of an individual's bodily death. Paul describes death as having entered and reigned. He sets this reign against the reign of grace through righteousness unto eternal life by Jesus Christ (v. 21).

Romans 5 therefore provides a broad Adam–Christ framework for understanding sin, condemnation, death, grace, righteousness, and life. The precise interpretation of “for that all have sinned” in verse 12 and the relation of Adam's act to the condition of all humanity have been debated. This study does not settle those debates by isolating one phrase.

### 5.3 Ephesians: the condition called “dead”

Ephesians 2:1–3 describes the readers as dead in trespasses and sins, in which they formerly walked according to the course of this world, the ruler of the power of the air, and the desires of the flesh and mind. The paragraph includes actual conduct and desire. It does not portray the people as biologically dead or as inactive in every human capacity.

Ephesians 2:4 begins, “But God,” and verses 5–6 describe God making them alive together with Christ, raising them together, and seating them together in heavenly places in Christ Jesus. Verses 8–10 continue with salvation by grace through faith and the creation of God's workmanship in Christ Jesus unto good works.

The contrast within the passage is therefore not between biological inactivity and biological activity. It is between the former condition and walk characterized by trespasses, sins, disobedience, and wrath, and God's action in making alive with Christ, followed by the new walk in good works.

The KJV supplies “hath he quickened” in verse 1 in English; the Greek sentence begins with “and you, being dead...” and the finite “made alive together” verb appears in verse 5. The NET notes that the syntax of verses 1–3 leaves the participial opening unresolved until the “But God” transition in verse 4. This grammatical feature reinforces the literary movement; it does not by itself settle every theological question about spiritual death.

### 5.4 Ephesians 4: alienation from the life of God

Ephesians 4:17–19 describes the Gentiles' former walk as involving vanity of mind, darkened understanding, alienation from the life of God through ignorance and hardness of heart, and a resulting course of conduct.

This passage gives an important internal explanation of the kind of condition described in Ephesians 2. “Alienated from the life of God” is explicit language in Ephesians 4:18. It supports describing the condition in Ephesians 2 as spiritual and relational alienation, while the surrounding verses also connect it with understanding, heart, conduct, and moral response.

This is a contextual and canonical inference from the same letter. It is not a reason to replace Ephesians 2's own wording with a single abstract definition.

### 5.5 Colossians: a close parallel to Ephesians 2

Colossians 2:11–15 describes believers as buried with Christ in baptism and raised with Him through faith in the operation of God, who raised Christ from the dead. Verse 13 states that when they were dead in sins and the uncircumcision of their flesh, God quickened them together with Christ, having forgiven their trespasses.

The parallel with Ephesians 2:1, 5 is direct in subject and wording: being dead in sins, God's action in making alive together with Christ, and forgiveness. Colossians also places this within Christ's death and resurrection and the cancellation of the record of ordinances against the people.

This passage strengthens the conclusion that “dead in sins” in Ephesians 2 concerns the former condition from which God delivers through Christ. It does not turn the phrase into a statement of biological death.

### 5.6 John 5: present passage from death to life and future resurrection

John 5:24 records Jesus saying that the one who hears His word and believes the Father who sent Him has everlasting life and “is passed from death unto life.” Verse 25 speaks of an hour that “now is” when the dead hear the voice of the Son of God and live.

But verses 28–29 speak of a coming hour when all that are in the graves shall hear His voice and come forth, some to the resurrection of life and others to the resurrection of damnation.

The passage places present life and future bodily resurrection in the same discourse. They are related, but the verses are not interchangeable:
- verses 24–25 speak of a present passage from death to life and the present hour;
- verses 28–29 explicitly speak of those in graves coming forth in resurrection.

The text therefore warns against reducing every reference to death and life either to present spiritual experience or exclusively to future bodily resurrection.

### 5.7 Romans 6: “dead to sin” is not the same wording as “dead in sins”

Romans 6:1–14 asks whether believers should continue in sin so that grace may abound. Paul answers that those who have died to sin should not continue living in it. He connects this death with baptism into Christ's death and with walking in newness of life (vv. 3–4). He then commands believers to reckon themselves dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God through Jesus Christ (v. 11), and not to let sin reign in their mortal bodies (vv. 12–14).

A distinction of wording and argument matters:

- **Ephesians 2:** “dead in trespasses and sins” describes the former condition from which God makes people alive with Christ.
- **Romans 6:** “dead to sin” describes the believer's relation to sin in connection with Christ's death and resurrection, and grounds the call not to let sin reign.

The two expressions belong to the same broad biblical death-and-life theme, but they are not identical propositions. Romans 6 itself continues to address the believer's mortal body, desires, and conduct. Therefore “dead to sin” cannot mean that the believer has become physically inactive or that temptation and the need for obedience no longer exist.

### 5.8 Other figurative uses: Luke 15 and 1 Timothy 5

In Luke 15:24 and 15:32, the father says that his son was dead and is alive again, was lost and is found. The son had not undergone bodily death and resurrection in the parable. The language describes his lost condition and restored relationship within the story.

In 1 Timothy 5:6, Paul says that a woman who lives in pleasure “is dead while she liveth.” The wording itself shows that “dead” can describe a grave moral or spiritual condition while the person remains physically alive.

These passages establish that biblical language of death can be used figuratively. They do not mean that every figurative use has exactly the same meaning. Context identifies what kind of loss, alienation, or condition is being described.

### 5.9 Revelation: the second death

Revelation 20:11–15 describes the final judgment and the lake of fire, identifying this as the second death. Revelation 21:8 again names the second death in connection with the lake of fire.

This is an eschatological use of death associated with final judgment. It must not be collapsed into the present condition described in Ephesians 2:1, nor into ordinary bodily death. The wider biblical account relates these themes, but the passages distinguish their contexts and horizons.

## 6. Working distinctions

The following are descriptive categories for comparing passages. They are not a claim that Scripture provides a single technical taxonomy with these exact labels.

### Bodily or physical death

The cessation of a person's bodily life, as narrated in passages such as Genesis 5:5 and addressed in John 5:28–29 and 1 Corinthians 15. This is not the sense in which the readers of Ephesians 2:1 were dead while they walked and acted.

### Death in trespasses and sins

A condition described in Ephesians 2:1, 5 and Colossians 2:13. The context associates it with trespasses, sins, disobedience, alienation, and a former walk, from which God makes people alive together with Christ.

“Spiritual death” is a common theological shorthand for this condition. It is a summary label, not a phrase that should be allowed to replace the detailed descriptions in the passages.

### Death as dominion or reign

Romans 5:12–21 personifies death as entering and reigning through Adam's transgression, in contrast with grace reigning through righteousness unto eternal life in Jesus Christ. This language describes more than an individual's moment of bodily death.

### Death to sin

Romans 6:1–14 uses this expression for the believer's relation to sin through union with Christ's death and resurrection. It grounds a changed walk and the command not to let sin reign. It is not the same phrase or argument as being “dead in sins.”

### Figurative or moral death

Luke 15:24, 32 and 1 Timothy 5:6 use death language for a condition that is not bodily death. Each passage supplies its own narrative or moral context.

### The second death

Revelation 20:14 and 21:8 use this term for final judgment. It is distinct from bodily death, the former condition in Ephesians 2, and the believer's death to sin in Romans 6.

## 7. Analysis: what does “dead in trespasses” establish?

### 7.1 It is not biological death

Ephesians 2:2–3 describes the people as walking, desiring, thinking, and conducting themselves. The word “dead” cannot mean that they were physically lifeless or incapable of activity.

### 7.2 It is more than a neutral metaphor for poor conduct

The passage does not merely say that the people had some bad habits. It describes their condition as death in trespasses and sins, their former allegiance and walk, and their status as children of wrath. The “But God” transition attributes the decisive action of making alive to God.

### 7.3 The same letter clarifies the relational dimension

Ephesians 4:18 describes alienation from the life of God. Read with Ephesians 2:1–5, this supports a description of spiritual death as a condition of alienation from God's life, bound up with ignorance, hardness of heart, disobedience, and sin.

This is a synthesis across passages in Ephesians, not a dictionary definition that exhausts the term.

### 7.4 The Adam–Christ comparison supplies a larger canonical frame

Romans 5 connects sin and death through Adam and contrasts death's reign with the gift of righteousness and life through Jesus Christ. Ephesians 2 identifies the readers' condition in sins and God's making alive with Christ. These passages can be read together within Paul's Adam–Christ and Christ-union themes.

The comparison must not erase the distinct arguments: Romans 5 develops the Adam–Christ contrast and the reign of sin, death, grace, and life; Ephesians 2 describes the readers' former condition, God's mercy, union with Christ, and the resulting walk.

### 7.5 Present life does not eliminate future resurrection

John 5:24–25 and 5:28–29 distinguish present passage from death to life from the future resurrection of those in the graves. A complete biblical account must preserve both. “Already alive in Christ” does not mean bodily resurrection has already occurred in its final, visible form.

### 7.6 Similar vocabulary does not mean identical theological function

“Dead in sins,” “dead to sin,” “dead while she liveth,” “dead and alive again,” bodily death, and “the second death” use related language in different contexts. Their relationship is meaningful, but the interpreter must not flatten them into a single definition.

## 10. Findings

1. Scripture uses death and life language in more than one context; the sense must be established from each passage.
2. Ephesians 2:1–3 describes people as dead in trespasses and sins while also describing their active former walk, desires, and conduct.
3. “Dead” in Ephesians 2 is not a claim of biological inactivity.
4. Ephesians 4:18 describes alienation from the life of God and supports a relational dimension in understanding Ephesians 2, while remaining a contextual inference.
5. Colossians 2:13 provides a close parallel: people dead in sins are made alive together with Christ by God's action.
6. Romans 5:12–21 describes sin and death entering and death reigning through Adam, in contrast with grace and life through Jesus Christ.
7. Romans 6:1–14 uses “dead to sin” in a different argument concerning union with Christ and the believer's changed relation to sin and conduct.
8. John 5:24–25 describes present passage from death to life, while John 5:28–29 describes future resurrection from the graves.
9. Luke 15:24, 32 and 1 Timothy 5:6 show figurative uses of death language for conditions other than bodily death.
10. Revelation 20:14 and 21:8 identify the second death in the context of final judgment; it must not be collapsed into the other uses.
11. “Spiritual death” can be used as a qualified theological shorthand for Ephesians 2's condition, but it must not replace the detailed biblical descriptions.
12. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
