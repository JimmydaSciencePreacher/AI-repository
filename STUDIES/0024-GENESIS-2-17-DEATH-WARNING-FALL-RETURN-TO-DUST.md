## 1. Question

What did God mean by the warning in Genesis 2:17—

> “for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.”

—given that Adam continued living bodily after eating, was expelled from the Garden, lost access to the tree of life, and eventually died?

The study examines:

1. The Hebrew expressions translated “in the day” and “thou shalt surely die.”
2. The events that Genesis 3 records immediately after the eating.
3. The judgment concerning the ground and the return to dust.
4. The tree of life and the guarded way.
5. Adam's later recorded death in Genesis 5:5.
6. Paul's interpretation of Adam's transgression and death in Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15.
7. The principal scholarly interpretations and the limits of what can be concluded.

## 2. Scope and boundaries

The primary text is Genesis 2:16–17; 3:1–24; and 5:1–5, read as a continuous narrative. Canonical comparisons include Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49. The study also consults prior repository work on the Garden, the tree of life, and the guarded way.

This study does not attempt to settle:
- the complete doctrine of original sin;
- every question about the pre-Fall physical condition of Adam and Eve;

- the full relationship between Genesis and evolutionary biology.

It does not assume that Adam was inherently immortal, inherently subject to inevitable death, or already eating from the tree of life before the transgression. Those claims require evidence and must not be silently supplied by the interpreter.

### Hebrew expressions

The principal Hebrew expressions are:

- **בְּיוֹם (*beyom*)** — literally, “in the day”; its contextual use can carry a more general temporal or conditional force.
- **מוֹת תָּמוּת (*mot tamut*)** — literally, “dying you will die”; conventionally translated “you shall surely die.”

The infinitive absolute followed by a finite verb from the same root emphasizes the certainty or force of the statement. The construction does not, by itself, specify whether the death is immediate bodily execution, an inevitable outcome, or a death condition described in another way. The temporal expression must also be interpreted in context.

## 5. Source observations: Genesis 2:16–17

### 5.1 The command and its setting

Genesis 2:16–17 gives a broad permission and one specific prohibition.

- The LORD God commands the man that he may freely eat of every tree of the Garden.
- The tree of the knowledge of good and evil is excepted.
- The consequence is stated in connection with eating from that tree.

The warning is not isolated from the surrounding gift and permission. The command places a boundary within an environment God has planted and provided.

### 5.2 “Thou shalt surely die”

The KJV translates the Hebrew *mot tamut* as “thou shalt surely die.”

The doubled verbal construction emphasizes the certainty or force of the warning. It is not a separate statement that Adam will die twice. Nor does the construction alone define two stages of death.

**Textual observation:** death is certainly announced as the consequence of eating.

**Grammar boundary:** the Hebrew construction does not, by itself, settle the exact mode or chronology of the death.

### 5.3 “In the day that thou eatest thereof”

The Hebrew *beyom* is literally “in the day.” Its interpretation in this clause is debated. It may be understood as a specific day/time expression, as “when,” or in a more general or conditional sense according to context.

Genesis 3:5 uses the corresponding expression in the serpent's statement that the eyes of the man and woman would be opened in the day they ate. Genesis 3:7 then records that their eyes were opened after they ate. This immediate narrative correspondence is relevant to the question of whether Genesis 2:17's expression is intended with the same temporal immediacy.

The comparison creates a real interpretive question; it does not, on its own, prove that both statements must have exactly the same force.

## 6. Source observations: what happened in Genesis 3?

### 6.1 The serpent directly contradicts the warning

Genesis 3:4 records the serpent's statement:

> “Ye shall not surely die.”

The wording directly answers and contradicts the warning in Genesis 2:17. The dispute in the narrative concerns the consequence of disobedience and the reliability of what God said.

### 6.2 The eyes are opened

Genesis 3:6–7 records the eating and then says:

> “And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked...”

The opening of their eyes is narrated as an immediate consequence in the sequence. The text then records their making coverings from fig leaves.

The passage does not say that they physically died at that moment.

### 6.3 Fear, hiding, and the divine question

Genesis 3:8–10 records that the man and his wife heard the voice of the LORD God, hid themselves among the trees, and that the man was afraid because he was naked.

God calls to the man:

> “Where art thou?”

The man answers that he heard God's voice, was afraid, and hid himself.

**Direct observation:** after disobedience, the man is afraid and hides from the presence of the LORD God.

**Interpretive boundary:** the text records fear, hiding, and confrontation. Describing this as relational rupture or alienation is an interpretation of those events, supported by the wider biblical account, not a phrase explicitly used in Genesis 3:8–10.

### 6.4 Judgment is pronounced

Genesis 3:14–19 records judgments addressed to the serpent, the woman, and the man.

For the man, the ground is cursed for his sake; he will eat of it in sorrow; it will bring forth thorns and thistles; he will eat bread by the sweat of his face.

The judgment concludes:

> “Till thou return unto the ground; for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.” (Genesis 3:19, KJV)

This is an explicit reference to the man's eventual bodily return to the ground. It recalls Genesis 2:7, where the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground.

**Textual sequence:** formed from the ground (2:7); disobedience (3:6); judgment concerning the ground and human toil (3:17–19); return to the ground (3:19).

The text explicitly connects man's origin from dust with his return to dust. It does not use the later theological label “mortality” in this sentence.

### 6.5 The tree of life and the prevention of endless life

Genesis 3:22 records God's statement that the man must not take also of the tree of life, eat, and live for ever.

Genesis 3:23 records that the LORD God sent the man forth from the Garden of Eden to till the ground from which he was taken.

Genesis 3:24 records that God drove the man out and placed cherubim and a flaming sword at the east of the Garden:

> “to keep the way of the tree of life.”

**Direct observations:**

- The tree of life was present in the Garden before the transgression (Genesis 2:9).
- After the transgression, the man is sent out of the Garden.
- Access to the tree of life is guarded.
- The stated concern in Genesis 3:22 is that the man might eat and live for ever.
- The tree is not said to have been destroyed; the way to it is guarded.

**Important unresolved point:** Genesis 2–3 does not explicitly say that Adam had already eaten from the tree of life before the transgression. It also does not explicitly say that he had never eaten from it. Any argument that depends on one of those assumptions must identify it as an inference.

## 7. Genesis 5: Adam's bodily death

Genesis 5:5 records:

> “And all the days that Adam lived were nine hundred and thirty years: and he died.”

This is the first explicit record of Adam's bodily death in the genealogical sequence.

The narrative therefore places a long interval between the eating in Genesis 3 and the record of Adam's death in Genesis 5:5. The warning in Genesis 2:17 must be read in a way that accounts for both the immediate events in Genesis 3 and the eventual death recorded in Genesis 5.

Genesis 5:5 does not say that the warning was withdrawn. Nor does it state that Adam died on the same calendar day that he ate.

## 8. Principal interpretations of Genesis 2:17

The following interpretations appear in scholarly discussion. They are not all equally established, and some may describe connected dimensions rather than mutually exclusive alternatives.

### 8.1 Immediate physical death

On a strict reading of “in the day,” the warning could be understood as announcing physical death on the very day of eating.

**Difficulty:** Genesis 3 records continued bodily life after eating, and Genesis 5:5 records Adam's death at 930 years.

Possible responses include a reprieve or mitigation of the announced penalty. However, Genesis does not explicitly state in so many words, “God postponed the death penalty.” This is a theological explanation inferred from the sequence.

### 8.2 Becoming mortal or losing the possibility of immortality

This interpretation understands the eating as the turning point after which human death becomes inevitable. It is sometimes expressed as “you will become mortal.”

The tree of life and the expulsion in Genesis 3:22–24 are relevant to this reading: access to the tree is prevented, and the man is deprived of the possibility represented by eating and living for ever.

**Limitation:** the Hebrew phrase *mot tamut* does not itself mean “you will become mortal.” Lee's thesis notes that the Hebrew Bible does not explicitly state Adam's pre-Fall physical status as mortal or immortal, and argues that Genesis 2:17 alone does not establish the “become mortal” translation. The interpretation may be a theological synthesis of Genesis 2–3 and later texts, but it should not be presented as the direct lexical meaning of the Hebrew phrase.

### 8.3 Death begins as a consequence and culminates in bodily death

This reading understands the transgression as the decisive beginning of a death-bound condition, with the eventual return to dust as its culmination.

It accounts for the fact that the man continues living after the day of eating while Genesis 3:19 announces his return to dust and Genesis 5:5 records that he dies.

**Limitation:** “death begins as a process” is a theological description of the narrative sequence, not a literal phrase in Genesis 2:17. It must be supported by the narrative and canonical comparisons, not claimed as a direct translation.

### 8.4 Relational or spiritual death

This interpretation understands the announced death as an immediate rupture of communion with God, expressed in the man's fear, hiding, judgment, and removal from the Garden.

The sequence in Genesis 3:8–10 provides textual material for the relational dimension: the man hides and fears the presence of the LORD God. Genesis 3:23–24 then records expulsion and guarded access to the tree of life.

Popović's 2021 study argues that spiritual death understood as broken communion with God best fits the narrative logic of Genesis 2–3. It understands the expulsion as the visible consequence of the broken relationship.

**Limitation:** Genesis 2:17 does not use the phrase “spiritual death,” and Genesis 3 does not provide a technical definition of it. “Spiritual death” is a theological interpretation of the warning and the narrative's relational consequences, not a direct lexical translation.

### 8.5 Expulsion and loss of access to the tree of life

This reading places emphasis on Genesis 3:22–24. The man is expelled and prevented from eating of the tree of life and living for ever.

On this reading, the announced death is connected to the loss of access to the means by which human life could continue without end.

**Limitation:** Genesis 3:22 explicitly says that eating from the tree of life would result in living for ever, and Genesis 3:24 says the way is guarded. It does not explicitly say that Adam had previously eaten from the tree, nor does it state that expulsion alone exhausts the full meaning of “thou shalt surely die.”

### 8.6 A warning, test, or conditional sentence

The command can be read as a decisive boundary that calls for obedience. The death warning functions as a serious warning of the consequence of disobedience.

Popović discusses how *mot tamut* can function as a warning or threat intended to prevent the act. He also surveys the interpretation of *beyom* as “when,” “if,” or a temporal turning point after which consequences follow.

**Limitation:** the warning's function as a test of obedience does not by itself settle the meaning of death. It explains the role of the command in the narrative, not the full nature of the consequence.

### 8.7 Mitigation or mercy

Another interpretation is that the announced death was not executed immediately because God showed mercy, allowing the man and woman to continue living under judgment rather than ending their lives at once.

Genesis 3:21 records that the LORD God made coats of skins and clothed them. The text records this provision but does not explicitly call it a mitigation of the death sentence. The continued life of Adam and Eve is a narrative fact; interpreting the interval as mercy is a theological conclusion.

## 9. Hebrew grammar: what it establishes and what it does not

### 9.1 The force of *mot tamut*

The infinitive absolute plus finite verb from the same root intensifies or emphasizes the verbal statement. In Genesis 2:17, the construction makes the death warning emphatic and certain.

It does not establish:
- two separate deaths;
- a complete doctrine of spiritual death;
- the exact physical mechanism of death;
- by itself, the precise interval between transgression and bodily death.

### 9.2 The force of *beyom*

The expression is literally “in the day.” Its contextual range and force must be examined in the sentence and narrative. Scholarly discussion includes:
- a specific day or immediate time;
- a more general “when”;
- a conditional sense such as “if”;
- the beginning of a changed condition or consequences from the decisive event onward.

The immediate correspondence with Genesis 3:5–7 is a serious reason to examine a more immediate reading. The later narrative, however, records no immediate bodily death. The interpreter must account for both facts.

### 9.3 Grammar does not settle the theological synthesis

The grammar establishes the seriousness and certainty of the warning. The narrative supplies the consequences and sequence. Canonical passages such as Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15 develop the relationship between Adam's transgression and human death.

A sound conclusion requires all three forms of evidence to be kept in view: grammar, narrative context, and canonical development.

## 10. Canonical comparison: Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15

### 10.1 Romans 5:12–21

Paul writes that by one man sin entered the world, and death by sin, and death passed upon all men (Romans 5:12). He describes death reigning from Adam to Moses (v. 14) and contrasts the offence of one with the gift through Jesus Christ (vv. 15–17). He concludes by contrasting condemnation and death with justification, grace, and life (vv. 18–21).

**What Paul explicitly contributes:**
- sin entered through one man;
- death entered through sin;
- death reigned;
- Adam's offence is contrasted with Christ's righteous act and gift;
- grace reigns through righteousness unto eternal life through Jesus Christ.

Romans 5 therefore explicitly connects Adam's transgression with the entrance and reign of death in Paul's theological argument.

**Boundary:** Romans 5 does not provide a detailed lexical commentary on the Hebrew expression *beyom* in Genesis 2:17. It supplies canonical interpretation of Adam, sin, death, condemnation, grace, and life.

### 10.2 1 Corinthians 15:21–22

Paul writes:

> “For since by man came death, by man came also the resurrection of the dead.  
> For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive.”

This passage explicitly places death through Adam in contrast with resurrection through Christ.

The surrounding chapter concerns bodily resurrection. The comparison therefore cannot be reduced to an exclusively inward or metaphorical condition. Paul's argument includes the future resurrection of the dead.

### 10.3 What the New Testament clarifies

Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15 strengthen the conclusion that Adam's transgression is connected to human death, and that Christ is the answer to death through resurrection and life.

They do not remove the need to examine the particular sequence in Genesis 2–3. The New Testament supplies canonical interpretation; it should not be used to pretend that Genesis narrates events it does not narrate.

## 11. Analysis: how the warning and events relate

The evidence supports several related but distinguishable observations.

### 11.1 The warning is not withdrawn in the narrative

Genesis 3 does not record God withdrawing the warning. Instead, it records judgment, expulsion, the loss of access to the tree of life, and the declaration that the man will return to dust.

### 11.2 The day of eating marks a decisive change

The eating is followed immediately by opened eyes, awareness of nakedness, fear, hiding, confrontation, and judgment. The narrative presents no return to the former Garden condition.

Calling this a decisive change is a description of the sequence. Defining every aspect of that change as “spiritual death” is an interpretation that must be qualified.

### 11.3 The return to dust is explicitly connected to the man's origin

Genesis 2:7 and 3:19 form a clear textual relation: man is formed from dust and is told that he will return to dust. Genesis 5:5 records the eventual bodily death of Adam.

### 11.4 The tree of life is central to the consequence

Genesis 3:22–24 does not merely record that the man leaves a location. It gives the reason for preventing access to the tree of life: the man must not take, eat, and live for ever. The way is guarded.

The loss of access to the tree is therefore an explicit part of the post-transgression condition. Whether the tree was a continuously available means of sustaining life, a potential means not yet used, or a promised provision remains unresolved by the text alone.

### 11.5 Relational alienation and bodily death should not be collapsed

Fear and hiding from God's presence, expulsion from the Garden, return to dust, and eventual bodily death are all present in the narrative. They are related consequences, but they are not identical events.

The study should not force the choice that only one of these can be meant by the warning. Nor should it assert that every dimension has been fully defined by Genesis 2:17 alone.

## 14. Findings

1. Genesis 2:17 emphatically warns that the man will surely die if he eats from the prohibited tree.
2. The Hebrew *mot tamut* emphasizes certainty or force; it does not by itself define the exact mode or chronology of death.
3. The contextual force of *beyom* is debated. It may be read as a specific time expression or more generally as “when,” “if,” or the beginning of consequences from the decisive event.
4. Genesis 3 records immediate consequences after eating: opened eyes, awareness of nakedness, fear, hiding, confrontation, and judgment.
5. Genesis 3:19 explicitly announces the man's return to the ground, recalling his formation from dust in Genesis 2:7.
6. Genesis 3:22–24 explicitly records expulsion and guarded access to the tree of life, preventing the man from eating and living for ever.
7. Genesis 5:5 records Adam's eventual bodily death at 930 years.
8. Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15:21–22 explicitly connect death with Adam's transgression and contrast it with life and resurrection through Christ.
9. Relational or spiritual death is a significant interpretation of the immediate rupture described in Genesis 3, but it is not the exact wording of Genesis 2:17.
10. Loss of access to the tree of life is an explicit consequence, but the text does not state whether Adam had previously eaten from that tree.
11. The text does not explicitly state whether Adam was created physically immortal or mortal before the transgression.
12. The most responsible present conclusion is that the transgression marks a decisive change involving alienation, judgment, loss of access to the tree of life, and the certainty of return to dust; the precise relation among these dimensions remains subject to further canonical examination.
13. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
