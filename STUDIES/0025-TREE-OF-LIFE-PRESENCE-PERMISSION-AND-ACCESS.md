## 1. Question

Did Adam and Eve have access to the tree of life before the Fall? Did they actually eat from it? What exactly does Genesis 3:22–24 tell us about the change in access after disobedience?

The question requires several distinctions:

1. Was the tree of life present in the Garden before the Fall?
2. Was it within the area in which the man was permitted to eat?
3. Does Genesis record Adam or Eve actually eating from it before the Fall?
4. What does the Hebrew wording of Genesis 3:22 say about the proposed future eating?
5. What changes when God sends the man out and guards the way in Genesis 3:23–24?
6. What does the text establish about the tree's life-giving effect, and what does it leave unexplained?

The terms **presence**, **permission**, **access**, and **actual consumption** must not be treated as interchangeable.

The key Hebrew expressions examined are:

- פֶּן (*pen*) — “lest,” introducing a feared or guarded possibility.
- גַּם (*gam*) — “also,” “even,” or an additive particle whose precise attachment and force must be read in context.
- וְלָקַח גַּם מֵעֵץ הַחַיִּים (*wə-lāqaḥ gam mē-‘ēṣ haḥayyîm*) — “and take also from the tree of life.”

The Hebrew text and morphology were checked against interlinear presentations of Genesis 2:16 and 3:22. Translation notes were consulted for the force of the permission in Genesis 2:16 and the expression “tree of life.”

## 5. Source observations: the tree is present in the Garden

### 5.1 Genesis 2:8–9

Genesis 2:8 records that the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden and placed the man there.

Genesis 2:9 records that the LORD God caused trees to grow from the ground, pleasant to the sight and good for food. The verse names two particular trees:
- the tree of life in the midst of the Garden;
- the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

**Direct finding:** The tree of life is present in the Garden before the transgression. Its presence is not introduced for the first time after the Fall.

The location phrase “in the midst of the garden” is associated with the named trees in the Hebrew sentence. The text does not describe the tree of life as outside the Garden or already behind a barrier.

### 5.2 Genesis 2:16–17: the grant of food and the one stated exception

Genesis 2:16 records God's instruction:

> “Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:”

Genesis 2:17 then states the exception:

> “But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it...”

The wording establishes a broad permission followed by one specific prohibition.

The tree of life is named in Genesis 2:9. The tree of knowledge is the one specifically prohibited in Genesis 2:17. Genesis 2:16 does not list the tree of life as an exception.

**Textual observation:** Under the ordinary reading of “every tree” in Genesis 2:16, the tree of life is included in the permission to eat, because the next verse identifies the tree of knowledge as the prohibited exception.

**Qualification:** The narrative does not separately say, “Adam, you are commanded to eat from the tree of life.” Permission is not compulsion. Nor does the narrative state that Adam understood the tree's life-giving significance.

### 5.3 Permission is not proof of consumption

The text establishes:
- the tree's presence;
- the broad permission to eat from the Garden's trees;
- the explicit prohibition concerning the tree of knowledge.

It does **not** narrate an act in which Adam or Eve eats from the tree of life before the transgression.

This is an important distinction. A permitted act may be available without the narrative recording that it occurred.

## 6. Source observations: Genesis 3:22–24

### 6.1 The statement in Genesis 3:22

After the transgression, the LORD God says:

> “Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever:” (KJV)

The verse presents a feared or prevented possibility:
1. the man might put forth his hand;
2. take from the tree of life also;
3. eat;
4. live for ever.

The sequence is prospective in the narrative: God acts to prevent the proposed taking and eating.

**Direct finding:** Genesis 3:22 does not narrate the man eating from the tree of life. It describes the possibility of his doing so and the consequence of living for ever.

### 6.2 The particle *pen* — “lest”

The Hebrew particle *pen* introduces the concern that the man might take and eat from the tree of life.

Van Ee examines the use of *pen* in Genesis 3:22 and argues that it introduces a new situation to be prevented rather than simply describing the continuation of an action already underway. He notes that an ongoing reading has been proposed, but argues that the wider syntax and the comparison with the tree of knowledge make that reading unlikely.

This is a scholarly grammatical argument. It should not be turned into a claim that every use of *pen* in Hebrew has one inflexible meaning. The conclusion is contextual: in Genesis 3:22, the natural narrative reading is that God prevents a prospective act of eating from the tree of life.

### 6.3 The particle *gam* — “also”

The phrase is:

**וְלָקַח גַּם מֵעֵץ הַחַיִּים**

A close rendering is “and take also from the tree of life.”

The particle *gam* is additive. In this sentence it places the proposed taking from the tree of life alongside the prior taking/eating from the tree of knowledge.

Van Ee argues that *gam* modifies the object from which the man might take — “also from the tree of life” — rather than making the verb mean “take again” or “continue taking.” The new object is the tree of life.

This distinction matters:
- “take also from the tree of life” describes an additional object of taking;
- “take again from the tree of life” would suggest a prior taking from that same tree.

The Hebrew wording does not require the second reading.

**Bounded finding:** “Also” is not evidence that Adam had already eaten from the tree of life. In the sentence's comparison, the additional act is taking from the other tree after the eating from the tree of knowledge.

### 6.4 The strongest narrative reading

The combined force of the prospective concern (*pen*), the additive particle (*gam*), and the sequence of hand-stretching, taking, eating, and living forever supports the reading that the tree-of-life eating is an act still to be prevented.

Van Ee concludes, following Mettinger on this point, that Genesis 3:22 implies that the humans had not eaten from the tree of life before this moment. He also notes that Genesis 2:16 appears to have permitted eating from every tree, including the tree of life.

This interpretation is persuasive within the narrative, but the distinction between explicit statement and grammatical inference must remain visible.

## 7. Scholarly alternatives and counter-evidence

### 7.1 The proposal that humans had already eaten from the tree of life

Stordalen's interpretation, as reported by Van Ee, understands Genesis 3:22 as preventing the humans from continuing to eat from the tree of life after eating from the tree of knowledge. On this reading, the tree may have sustained or delayed aging through repeated eating.

**Strength:** It takes seriously the possibility that the humans had access to the tree before the Fall and attempts to explain why the tree is present in the Garden.

**Difficulty:** It must explain why Genesis does not explicitly report prior eating from the tree of life. It also has to account for the prospective wording of Genesis 3:22 and the way *gam* compares the new taking from the tree of life with the previous taking from the tree of knowledge.

Van Ee argues that the ongoing-consumption interpretation is grammatically and narratively less persuasive. The possibility should nevertheless be recorded as a scholarly alternative rather than erased.

### 7.2 The proposal that the tree was unknown to the humans

Mettinger proposes that the tree of life may have been known to God and the narrator but not to Adam and Eve. On this reading, the tree of knowledge functions as a test, while the tree of life may have been a potential reward for obedience.

**Strength:** It explains why the humans might not have eaten from a tree that was not prohibited in the command as recorded.

**Difficulty:** Genesis does not explicitly say the tree was hidden from Adam and Eve, nor does it explicitly state that the tree of life was a reward for passing a test. The proposal is a literary-theological inference.

### 7.3 Traditional explanations

Some traditional Jewish commentators propose that Adam had no reason to eat from the tree before death was decreed, or that he had no desire to do so before the transgression. These explanations show that interpreters have long recognized the question raised by Genesis 2:16 and 3:22.

They are interpretations, not statements made directly by Genesis.

### 7.4 What remains unproven

The text does not establish:
- that Adam ate from the tree of life before the Fall;
- that Adam never had physical access to it before the Fall;
- that the tree was hidden from Adam and Eve;
- that its fruit needed to be eaten once only;
- that its fruit needed to be eaten repeatedly;
- that Adam was inherently immortal;
- that Adam was doomed to die regardless of obedience.

Each of these claims requires additional argument beyond the direct wording of the passages.

## 8. The change in access: Genesis 3:23–24

Genesis 3:23 records:

> “Therefore the LORD God sent him forth from the garden of Eden, to till the ground from whence he was taken.”

Genesis 3:24 records:

> “So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life.”

The narrative records a concrete change:
- the man is sent out of the Garden;
- the way to the tree of life is guarded;
- cherubim and a flaming sword are stationed to keep that way.

The text says the way is guarded. It does not say the tree is destroyed.

### 8.1 Before and after

| Question | Before the transgression | After the transgression |
|---|---|---|
| Is the tree of life present? | Yes — Genesis 2:9. | The tree remains the object of guarded access — Genesis 3:22–24. |
| Is it within the Garden? | Yes — Genesis 2:9. | The man is sent out of the Garden — Genesis 3:23. |
| Is eating from it prohibited? | No such prohibition is stated; Genesis 2:16 grants permission to eat from every tree, with the tree of knowledge excepted in 2:17. | God prevents the man from taking and eating from it — Genesis 3:22. |
| Does Genesis record actual eating from it? | No act of eating is narrated. | No act of eating is narrated; the threatened possibility is prevented. |
| Is the way guarded? | No guard is described before the transgression. | Yes — Genesis 3:24. |

### 8.2 Presence, permission, access, and consumption

The evidence becomes clearer when these four ideas are separated.

**Presence:** the tree is in the Garden before the Fall.

**Permission:** the broad grant in Genesis 2:16 appears to include the tree of life, since Genesis 2:17 names the tree of knowledge as the exception.

**Access:** the tree is within the Garden, but Genesis does not describe Adam's movements to or from it. After the Fall, the way is explicitly guarded and the man is expelled.

**Consumption:** Genesis does not report Adam or Eve eating from the tree of life before the Fall. Genesis 3:22 presents a future act that God prevents. The strongest narrative reading is that they had not yet eaten from it.

These distinctions answer the central question without claiming more than the text establishes.

## 9. What does “live for ever” establish?

Genesis 3:22 connects eating from the tree of life with living for ever. The tree is therefore not merely called “life” as a decorative name. The narrative assigns its fruit a life-giving significance.

The NET Bible notes understand “tree of life” in light of Genesis 3:22 as a tree producing life-giving fruit.

The text does not explain:
- the biological mechanism;
- whether one eating would be sufficient;
- whether continued eating would be necessary;
- whether the effect is best described as preventing death, sustaining life, or granting life without end.

Those questions should remain open unless another passage supplies the answer.

The important textual point is that God identifies eating from the tree as the means by which the man could “live for ever,” and then prevents access.

## 10. Canonical comparison

### 10.1 Proverbs — tree of life as a wisdom image

Proverbs uses “tree of life” language in several contexts:
- Proverbs 3:18 associates wisdom with a tree of life to those who lay hold upon her.
- Proverbs 11:30 uses the tree-of-life image in connection with the fruit of righteousness.
- Proverbs 13:12 associates a tree of life with fulfilled desire.
- Proverbs 15:4 describes a wholesome tongue as a tree of life.

These are wisdom sayings and figurative uses. They show that “tree of life” becomes a biblical image for life-giving benefit, but they do not explain the physical or narrative mechanism of the tree in Genesis.

### 10.2 Revelation — the tree of life in the final vision

Revelation 2:7 promises the overcomer the right to eat of the tree of life in the paradise of God.

Revelation 22:1–5 describes the river of the water of life, the tree of life, the throne of God and of the Lamb, the removal of the curse, and God's servants seeing His face.

The same named tree appears in Genesis and Revelation. This establishes a strong canonical correspondence between the Garden's tree of life and the final biblical vision of life in God's presence.

However, Revelation does not provide a detailed explanation of how the fruit in Genesis operated before the Fall. Nor should every detail of the Eden narrative be mapped one-to-one onto Revelation without textual support.

### 10.3 Christ and the Way

Study 0016 examined the canonical relationship between the guarded way in Genesis 3:24, Jesus' words in John 14:6, access through His blood in Hebrews 10:19–22, and the tree of life in Revelation.

That prior study concluded that Jesus explicitly says He is the Way, the Truth, and the Life (John 14:6). The connection between this declaration and the guarded way to the tree of life is a canonical theological comparison, not a claim that John 14:6 directly quotes Genesis 3:24.

This study adds no new Christological definition and does not identify the physical tree in Genesis as Jesus.

## 11. Analysis and bounded conclusion

The evidence supports the following sequence:

1. God plants the tree of life in the Garden (Genesis 2:9).
2. God gives the man permission to eat from every tree of the Garden (Genesis 2:16).
3. God names one exception: the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 2:17).
4. Genesis does not narrate the man or woman eating from the tree of life before the Fall.
5. After the transgression, God says the man must not take also from the tree of life, eat, and live for ever (Genesis 3:22).
6. God sends the man out of the Garden and guards the way to the tree of life (Genesis 3:23–24).

### Working conclusion

**Adam and Eve had the tree of life present within the Garden, and the broad permission in Genesis 2:16 appears to include it. Genesis does not record that they actually ate from it. The strongest reading of Genesis 3:22 is that God prevents a further, prospective act of taking and eating from the tree of life after the transgression.**

The word “also” does not prove prior eating from the tree of life. In context, it most naturally places the proposed taking from the tree of life alongside the earlier taking from the tree of knowledge.

After the Fall, the situation changes decisively: the man is expelled and the way to the tree of life is guarded.

The text does not tell us whether the tree was hidden, whether the humans understood its purpose, or whether its fruit required one or repeated eating. It does not establish a complete doctrine of Adam's biological condition.

This conclusion is stronger than saying simply “we do not know whether they had access.” The narrative supports pre-Fall presence and apparent permission; it does not record actual consumption. The difference between access and use is essential.

## 14. Findings

1. Genesis 2:9 explicitly places the tree of life in the Garden before the Fall.
2. Genesis 2:16 gives permission to eat from every tree of the Garden; Genesis 2:17 identifies the tree of knowledge as the exception. The tree of life therefore appears to be included in the permission.
3. Permission does not prove that Adam or Eve actually ate from the tree.
4. Genesis does not narrate an act of eating from the tree of life before the transgression.
5. Genesis 3:22 describes the possibility of the man taking and eating from the tree of life and living for ever.
6. The Hebrew particle *gam* (“also”) is best read as an additive comparison with the previous taking/eating from the tree of knowledge, not as proof that the man had already eaten from the tree of life.
7. The prospective concern introduced by *pen* (“lest”) supports the reading that God prevents a new act of eating from the tree of life.
8. Some scholars propose that the humans had already eaten from the tree of life, but this interpretation must account for the lack of an explicit prior eating and the syntax of Genesis 3:22.
9. Mettinger proposes that the tree's significance may have been unknown to the human pair and that it may have functioned as a potential reward for obedience. This remains an inference.
10. Genesis 3:23–24 records the man's expulsion and the guarding of the way to the tree of life.
11. The text says the way is guarded; it does not say the tree is destroyed.
12. Genesis connects eating from the tree with living for ever, but does not explain whether one or repeated eating is required or how the tree's life-giving effect operates.
13. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
