## 1. Question

What does Paul mean by the closing words of Romans 5:12:

> “and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned:” (KJV)

How does the phrase “for that all have sinned” relate to Paul's preceding statement that sin entered the world through one man and death through sin?

The investigation must distinguish several questions that are often collapsed:

1. What does the Greek expression ἐφ’ ᾧ (*eph’ hō*) mean in this sentence?
2. Does “all have sinned” refer to each person's own sin, humanity's participation in Adam's sin, or a result that follows from death's spread?
3. What does Romans 5:13–14 add to Paul's explanation?
4. How do verses 15–19 clarify the relationship between Adam's one transgression and the many?
5. What is the relationship between Adam's act, humanity's condition, and each person's own sin?
6. How does Paul's Adam–Christ comparison constrain the interpretation?

This study does not assume that a disputed grammatical phrase can settle every later doctrinal question about original sin, imputation, inherited corruption, mortality, or individual responsibility.

## 5. Source observations: Romans 5:12

### 5.1 The KJV wording

Romans 5:12 reads:

> “Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned:”

The verse contains a sequence:

1. Sin entered the world through one man.
2. Death entered through sin.
3. Death passed to all people.
4. Paul adds the phrase “for that all have sinned.”

The first three statements are not grammatically identical to the fourth. The final phrase explains, qualifies, or states the consequence of the preceding spread of death, depending on the interpretation of *eph’ hō*.

**Direct observation:** Paul explicitly identifies one man as the means through whom sin entered the world. He explicitly connects death with sin. He explicitly describes death as spreading to all people.

The dispute concerns the exact relationship expressed by the final clause.

### 5.2 “For that” and the Greek phrase

The KJV's “for that” is an older English way of introducing an explanation or reason. The underlying Greek phrase is ἐφ’ ᾧ (*eph’ hō*).

The form ᾧ (*hō*) is dative singular and can be masculine or neuter in form. This makes a relative-pronoun interpretation grammatically possible, but the phrase's function and referent must be determined from Greek usage and the context.

The words πάντες ἥμαρτον (*pantes hēmarton*) mean “all sinned.” The verb is an aorist active indicative, third person plural.

**Important limitation:** The aorist form by itself does not prove that all human beings committed one simultaneous act at the moment Adam sinned. Nor does the tense alone prove that Paul refers only to a series of separate individual acts. The interpretation depends on the clause, its syntax, and the argument as a whole.

## 6. The principal interpretations of ἐφ’ ᾧ

### 6.1 Causal: “because all sinned”

This is the rendering used by many modern English translations, including the NET Bible.

On this reading:
- Adam's sin is the historical entrance of sin and death into the human world.
- Death spreads to all people because all have sinned.
- Each person's own sin is real and relevant.
- Adam remains the initiating figure in Paul's account.

**Strengths:**
- It gives the phrase a familiar causal sense.
- It fits Paul's repeated teaching that all have sinned (Romans 3:23).
- It preserves personal human sin and responsibility.
- It is the dominant rendering in many contemporary English translations.
- Petr Mareček's 2021 peer-reviewed study argues specifically for the causal reading, citing ancient usage, Paul's other uses of the construction, and the sentence's chiastic structure.

**Questions and difficulties:**
- How does this reading relate the universal reign of death to Adam's one transgression?
- Does it make each person's personal sin a separate cause of death, or does personal sin occur within the death-and-sin condition initiated through Adam?
- How does it account for Paul's emphasis in verses 15–19 on the one man's transgression and the many who are affected by it?

A causal translation does not, by itself, answer these theological questions.

### 6.2 Consecutive or resultative: “with the result that all sinned”

Joseph Fitzmyer argues that *eph’ hō* can carry a consecutive or resultative sense. On this reading, the sequence is:

- sin entered through one man;
- death entered through sin;
- death spread to all people;
- with the result that all sinned.

Dunson similarly argues for a resultative reading, paraphrasing the phrase as “from which it follows.”

**Strengths:**
- It emphasizes the consequence of Adam's act and the spread of death.
- It gives a close connection between the universal reign of death and the universal reality of sin.
- It can be read alongside Romans 5:13–14, where Paul discusses sin before the Mosaic law and death reigning from Adam to Moses.

**Questions and difficulties:**
- The consecutive interpretation is not the dominant translation in contemporary English Bibles.
- The exact force of *eph’ hō* remains debated.
- A resultative reading does not by itself define the mechanism by which Adam's act affects humanity.
- It must still account for Paul's statements about individual sin and responsibility elsewhere.

This reading is a serious scholarly option, not a settled grammatical fact.

### 6.3 Relative: “in whom all sinned”

A traditional interpretation reads the phrase as referring back to Adam: “in whom all sinned.”

This interpretation became especially influential in the Western theological tradition through the Latin rendering *in quo omnes peccaverunt* (“in whom all sinned”) and Augustine's use of it.

**Strengths:**
- It makes the connection between Adam's act and the universal human condition especially direct.
- It is consistent with the strong representative role Paul assigns to Adam in verses 15–19.
- It gives a concise account of humanity's connection with the one man's act.

**Questions and difficulties:**
- The antecedent “Adam” is not explicitly named immediately before the phrase.
- The Greek prepositional construction is disputed as a way of saying “in whom.”
- The Latin translation is not the Greek wording and cannot, by itself, decide the Greek syntax.
- The interpretation can be made to carry a detailed doctrine of original guilt that requires more argument than this phrase alone provides.

The historical influence of this reading must be distinguished from proof that it is the most likely Greek construction.

### 6.4 Further scholarly qualification

The debate is not simply “traditional versus modern.” Mareček's 2021 article argues that the causal reading is preferable on linguistic and structural grounds. Fitzmyer and Dunson defend a consecutive/resultative reading. Eby's thesis develops an interpretation in which inherited death is the basis for personal sin. These sources show that both the syntax and the theological sequence remain actively debated. No single proposal should be reported as the scholarly consensus without qualification.

### 6.5 Other proposals

Scholars have proposed additional nuances, including “on the basis of which,” “for this reason,” and interpretations that connect the phrase to the condition or sphere of death.

These proposals demonstrate that the phrase has a complex interpretive history. This study concentrates on the three major options recorded in the NET notes and the major scholarly arguments examined above.

## 7. Romans 5:13–14: Paul's explanation

Paul continues:

> “For until the law sin was in the world: but sin is not imputed when there is no law. Nevertheless death reigned from Adam to Moses, even over them that had not sinned after the similitude of Adam's transgression, who is the figure of him that was to come.” (Romans 5:13–14, KJV)

### 7.1 Sin before the Mosaic law

Paul states that sin was already in the world before the law was given.

He distinguishes sin's presence from the accounting or reckoning of sin where there is no law in the same form. This does not mean there was no sin before Moses. Paul explicitly says sin was in the world.

Romans 4:15 provides a related statement:

> “because the law worketh wrath: for where no law is, there is no transgression.”

Paul's concern is not to say that the pre-Mosaic world was morally sinless. His concern is to explain how death reigned before the Mosaic law.

### 7.2 Death reigned from Adam to Moses

Paul emphasizes that death reigned even over those who did not sin after the likeness of Adam's transgression.

Adam received a specific prohibition in Genesis 2:16–17. Those who lived between Adam and Moses did not receive that same Eden command, yet death still reigned.

**Direct observation:** Paul distinguishes Adam's particular transgression from the sins of people who did not transgress in the same manner.

This does not establish that those people committed no sin at all. Romans 5:13 says sin was in the world. The phrase “had not sinned after the similitude of Adam's transgression” concerns the likeness of the transgression, not the total absence of all sin.

### 7.3 The argument's significance

Romans 5:13–14 is important because Paul does not explain death's universal reign merely by pointing to the Mosaic law. Death's reign is already present from Adam to Moses.

Dunson interprets this as support for the resultative reading: death spread, and from this it follows that all sinned. Other interpreters maintain the causal reading while also recognizing that Adam's act initiated the reign of sin and death.

The text establishes the reign of death before Moses. The precise grammatical force of *eph’ hō* remains disputed.

## 8. Romans 5:15–19: Paul's extended Adam–Christ comparison

The following verses are essential. Verse 12 should not be isolated from Paul's explanation.

### 8.1 One man's transgression and the many — verse 15

Paul says:

> “For if through the offence of one many be dead, much more the grace of God, and the gift by grace, which is by one man, Jesus Christ, hath abounded unto many.”

The contrast is between:
- the offence of one man;
- the death of the many;
- the grace of God;
- the gift through Jesus Christ.

Paul does not describe Adam merely as an example whom everyone independently copies. The one man's offence has consequences for the many.

### 8.2 One offence and condemnation — verse 16

Paul contrasts judgment following one offence with the gracious gift following many offences and resulting in justification.

The contrast is not symmetrical:
- one offence leads to condemnation;
- the gracious gift addresses many offences and leads to justification.

The gift is not simply the opposite of one individual bad example. It is God's answer to a condition involving many transgressions.

### 8.3 Death reigned through one — verse 17

Paul states that death reigned through the offence of one.

He then contrasts this with those who receive abundance of grace and the gift of righteousness reigning in life through Jesus Christ.

The emphasis is on two reigns:
- death through the one man's offence;
- life through Jesus Christ.

### 8.4 One offence and one righteous act — verse 18

Paul sets one offence against one righteous act and describes the consequences in terms of condemnation and justification of life.

The verse is often read as a universal parallel. Its exact scope must be read with the wider letter and with verse 17's description of those who receive the gift. The present study does not settle every question concerning the scope of “all men” in verse 18.

### 8.5 One man's disobedience and the many made sinners — verse 19

Paul writes:

> “For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous.”

This is a particularly important statement.

Paul does not say only that Adam gave humanity a bad example that each person later imitated. He says that through one man's disobedience the many “were made sinners.”

The verb translated “were made” is from καθίστημι (*kathistēmi*). In this context, the statement concerns the status or condition of the many in relation to the one man's disobedience. The precise mechanism is not explained by the verb alone.

The parallel is Christ's obedience and the many being made righteous. Paul's emphasis is representative and consequential: the action of one has effects for the many.

### 8.6 What verses 15–19 establish

Whatever translation is chosen for *eph’ hō* in verse 12, the wider paragraph establishes that:
- Adam's one transgression has consequences beyond Adam himself;
- death reigns through the one man's offence;
- condemnation is connected with the one transgression;
- the many are described as made sinners through the one man's disobedience;
- Christ's obedience and God's gift bring the contrasting outcome of righteousness and life.

This wider argument prevents the final phrase of verse 12 from being used to erase Adam's representative significance.

## 9. Personal sin and Adamic consequence: must they be treated as competing explanations?

A frequent interpretive mistake is to frame the options as though only one of these statements can be true:

1. Adam's transgression brought sin and death into the world.
2. Human beings personally sin.

Paul's argument contains both themes.

Romans 5:12 identifies Adam as the one through whom sin entered and death through sin. The closing phrase says “all have sinned,” with its precise grammatical relation disputed.

Romans 5:13 says sin was in the world before the law. Romans 5:14 says death reigned even over those who did not sin in the same manner as Adam. Romans 5:15–19 repeatedly attributes universal consequences to the one man's offence and disobedience.

Romans 3:23 separately states:

> “For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God;”

Romans 5 must be read with that earlier argument, but Romans 3:23 should not simply be substituted as the definition of every phrase in Romans 5:12.

**Bounded synthesis:** Paul holds Adam's representative act and the universal reality of human sin together. The disputed phrase concerns the exact connection between the spread of death and the statement that all sinned. It does not justify removing either Adam's initiating role or the reality of personal human sin.

The text does not provide, in this passage alone, a complete technical account of how Adam's disobedience is transmitted to each descendant.

## 10. The Adam–Christ structure

Paul identifies Adam as “the figure of him that was to come” (Romans 5:14).

The contrast is not that Adam and Christ are identical, or that their acts have equal moral character. Paul explicitly says the gift is “not as the offence” and that grace abounds “much more.”

The basic comparison is:

| Adam | Jesus Christ |
|---|---|
| One man's disobedience | One man's obedience |
| One offence | Righteous act |
| Sin entered through the one | Grace and gift through the One |
| Death through sin | Life through righteousness |
| Death reigned | Those receiving grace reign in life |
| Condemnation | Justification |
| The many made sinners | The many made righteous |

The comparison is asymmetrical: Christ's gift is greater than Adam's offence.

This is central to Paul's argument. The passage is not merely a discussion of the origin of human sin. It is also an announcement of the greater work of Christ.

## 11. Analysis: what can and cannot be concluded?

### 11.1 What is explicit

- Sin entered the world through one man (Romans 5:12).
- Death entered through sin (Romans 5:12).
- Death spread to all people (Romans 5:12).
- Paul says “all have sinned” (Romans 5:12).
- Sin was in the world before the Mosaic law (Romans 5:13).
- Death reigned from Adam to Moses (Romans 5:14).
- Adam is a figure of the One to come (Romans 5:14).
- One man's transgression brought death to the many (Romans 5:15).
- Death reigned through the one man's offence (Romans 5:17).
- One man's disobedience made the many sinners (Romans 5:19).
- Christ's obedience is the basis of the contrasting gift of righteousness (Romans 5:15–19).

### 11.2 What is grammatically disputed

The precise meaning of *eph’ hō*:
- causal: “because”;
- consecutive/resultative: “with the result that”;
- relative: “in whom” or a related relative construction.

The NET Bible explicitly records the first three as major options. Fitzmyer argues for the consecutive reading. Dunson also defends a resultative reading in the context of Romans 5:13–14. The causal reading remains the dominant rendering in many translations and commentaries.

### 11.3 What is not established by the disputed phrase alone

- That all people literally performed Adam's act at the same instant.
- That the aorist verb by itself proves a single collective event.
- That each person dies only because of his or her own independent personal sins, with no representative effect from Adam.
- That the phrase alone proves a complete doctrine of inherited guilt.
- That the Greek phrase must mean “in Adam” because the Latin Vulgate used *in quo*.
- That the phrase's meaning is settled beyond dispute.

### 11.4 Working conclusion

The strongest conclusion that can be stated without overstating the Greek is:

**Paul explicitly teaches that Adam's one transgression was the historical entrance of sin and death into the world and that the consequences extend to humanity. He also states that all have sinned. The precise grammatical relationship expressed by ἐφ’ ᾧ remains disputed: causal, consecutive/resultative, and relative interpretations have all been defended. Yet verses 15–19 make clear that Paul attributes the reign of death and the condition of the many as sinners to the one man's disobedience, while setting that representative action against the greater obedience and gift of Jesus Christ.**

This preserves Paul's explicit statements while refusing to make a disputed grammatical phrase carry more doctrinal weight than it can bear.

## 14. Findings

1. Romans 5:12 explicitly attributes the entrance of sin into the world to one man.
2. Paul explicitly connects death with sin and says death spread to all people.
3. The KJV's “for that all have sinned” translates the Greek phrase ἐφ’ ᾧ πάντες ἥμαρτον (*eph’ hō pantes hēmarton*).
4. The phrase has three major interpretations: causal (“because”), consecutive/resultative (“with the result that”), and relative (“in whom” or a related construction).
5. The causal reading is the dominant rendering in many modern English translations; Fitzmyer and Dunson defend a consecutive/resultative reading.
6. The Latin Vulgate's *in quo* influenced the Western “in whom” tradition, but the Latin rendering does not independently settle the Greek syntax.
7. The aorist form of *hēmarton* does not, by itself, prove that all humanity committed one simultaneous act in Adam.
8. Romans 5:13–14 says sin was present before the Mosaic law and death reigned from Adam to Moses, including over those who did not sin in the same manner as Adam.
9. Romans 5:15–19 repeatedly attributes death, condemnation, and the many being made sinners to the one man's transgression and disobedience.
10. Paul therefore presents both Adam's representative consequence and the universal reality of human sin; the two must not be treated as mutually exclusive.
11. The passage does not, by itself, explain every detail of the mechanism by which Adam's disobedience affects each descendant.
12. The Adam–Christ comparison is central, and Christ's gift is described as greater than Adam's offence.
13. No GUA-W architecture change is proposed.
