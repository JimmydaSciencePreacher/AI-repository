## 1. Question

What does Paul mean when he writes:

> “For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous.” (Romans 5:19, KJV)

The Greek verb translated “were made” and “shall be made” is from **καθίστημι** (*kathistēmi*). This investigation asks:

1. What range of meaning does *kathistēmi* have in the New Testament?
2. What do the two passive forms in Romans 5:19 say grammatically?
3. Does “made” mean appointed, constituted, placed in a status, brought into a condition, or morally transformed?
4. How do Romans 5:15–21 and especially verses 16–18 constrain the interpretation?
5. What does the parallel between Adam's disobedience and Christ's obedience establish?
6. What does the verse leave unexplained about the relation between Adam, the many, personal sin, and righteousness in Christ?

The question follows Study 0026, which identified Romans 5:19 as a key statement about the consequences of one man's disobedience.

## 4. Sources and method

### 4.1 Primary text and Greek wording

Romans 5:19 in the KJV:

> “For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous.”

The two relevant Greek forms are:

- **κατεστάθησαν** (*katestathēsan*) — “were made / were constituted”; aorist passive indicative, third person plural.
- **κατασταθήσονται** (*katastathēsontai*) — “will be made / will be constituted”; future passive indicative, third person plural.

Both are forms of **καθίστημι** (*kathistēmi*).

The construction is parallel:
- through the disobedience of the one → the many were constituted sinners;
- through the obedience of the one → the many will be constituted righteous.

## 5. Source observations: Romans 5:19

### 5.1 The sentence's parallel structure

Paul uses two corresponding clauses:

| Adam | Christ |
|---|---|
| One man's disobedience | The obedience of one |
| The many were made sinners | The many shall be made righteous |
| Aorist passive | Future passive |

The repeated construction matters. Paul does not describe Adam's action in one category and Christ's action in an unrelated category. He sets the one man's disobedience beside the one man's obedience and sets the condition of the many in Adam's case beside the condition of the many in Christ's case.

The parallel is real, but it is not a claim that Adam and Christ are equal in person, authority, or outcome. Romans 5:15–17 explicitly emphasizes that the gift is “not as the offence” and that grace abounds “much more.”

### 5.2 The passive voice

Both verbs are passive:
- the many **were constituted** sinners;
- the many **will be constituted** righteous.

The grammatical subject is “the many.” The phrases “by one man's disobedience” and “by the obedience of one” identify the means or ground through which the contrasting results occur.

The passive voice does not, by itself, name the agent who performs the constituting. Nor does it explain the entire mechanism by which the result applies to the many. The surrounding paragraph attributes the consequences to the one man's disobedience and the contrasting gift to God's grace through Jesus Christ.

### 5.3 The tense difference

The first verb is aorist: “were made / were constituted.”
The second verb is future: “shall be made / shall be constituted.”

The forms distinguish the clauses temporally. The first looks back to the consequence associated with Adam's disobedience; the second looks forward to the consequence associated with Christ's obedience.

The future form should not be silently changed into a present tense. At the same time, it should not be used in isolation to argue that righteousness has no present reality. Romans 5:17 speaks of receiving the abundance of grace and the gift of righteousness, and Romans 5:1 has already spoken of justification by faith. The future in verse 19 must be read within Paul's complete argument.

## 6. Lexical examination: καθίστημι (*kathistēmi*)

### 6.1 Range of meaning

The verb can carry meanings such as:
- to set or place;
- to appoint;
- to establish;
- to constitute;
- to bring or put someone into a position or condition.

The exact sense depends on the object, construction, and context.

A common mistake is to choose one English gloss and make it carry every possible nuance. “Appoint,” “constitute,” and “make” overlap in some contexts but are not interchangeable in every sentence.

### 6.2 New Testament examples

The New Testament uses the same verb in different contexts:

- **Acts 6:3:** the community is to select qualified men whom the apostles may appoint over a task.
- **Hebrews 5:1:** a high priest is taken from among men and appointed for men in things pertaining to God.
- **Hebrews 7:28:** the law appoints men who have infirmity as high priests.
- **James 4:4:** friendship with the world constitutes or makes one an enemy of God.

These examples show that the verb can describe appointment to an office, establishment in a role, or placement in a relation or condition. It does not always mean inward moral transformation.

### 6.3 What the lexical range contributes

The lexical evidence supports translating the Romans 5:19 forms as “made,” “constituted,” or “placed in a condition/status,” depending on the translation's style.

The lexical evidence alone does not decide whether Paul is describing:
- a legal or representative standing;
- a condition shared by the many;
- a transformed moral character;
- a combination of status and condition.

That decision must be tested against Romans 5:15–21 and Paul's wider argument.

## 7. Immediate context: Romans 5:15–21

### 7.1 Verse 15: the one and the many

Paul says that through the offence of one, many are dead, and contrasts this with the grace of God and the gift by Jesus Christ.

The one man's act has consequences for the many. The comparison is not merely about one person's bad example followed by others.

### 7.2 Verse 16: judgment and gift

Paul contrasts judgment from one offence, resulting in condemnation, with the gift that follows many offences and results in justification.

This verse introduces explicitly judicial language:
- judgment;
- condemnation;
- free gift;
- justification.

The presence of these terms is important when interpreting “made sinners” and “made righteous” in verse 19. The paragraph includes a status or standing dimension; it cannot be reduced to a description of inward behavior alone.

### 7.3 Verse 17: death reigning and the gift of righteousness

Paul says death reigned through the offence of one. He contrasts this with those who receive abundance of grace and the gift of righteousness, who reign in life by Jesus Christ.

The language includes:
- a reign or dominion;
- a gift received;
- righteousness;
- life through Christ.

This further cautions against defining verse 19 as only an inward moral change. Paul's paragraph concerns the consequences of the two representative acts and the reigns associated with them.

### 7.4 Verse 18: condemnation and justification of life

Paul places one offence and one righteous act in parallel, with condemnation and justification of life as their corresponding outcomes.

This judicial language strengthens the reading that verse 19 concerns more than a simple imitation of Adam's behavior. However, the exact relation between “all men” in verse 18 and “many” in verse 19 remains a separate scope question.

### 7.5 Verse 19: the central comparison

Paul summarizes:

> “For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous.”

The one man's disobedience is the means associated with the many being constituted sinners. The obedience of Christ is the means associated with the many being constituted righteous.

The verse states a consequential relation between one person's act and the many. It does not describe the process in technical metaphysical terms.

### 7.6 Verses 20–21: law, offence, grace, and reign

Paul explains that the law entered so that the offence might abound, but where sin abounded, grace did much more abound. Grace reigns through righteousness unto eternal life by Jesus Christ.

This ending reinforces the asymmetry: grace is not merely a matching counterweight to Adam's act. It superabounds and reigns through Jesus Christ.

## 8. Interpretive possibilities and limits

### 8.1 “Made” as appointed or constituted in a standing

This interpretation emphasizes the verb's sense of being placed or established in a position or condition.

**Strengths:**
- It respects the lexical range of *kathistēmi*.
- It fits the surrounding language of judgment, condemnation, justification, and gift.
- It preserves the representative relation between the one and the many.
- It avoids reducing Paul's statement to moral imitation.

**Limits:**
- “Constituted” can sound technical in English and may imply a fully specified doctrine that the verse itself does not spell out.
- The verb alone does not explain how this standing applies to each person.
- It does not settle the relation between representative status and personal conduct.

### 8.2 “Made” as brought into a condition

This interpretation emphasizes that the many are not merely described as having received a label; they are placed in a condition associated with Adam's disobedience or Christ's obedience.

**Strengths:**
- It takes seriously the consequential language of the paragraph.
- It can accommodate the connection between Adamic consequence and the reign of death.
- It does not restrict the verb to an administrative appointment.

**Limits:**
- “Condition” can remain vague unless the context defines it.
- It must not be used to import a complete theory of inherited corruption or biological change.
- The verse does not specify every feature of the condition.

### 8.3 “Made” as inward moral transformation

Some readers may hear “made sinners” as meaning that Adam's descendants become morally sinful in their actions or character, and “made righteous” as meaning that Christ's people become inwardly morally transformed.

**Strengths:**
- Scripture elsewhere speaks of actual conduct, renewed life, and transformation.
- Romans 6 addresses the believer's changed relation to sin and the call to walk in newness of life.

**Limits:**
- Romans 5:16–18 explicitly includes condemnation and justification, which are not reducible to inward moral transformation.
- The parallel clauses are grounded in the acts of the one, not simply in the later behavior of the many.
- Romans 5:19 alone does not explain the relation between status, inward renewal, and conduct.

Therefore an inward-transformation-only reading is too narrow for the paragraph.

### 8.4 “Made” as moral imitation alone

On this reading, Adam's descendants are “made sinners” because they imitate his disobedience, while Christ's followers are “made righteous” because they imitate His obedience.

**Difficulty:**
This reading cannot adequately account for the full sequence in Romans 5:15–18: death through one, condemnation from one offence, and the gift of justification. Imitation may be a theme elsewhere, but it is insufficient as the sole explanation of Paul's argument here.

### 8.5 Working lexical and contextual judgment

The best bounded rendering for explanatory purposes is:

**“Through the disobedience of the one, the many were constituted sinners; through the obedience of the One, the many will be constituted righteous.”**

“Constituted” communicates that the verb concerns being established or placed in a resulting status/condition. It does not, by itself, decide whether the complete theological account should be described in terms of imputation, participation, inherited condition, or another formulation.

The surrounding verses require a judicial/standing dimension, while Paul's wider teaching also addresses real conduct and transformation. Neither dimension should be erased.

## 9. Adam and Christ: the parallel without false equivalence

Paul's comparison can be stated carefully:

| Adam | Jesus Christ |
|---|---|
| One man's disobedience | The obedience of the One |
| The many constituted sinners | The many constituted righteous |
| One offence associated with condemnation | The gift associated with justification |
| Death reigns through the offence | Those receiving grace reign in life |
| Sin and death enter and reign | Grace reigns through righteousness unto eternal life |

Three boundaries are necessary:

1. **The parallel is not equality.** Paul says grace abounds “much more” (Romans 5:15, 17, 20).
2. **The one and the many are not interchangeable.** Paul distinguishes the initiating act from its consequences among the many.
3. **The verse does not explain the complete mechanism.** It states the relationship and outcome; it does not provide a technical account of transmission.

Christ is not merely a second Adam who provides a better example. In Paul's paragraph, Christ is the source of a gift that overcomes the consequences of the offence.

## 10. Relationship to other Pauline passages

### 10.1 Romans 3: righteousness and the reality of sin

Romans 3:9–26 describes all as under sin and then speaks of God's righteousness through faith in Jesus Christ, justification freely by grace, and redemption in Christ.

This passage confirms that Paul's language of righteousness includes God's justifying action. It does not make Romans 5:19 a mere synonym for inward moral improvement.

### 10.2 Romans 6: a changed walk follows

Romans 6:1–14 addresses the believer's relation to sin through union with Christ's death and resurrection. Paul calls believers to reckon themselves dead indeed unto sin and alive unto God, and not to let sin reign in their mortal bodies.

This passage shows that the gift and standing associated with Christ have consequences for conduct. But Romans 6's exhortation should not be collapsed into the meaning of *kathistēmi* in Romans 5:19.

### 10.3 1 Corinthians 15: Adam, Christ, death, and life

1 Corinthians 15:21–22 states that by man came death and by man came also the resurrection of the dead; as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive.

Verses 45–49 further contrast the first Adam and the last Adam, the natural and spiritual, and the earthly and heavenly.

This parallel supports the broad representative contrast between Adam and Christ. It does not, without further analysis, settle every question about the scope of “all” or the precise mechanism of participation.

### 10.4 2 Corinthians 5: righteousness in Christ

2 Corinthians 5:17–21 speaks of the new creation, reconciliation through Christ, and being made the righteousness of God in Him.

This passage is relevant because it connects righteousness with Christ and reconciliation. Its wording and context differ from Romans 5:19; it should be used as a canonical comparison, not as a replacement for Romans 5's argument.

### 10.5 Ephesians 2: dead in sins and made alive with Christ

Ephesians 2:1–10 describes the former condition in trespasses and sins, God's action in making alive together with Christ, salvation by grace through faith, and creation unto good works.

This passage helps distinguish the former condition, God's action, and the resulting walk. It does not define *kathistēmi* in Romans 5:19.

## 11. Findings: what the evidence supports

### 11.1 Directly supported by the text

- Paul attributes the constitution of the many as sinners to the disobedience of one man (Romans 5:19).
- Paul attributes the constitution of the many as righteous to the obedience of Jesus Christ (Romans 5:19).
- Both verbs are passive forms of *kathistēmi*.
- The first form is aorist; the second is future.
- The surrounding paragraph explicitly includes judgment, condemnation, justification, gift, righteousness, death, life, and reign.
- Paul presents Christ's gift as exceeding Adam's offence.

### 11.2 Supported lexical and contextual interpretation

- *Kathistēmi* can mean set, appoint, establish, constitute, or place in a position/condition.
- “Constituted” is a defensible explanatory rendering in Romans 5:19.
- The paragraph requires more than an imitation-only reading.
- The paragraph includes a status or judicial dimension; it cannot be reduced to inward moral transformation alone.
- The verse describes a representative and consequential relationship between the one and the many.

### 11.3 Not established by the verb alone

- The exact metaphysical mechanism by which Adam's act affects his descendants.
- A complete doctrine of inherited guilt or imputation.
- That “made sinners” means only inward moral corruption.
- That “made righteous” means only inward moral transformation.
- That “made righteous” means only a legal declaration with no resulting life or conduct.
- That the future tense means righteousness has no present reality.
- That the future tense by itself identifies a complete eschatological timetable.
- The exact numerical or universal scope of “many” in the verse.

## 14. Limitations and counter-evidence

- Lexical dictionaries list possible senses; they do not determine a word's meaning in a particular sentence.
- The passive voice does not by itself name the ultimate agent or explain the process.
- “Constituted” may be more precise than ordinary “made” for some readers, but it can sound more technical than the Greek requires.
- The immediate context includes legal language, but it also includes reign, life, and grace. A purely forensic or purely transformative reading is too narrow.
- The future tense in the Christ clause requires further study in relation to Paul's present language of justification and the future hope of resurrection.
- The scope of “many” in verse 19 and “all men” in verse 18 requires a separate investigation.
- The Expositor's Greek Testament raises the possibility that the many are constituted sinners mediately through their own sin, which is traced back to Adam; this alternative deserves further contextual evaluation and must not be omitted.
- The relation between Adamic consequence and each person's actual sins remains connected to the unresolved questions from Study 0026.
- No scientific evidence is relevant to deciding the lexical meaning of this Greek verb; the scientific constraint is therefore not a claim of scientific confirmation but a safeguard against importing unsupported mechanisms.
