## 1. Question

Why does Paul write that through the obedience of the One “shall many be made righteous” (Romans 5:19, KJV), using a future passive verb, after already speaking of present justification and the gift of righteousness in Romans 5:1, 5:9, and 5:17?

This study investigates:

1. What does the future passive form in Romans 5:19 establish grammatically?
2. How does its future wording relate to Paul's present-tense and completed-action language about justification?
3. Does the future point to final judgment, resurrection and consummation, the continuing application of Christ's work to believers, or a broader certainty?
4. How do Romans 5:9–10, Romans 6, Romans 8, and 1 Corinthians 15 clarify the relationship between present standing and future completion?
5. What can be concluded without making the tense carry more than it can establish?

The immediate question is carried forward from Study 0027. That study established that the second verb in Romans 5:19 is future passive indicative, but did not determine the temporal or theological reference of that future.

## 5. Source observations

### 5.1 Romans 5:1 — justification already spoken of as received

Paul writes:

> “Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.”

The phrase “being justified” translates a Greek aorist passive participle. In the sentence, Paul speaks of justification as the basis for the believers' present peace with God. The verse does not describe justification as something that has no present reality until a future event.

### 5.2 Romans 5:2 — present access and future hope

Paul says believers have access by faith into grace, “wherein we stand,” and “rejoice in hope of the glory of God.”

The sentence holds present standing and future hope together. Present access does not eliminate the future hope; future hope does not erase present standing.

### 5.3 Romans 5:9–10 — justified now, saved in the future

Paul writes:

> “Much more then, being now justified by his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him.” (Romans 5:9, KJV)

And:

> “For if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, we shall be saved by his life.” (Romans 5:10, KJV)

These sentences are important controls. Paul explicitly places a present or completed reality (“now justified”; “reconciled”) beside a future reality (“we shall be saved”).

The future is not evidence that the present justification was unreal. Nor is present justification evidence that the future salvation is unnecessary.

### 5.4 Romans 5:17 — gift received and life to come

Paul speaks of “they which receive abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness” reigning in life by Jesus Christ.

The language of receiving the gift is not simply postponed to the future. Yet the outcome is described as reigning in life through Christ. The verse therefore contributes to the same present-and-future pattern.

### 5.5 Romans 5:19 — the future passive

The Greek form is:

**κατασταθήσονται — *katastathēsontai***

Its parsing is:

- future tense-form;
- passive voice;
- indicative mood;
- third person plural.

The grammatical subject is “the many.” The clause is:

> “so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous.”

The future form is real and must not be silently converted into a present tense. The passive voice also remains significant: the many receive the action; the form does not itself provide a complete account of the agent or mechanism.

Grammar establishes that Paul uses a future passive indicative. Grammar alone does not identify whether the future is chiefly final, distributive, gnomic, or inclusive of more than one aspect.

### 5.6 Romans 5:20–21 — grace reigns unto eternal life

Paul concludes the unit by saying that grace “might reign through righteousness unto eternal life by Jesus Christ our Lord.”

The passage's movement is not merely from a past event to a present legal label. It moves through sin, grace, righteousness, reign, and eternal life. The future clause in verse 19 belongs to this larger movement.

## 6. The interpretive question: what does the future indicate?

Three major readings appear in the commentaries examined. They should not be collapsed into one another.

### 6.1 Future consummation or eschatological completion

On this reading, the future points toward the final completion of the righteous condition secured through Christ. It may include the final public vindication of those who belong to Christ and the completion of salvation in resurrection life.

**Contextual support:**

- Romans 5:2 speaks of hope of the glory of God.
- Romans 5:9–10 explicitly contrasts being justified/reconciled with future salvation.
- Romans 8:18–23 speaks of future glory and the redemption of the body.
- 1 Corinthians 15:20–23 places the resurrection of those who belong to Christ after Christ the firstfruits, at His coming.
- 1 Corinthians 15:42–54 describes the raising of the dead and the transformation of the mortal into immortality.
- Philippians 3:20–21 speaks of the Saviour from heaven changing the body of our humiliation.

This reading has substantial canonical support for a future completion of salvation. The Pillar commentary identifies an eschatological aspect as a serious possibility.

**Limit:** The future form in Romans 5:19 does not itself name the Judgment Day, resurrection, or a specific event. Those associations come from reading the verse within Paul's wider teaching.

### 6.2 Future application as people come to faith

A second reading takes the future as describing the continuing application of Christ's obedience to people as they come to faith. The future is not restricted to one final moment; it may extend across the unfolding history of the gospel.

The Cambridge commentary and Expositor's Greek Testament represent versions of this reading. The Expositor's Greek Testament argues that if Paul intended only a single completed event parallel to Adam's past act, an aorist might have been expected; it therefore reads the future in relation to the many being constituted righteous as they believe.

**Contextual support:**

- Romans 5:1 has already spoken of justification by faith.
- Romans 5:17 describes those who receive the abundance of grace and the gift of righteousness.
- Paul's gospel is proclaimed and received by people across time.

**Limit:** The future tense alone does not prove a distributive sequence of individual conversions. That interpretation requires a broader contextual argument. It also must not reduce the future to the repeated moment of initial belief and exclude the final completion of salvation.

### 6.3 Gnomic or certainty-emphasizing future

A third proposal treats the future as emphasizing the certainty or general truth of what God will do for those who belong to Christ. The Wenstrom exposition calls this a “gnomic future.”

**Strength:** It recognizes that the future clause may express more than a date on a timeline. Paul's statement is assured, not speculative.

**Limit:** Calling the form “gnomic” is an interpretive grammatical classification, not something established merely by observing the future morphology. It should not be treated as settled consensus or used to erase the future orientation of the clause.

## 7. Present justification and future righteousness are not contradictory

The apparent tension is reduced when Paul's own distinctions are preserved.

| Passage | What Paul says | Temporal emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Romans 5:1 | “being justified by faith” | Present standing grounded in Christ |
| Romans 5:2 | “wherein we stand” and hope of glory | Present access and future hope |
| Romans 5:9 | “now justified” and “we shall be saved” | Present justification and future salvation |
| Romans 5:10 | Reconciled, “we shall be saved by his life” | Reconciliation and future salvation |
| Romans 5:17 | Receive the gift of righteousness | Gift received; life in Christ |
| Romans 5:19 | “shall many be made righteous” | Future passive result |
| Romans 8:23 | Waiting for “the redemption of our body” | Future bodily completion |
| 1 Corinthians 15:23 | Those who are Christ's at His coming | Resurrection order and future event |

The texts do not require the conclusion that believers are unrighteous until the last day. Romans 5:1 and 5:9 explicitly speak of present justification.

They also do not require the conclusion that every aspect of salvation is already complete in the believer's present experience. Romans 5:9–10 and 1 Corinthians 15 retain a future horizon.

A careful distinction is therefore necessary:

- **Present justification:** Paul speaks of believers as justified now through Christ.
- **Present reception and participation:** Paul speaks of receiving grace and the gift of righteousness, and Romans 6 describes present union with Christ and newness of life.
- **Future completion:** Paul also speaks of future salvation, glory, resurrection, and the redemption of the body.

These are related dimensions in Paul's account. They should not be treated as interchangeable words for one event, nor separated as though they were unrelated saving works.

## 8. Romans 6: present participation and future likeness

Romans 6 is an important companion passage, but it does not define the future tense in Romans 5:19 by itself.

### 8.1 Present union and newness of life

Romans 6:4 says believers are buried with Christ by baptism into death so that, as Christ was raised, “even so we also should walk in newness of life.”

Paul's argument moves from union with Christ to a changed present walk. This means that the righteousness associated with Christ cannot be treated as a merely external label that has no bearing on life.

### 8.2 Future likeness to His resurrection

Romans 6:5 says:

> “For if we have been planted together in the likeness of his death, we shall be also in the likeness of his resurrection.”

Here Paul explicitly joins a present relation to Christ's death with a future likeness to His resurrection.

Romans 6:8 likewise says:

> “Now if we be dead with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with him.”

The pattern is significant: present participation does not exhaust the future outcome.

### 8.3 The present command

Romans 6:11 tells believers to reckon themselves “to be dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God through Jesus Christ our Lord.” Romans 6:12–14 then calls them not to let sin reign in their mortal bodies.

This is a present consequence and responsibility. It must not be confused with the future passive verb in Romans 5:19, but it belongs to the wider Pauline account of life in Christ.

## 9. 1 Corinthians 15: resurrection as a clearly future completion

1 Corinthians 15 supplies a particularly clear resurrection context.

### 9.1 Adam and Christ

Paul writes:

> “For since by man came death, by man came also the resurrection of the dead.” (1 Corinthians 15:21, KJV)

And:

> “For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive.” (1 Corinthians 15:22, KJV)

The Adam–Christ comparison is explicit, as in Romans 5. However, the verbs are not identical: Romans 5:19 uses *kathistēmi* (“constitute/make”), while 1 Corinthians 15:22 uses a form of *zōopoieō* (“make alive”). The passages are related but should not be treated as though they use the same word or answer exactly the same question.

### 9.2 The order of resurrection

1 Corinthians 15:23 adds:

> “But every man in his own order: Christ the firstfruits; afterward they that are Christ's at his coming.”

This verse explicitly locates the resurrection of those who belong to Christ at His coming. It therefore supplies strong evidence that Paul's salvation teaching includes a future bodily completion.

### 9.3 The nature of the completion

1 Corinthians 15:42–54 describes the body as raised in incorruption, glory, and power. The mortal puts on immortality, and death is swallowed up in victory.

This is more specific than Romans 5:19. It establishes a resurrection horizon in Paul's teaching; it does not prove that “shall be made righteous” in Romans 5:19 is simply another way of saying “shall be resurrected.”

### 9.4 Scope caution

The phrase “in Christ shall all be made alive” must be read with Paul's following order: Christ the firstfruits, then those who are Christ's at His coming. This study does not use the verse to settle every debate about the scope of “all.”

## 10. Romans 8: future glory and bodily redemption

Romans 8:18–30 develops the future horizon of salvation:

- present sufferings are not worthy to be compared with the glory to be revealed;
- creation waits for deliverance;
- believers who have the firstfruits of the Spirit groan while waiting for adoption, “to wit, the redemption of our body” (8:23);
- believers are saved by hope (8:24);
- those whom God justified are also described in relation to glorification (8:30).

Romans 8:30 uses a sequence of past-form verbs, including “glorified,” in a statement of God's purpose. That grammatical form should not be used to deny the future hope described explicitly in verses 18–25. It presents the certainty of God's purpose in Paul's argument.

This context supports a real distinction between present justification and the future manifestation/completion of salvation. It does not establish that Romans 5:19's future has only one possible temporal nuance.

## 11. Findings: what the evidence supports

### 11.1 Directly supported by the text and grammar

- Romans 5:19 uses the future passive indicative **κατασταθήσονται** (*katastathēsontai*).
- The KJV accurately preserves the future wording: “shall many be made righteous.”
- Romans 5:1 and 5:9 speak of justification as a present or already accomplished reality for believers.
- Romans 5:9–10 also speak of future salvation.
- Romans 6 joins present participation in Christ with future likeness to His resurrection.
- 1 Corinthians 15 explicitly places the resurrection of those who belong to Christ at His coming.
- Romans 8 speaks of future glory and bodily redemption.

### 11.2 Supported interpretation

- Present justification and future salvation are complementary dimensions in Paul's argument, not contradictions.
- The future in Romans 5:19 should not be erased or flattened into a present tense.
- The future is compatible with an eschatological completion of the righteous condition secured through Christ.
- The future may also be understood as the continuing application of Christ's work to people as they come to faith; this interpretation is represented in historical commentaries.
- A certainty-emphasizing or gnomic reading is proposed by some interpreters, but is not established by morphology alone.
- Romans 6 and 1 Corinthians 15 show that Paul's account includes both present life in Christ and future resurrection.

### 11.3 Not established by the future form alone

- That Romans 5:19 refers exclusively to the final judgment.
- That it refers exclusively to the moment an individual first believes.
- That it means righteousness has no present reality.
- That it means all aspects of salvation are already complete in present experience.
- That “made righteous” is simply identical to bodily resurrection.
- That the future tense by itself supplies a detailed chronology of the end.
- That all interpreters agree on one explanation of the future.

## 14. Limitations and counter-evidence

- A future passive indicative establishes grammatical form, not a complete eschatological timetable.
- Commentators differ on whether Romans 5:19's future is primarily eschatological, distributive, or certainty-emphasizing.
- Romans 5:9–10 clearly establishes the coexistence of present justification/reconciliation and future salvation, but does not mechanically determine the exact nuance of the future in verse 19.
- Romans 6 and 1 Corinthians 15 provide canonical context, but they do not use the same verb as Romans 5:19.
- The resurrection context strongly supports future completion in Paul's theology; it does not make “made righteous” and “made alive” interchangeable.
- The scope of “many” in Romans 5:19 remains unresolved from Study 0027.
- This study does not decide the complete relation among justification, sanctification, glorification, and participation in Christ.
- The commentaries consulted represent differing interpretive traditions and should not be treated as a complete survey of modern Pauline scholarship.
