## 1. Question

How does 2 Corinthians 4:7–14 connect the life of Jesus being manifested in mortal flesh with God's future raising of believers?

The investigation asks:

1. What is the “treasure” in verse 7, and what does the earthen vessel image describe?
2. Why does Paul place divine power and human weakness in direct contrast?
3. What do the hardship contrasts in verses 8–9 establish—and what do they not establish?
4. What does Paul mean by carrying about “the dying of the Lord Jesus” in the body?
5. How is the life of Jesus manifested in mortal flesh in the present?
6. What does “death worketh in us, but life in you” mean in the context of Paul's ministry?
7. How does the quotation from Psalm 116:10 (Psalm 115:1 in the Septuagint numbering) relate faith, speaking, suffering, and witness?
8. What is the force of Paul's statement that the God who raised Jesus will also raise “us”?
9. How should present manifestation of Christ's life be distinguished from future bodily resurrection?
10. What can be established from the text, what is interpretation, and what remains unresolved?

## 5. Context — from the glory of God in Christ to the earthen vessels

Second Corinthians 4:7 does not begin a detached meditation on human weakness. Paul has just described the ministry of the new covenant and the glory of God revealed in the face of Jesus Christ.

In 2 Corinthians 3:18, Paul describes believers as being changed into the same image from glory to glory, “even as by the Spirit of the Lord.” In 4:4, he refers to “the light of the glorious gospel of Christ,” and in 4:6 he describes God shining in hearts “to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.”

Verse 7 begins with “But.” The contrast is between the treasure and the earthen vessels that carry it. The glory of the message does not mean that its human ministers appear powerful, invulnerable, or impressive.

This immediate context matters because it limits what the treasure may mean. It is connected to the preceding description of the gospel, the knowledge of God's glory in Christ, and the ministry through which that message is proclaimed.

## 6. 2 Corinthians 4:7 — treasure in earthen vessels

KJV:

> “But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us.”

### 6.1 The treasure

The verse does not give a formal definition of “this treasure.” The nearest context in 4:4–6 concerns the gospel of Christ and the knowledge of God's glory in the face of Jesus Christ. The wider discussion also concerns Paul's ministry (4:1).

Three related emphases appear in the scholarship examined:

- the gospel entrusted to and proclaimed by Paul;
- the ministry through which the gospel is conveyed;
- the knowledge of God's glory in Christ, revealed through the gospel.

Collins identifies the treasure with Paul's important ministry and all that it entails. Blackwell considers the knowledge of God's glory in Christ (4:6) the most likely referent because of its proximity. Bible.org similarly emphasizes the gospel as the treasure.

These emphases are related, but they should not be falsely presented as if the verse itself supplies a single technical definition. The secure observation is that the treasure is precious and God-given, while the human bearer is fragile.

### 6.2 The earthen vessel

The Greek phrase is ὀστρακίνοις σκεύεσιν (*ostrakinois skeuesin*), commonly rendered “earthen vessels,” “clay jars,” or “earthenware vessels.”

The image points to a container made of ordinary, breakable material. Plummer identifies the vessel with the frailty of the human body. Collins likewise emphasizes fragility and weakness.

The contrast is not between a worthless creation and a valuable spirit. It is between the extraordinary treasure and the weakness of the human instrument carrying it.

Plummer explicitly cautions that the metaphor does not require the idea that material existence or the human body is inherently evil. The text's point is the weakness of the vessel in contrast with the divine source of the power.

### 6.3 The purpose clause

Paul states the purpose:

> “that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us.”

The KJV's “excellency” renders the sense of surpassing greatness or extraordinary character. The point is attribution: the power belongs to God, not to the human minister.

This is not merely an admission that people have limitations. It is Paul's explanation for why the treasure is carried in fragile vessels: the result should direct recognition away from the human bearer and toward God.

### Bounded finding

The vessel does not originate the treasure or the power. It bears and conveys what it did not originate. The text does not say that the vessel is the source of the treasure.

## 7. 2 Corinthians 4:8–9 — afflicted, but not crushed

KJV:

> “We are troubled on every side, yet not distressed; we are perplexed, but not in despair;
>
> Persecuted, but not forsaken; cast down, but not destroyed;”

Paul uses paired contrasts. Each first phrase names real pressure or danger. The second denies that the hardship has brought about the final defeat described by the opposing term.

Collins describes the list as a series of contrasting hardship statements and notes the repeated emphasis in the words “but not.” He reads the passive forms as suggesting God's sustaining agency. Plummer likewise sees the hardship descriptions as evidence that weakness does not invalidate Paul's ministry.

### 7.1 What the contrasts say

- Affliction is real.
- Confusion or perplexity is real.
- Persecution is real.
- Being struck down is real.
- Yet Paul has not been finally crushed, abandoned, or destroyed.

The text does not portray Paul as untouched by suffering. The first half of each contrast is essential to the statement.

### 7.2 What the contrasts do not say

They do not establish:

- that believers will never be injured;
- that faithful witnesses will never be imprisoned, beaten, or killed;
- that every crisis will end in immediate earthly rescue;
- that psychological distress is evidence of unbelief;
- that a person who dies has been “destroyed” in the ultimate sense Paul is discussing;
- that a Christian ministry can be evaluated by outward success or physical safety.

Paul elsewhere describes an experience in Asia in which he was burdened beyond strength and despaired even of life (2 Corinthians 1:8–10). That context prevents the hardship contrasts in chapter 4 from being turned into a simplistic promise of emotional or physical invulnerability.

### Bounded finding

The contrast is not “the faithful do not suffer.” It is “suffering does not demonstrate that God's power or purpose has failed.”

## 8. 2 Corinthians 4:10–11 — carrying the dying of Jesus and manifesting His life

KJV:

> “Always bearing about in the body the dying of the Lord Jesus, that the life also of Jesus might be made manifest in our body.
>
> For we which live are alway delivered unto death for Jesus' sake, that the life also of Jesus might be made manifest in our mortal flesh.”

### 8.1 “The dying of the Lord Jesus”

The Greek noun is νέκρωσις (*nekrōsis*), a rare term referring to the process or experience of being put to death or dying.

Paul describes his apostolic suffering as a bodily participation in the pattern of Jesus' suffering. Collins emphasizes that Paul is speaking about the ongoing experience or process of dying, not simply repeating a statement about the historical death of Jesus.

Plummer likewise understands the word as describing a process leading toward death rather than the completed end of that process.

The phrase does not mean that Paul's suffering adds to Christ's atoning work or repeats the sacrifice of the cross. Christ's death is the source and pattern; Paul's suffering is the experience of a servant who bears witness to Christ.

### 8.2 “That the life also of Jesus might be made manifest”

The purpose clause is important. Paul's bodily suffering is the context in which the life of Jesus becomes visible.

The text does not say that Paul becomes the source of Jesus' life. It says that the life of Jesus is manifested in Paul's body.

The word “manifest” translates φανερόω (*phaneroō*): to make visible, reveal, or bring into manifestation. The immediate emphasis is present. The life of Jesus is being made manifest in the body while the apostle experiences suffering.

### 8.3 “Our mortal flesh”

Verse 11 repeats and clarifies the bodily language:

> “that the life also of Jesus might be made manifest in our mortal flesh.”

Paul does not describe a life that bypasses mortal existence. The life of Jesus is manifested in mortal flesh.

The present manifestation does not mean that the body has already become immortal. The adjective “mortal” preserves the reality of human vulnerability and death.

### 8.4 Present manifestation is not future resurrection

Verses 10–11 speak of the present manifestation of Jesus' life amid apostolic suffering. Verse 14 will speak of God's future raising of believers.

The two are connected, but they are not identical:

- **Present:** Jesus' life is manifested in mortal bodies through the apostolic witness.
- **Future:** God will raise those who belong to Christ.

This is the central distinction of the study.

## 9. 2 Corinthians 4:12 — death in the ministers, life in the hearers

KJV:

> “So then death worketh in us, but life in you.”

Paul turns from the experience of the apostolic ministers to the effect upon the Corinthian community.

The “us” in this immediate section refers first to Paul and his fellow gospel ministers. Collins observes that the argument also has relevance to the believers addressed, but its first focus is apostolic ministry.

Paul's suffering in the service of the gospel is related to the Corinthians receiving and experiencing the life of Christ through that ministry.

This does not mean that death itself is a source of spiritual life. Nor does it mean that Paul is the author of the Corinthians' life. The surrounding passage has already said that the surpassing power belongs to God, not to the ministers.

The relation is ministerial and derivative:

- Paul bears suffering in the service of Christ.
- The gospel is proclaimed.
- The Corinthians receive the benefit of that ministry.
- The life of Christ is at work among them.
- The power and glory remain God's.

### Bounded finding

Paul describes the cost borne by the messenger and the life received by the hearers. The messenger is not the originating source of the life being conveyed.

## 10. 2 Corinthians 4:13 — faith speaks

KJV:

> “We having the same spirit of faith, according as it is written, I believed, and therefore have I spoken; we also believe, and therefore speak;”

Paul quotes Psalm 116:10 in the KJV's numbering. The verse appears as Psalm 115:1 in the Septuagint numbering used in the Greek biblical tradition.

The Psalm describes a speaker who trusts God and speaks from that trust. Paul identifies with this pattern: “we also believe, and therefore speak.”

The sequence in Paul's argument is:

1. The apostolic ministers endure suffering.
2. They have faith in God.
3. Their faith leads them to continue speaking.
4. Their speaking serves the proclamation of the gospel.
5. The proclamation leads to thanksgiving and God's glory (4:15).

The “spirit of faith” in verse 13 should not be casually treated as a separate technical definition of the Holy Spirit. In this immediate context, Paul speaks of the disposition or spirit of trusting faith expressed in speech. Collins interprets the phrase as trust in God and notes the connection with the Psalmist's trust.

### Bounded finding

Faith does not originate the resurrection promise. Faith trusts and speaks in response to what God has done and promised.

## 11. 2 Corinthians 4:14 — the God who raised Jesus will raise us

KJV:

> “Knowing that he which raised up the Lord Jesus shall raise up us also by Jesus, and shall present us with you.”

This verse moves explicitly to future resurrection.

### 11.1 God is the acting subject

Paul identifies God as the One who raised Jesus. The same God “shall raise up us also.”

The resurrection of Jesus is not presented as an isolated event. It is the basis for the future resurrection of those who belong to Him.

Collins emphasizes that Paul understands God to be the agent of Jesus' resurrection and that Paul's confidence in the resurrection of Jesus includes confidence that God will raise the believers as well.

### 11.2 “Us also”

The “us” includes Paul and his fellow ministers. The phrase “and shall present us with you” explicitly includes the Corinthian believers in the shared future hope.

The apostolic messenger and the community who receive the gospel are not assigned separate resurrection destinies in this sentence. Paul expects to be raised and presented with them.

### 11.3 “By Jesus”

The KJV renders the phrase “by Jesus.” The resurrection hope is inseparable from Jesus Christ. The wording should not be expanded into a detailed mechanism beyond Paul's statement.

### 11.4 The relation to Romans 8:11

Romans 8:11 and 2 Corinthians 4:14 share a clear pattern:

- God raised Jesus.
- God will give life to or raise those who belong to Christ.

Romans 8:11 specifically speaks of quickening mortal bodies in relation to the indwelling Spirit. Second Corinthians 4:14 speaks of God's raising of the apostolic ministers and presenting them with the Corinthians.

The passages reinforce the same resurrection hope while retaining their different immediate emphases.

## 12. The sequence in 2 Corinthians 4:7–14

The passage can be summarized without turning its literary movement into an additional GUA-W architecture:

| Verses | What Paul states | Temporal emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| 4:7 | Treasure is carried in earthen vessels so the power is recognized as God's. | Present ministry |
| 4:8–9 | Real hardship is endured without final destruction. | Present suffering |
| 4:10–11 | The dying of Jesus is borne in the body; His life is manifested in mortal flesh. | Present manifestation |
| 4:12 | Death is at work in the ministers; life in the hearers. | Present ministry and reception |
| 4:13 | Faith believes and therefore speaks. | Present witness |
| 4:14 | God who raised Jesus will also raise the ministers and present them with the believers. | Future resurrection |

The sequence is not a claim that human suffering produces resurrection by its own power. God's action remains primary.

## 13. Relation to 2 Corinthians 4:15–18 and 5:1–5

Although the central scope is verses 7–14, the next verses clarify the direction of Paul's argument.

### 13.1 Thanksgiving and God's glory — 4:15

Paul says that all things are for the Corinthians' sake, so that grace may abound through many and thanksgiving may redound to God's glory.

The ministry's outcome is not the elevation of the messenger. It is the multiplication of thanksgiving to God.

### 13.2 Daily inward renewal — 4:16

Paul says that although the outward person is perishing, the inward person is renewed day by day.

Plummer distinguishes the body's ongoing decay from the inward person's ongoing renewal. This is a present process, not the completed resurrection of the body.

### 13.3 Present affliction and future glory — 4:17–18

Paul contrasts temporary affliction with an eternal weight of glory. Plummer explicitly warns against treating the phrase “worketh for us” as a payment earned by suffering. The text does not say that affliction purchases glory as a wage.

Paul's focus is on the future glory that relativizes present affliction, not on denying that the affliction is severe.

### 13.4 The earthly house and the building from God — 5:1–5

Paul continues with the image of an earthly house or tent and a building from God, eternal in the heavens. He speaks of longing to be clothed rather than found naked, and of mortality being swallowed up of life.

He identifies God as the One who has prepared believers for this and has given the earnest of the Spirit.

This continues the distinction already observed:

- the present embodied condition is vulnerable and mortal;
- the future hope is life with God;
- the Spirit is given now as earnest;
- God is the One who prepares and brings about the promised completion.

The imagery in 5:1–5 is metaphorical and has generated debate about the intermediate state and the precise meaning of being “clothed.” Collins cautions that Paul's language about future life is figurative and not a technical description of a physical mechanism. This study does not settle every question raised by the passage.

## 14. Findings: what the evidence supports

### 14.1 Directly supported by the biblical text

- The treasure is carried in earthen vessels (2 Corinthians 4:7).
- The purpose is that the surpassing power be recognized as God's and not from the ministers (4:7).
- Paul describes real affliction, perplexity, persecution, and being struck down (4:8–9).
- He describes carrying the dying of Jesus in the body (4:10).
- The life of Jesus is to be manifested in mortal flesh (4:10–11).
- Death is at work in the apostolic ministers, while life is at work in the Corinthians (4:12).
- Paul believes and therefore speaks, using the language of Psalm 116:10 (4:13).
- God raised Jesus and will also raise Paul and the believers, presenting them together (4:14).
- The purpose extends to grace, thanksgiving, and God's glory (4:15).

### 14.2 Supported interpretations

- The earthen vessel image refers to the fragility and weakness of human ministers and their embodied existence.
- The treasure is closely connected with the gospel, the knowledge of God's glory in Christ, and the ministry that conveys it.
- The hardship contrasts show that suffering does not establish the failure of God's power or purpose.
- “The dying of Jesus” describes Paul's ongoing participation in the pattern of Christ's suffering, not a repetition of Christ's atoning death.
- The manifestation of Jesus' life in mortal flesh is a present reality in Paul's ministry.
- The raising in verse 14 is future bodily resurrection.
- The passage connects present manifestation and future resurrection without making them the same event.

### 14.3 Contested or unresolved

- The precise referent of “this treasure” in verse 7: gospel, knowledge of God's glory in Christ, apostolic ministry, or a closely related combination.
- The precise scope of “we” in each verse: Paul and his fellow ministers in the immediate context, with implications that extend to the community of believers.
- The extent to which Paul's experience is intended as a paradigm for all Christians, rather than a description first of apostolic ministry.
- The detailed meaning of the metaphorical clothing and dwelling language in 2 Corinthians 5:1–5.
- The exact relation between the present inward renewal and the future bodily transformation.

### 14.4 Not established by the examined material

- That believers will never be crushed, injured, persecuted, or killed in the ordinary physical sense.
- That suffering itself is an atoning act.
- That the human body is intrinsically evil because Paul calls it an earthen vessel.
- That the present manifestation of Jesus' life is identical to future bodily resurrection.
- That faith is the originating cause of resurrection.
- That a scientific mechanism for resurrection is described in the passage.
- That the metaphor of the earthen vessel defines a new GUA-W component.

## 17. Limitations and counter-evidence

- “Treasure” is not given a single explicit definition in verse 7. The study records several closely related scholarly interpretations.
- The immediate “we” primarily refers to Paul and his fellow ministers, but the passage's implications extend beyond them. The precise scope should not be flattened.
- The hardship contrasts do not mean that Paul escaped every earthly consequence of suffering. Second Corinthians 1:8–10 provides an important control against that interpretation.
- The meaning of “the dying of Jesus” is debated in detail. The study follows the reading of ongoing participation in Christ's suffering while refusing to treat it as a second atonement.
- Collins notes the figurative and sometimes mixed character of Paul's language about future life in 2 Corinthians 4:7–5:10. The metaphors should not be forced into a technical physical description.
- Plummer is an older commentary; it is used as historical exegetical comparison, not as the sole basis for the findings.
- Blackwell's work is a doctoral thesis and advances a particular theological framework. Its observations are considered, but its terminology is not adopted as a GUA-W definition.
- No scientific mechanism or scientific confirmation of resurrection is claimed.
