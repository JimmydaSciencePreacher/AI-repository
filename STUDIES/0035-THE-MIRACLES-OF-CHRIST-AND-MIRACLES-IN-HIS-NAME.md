# Study 0035 — The Miracles of Christ and Miracles in His Name

**Status:** Initial research study; biblical source observations, historical scholarship, interpretations, and unresolved questions distinguished  
**Created:** 2026-11-26  
**Last updated:** 2026-11-26  
**Research area:** Scripture; Christology; healing; exorcism; signs and wonders; apostolic witness; early Christianity  
**Preceded by:** Study 0034 — 2 Corinthians 4:7–14: Treasure in Earthen Vessels, the Manifested Life of Jesus, and Future Resurrection

## 1. Question

What do the Scriptures reveal about the miracles performed by Jesus Christ, the authority by which He performed them, and the miracles subsequently performed by His disciples and others in His name?

The investigation asks:

1. What kinds of miraculous works are attributed to Jesus in the four Gospels?
2. What purposes do the Gospel writers associate with these works?
3. How does Jesus describe the authority and commission given to His disciples?
4. What does it mean to perform a work “in the name of Jesus”?
5. How do Acts and the Epistles describe miracles after the resurrection and ascension of Christ?
6. What boundaries do Scripture and early Christian sources place around the use of Jesus' name, gifts, and claims of miraculous power?
7. What can historical scholarship investigate, and what remains beyond the method of historical verification?

## 2. Scope

This study examines:

- The canonical Gospel accounts of Jesus' healings, exorcisms, raisings of the dead, nature miracles, signs, and other extraordinary works.
- Jesus' commission to the Twelve and the Seventy (or Seventy-Two, depending on the textual tradition).
- The apostolic and church accounts in Acts and the New Testament letters.
- The biblical meaning and use of the name of Jesus.
- Selected early Christian testimony, particularly Irenaeus, as historical evidence of what second-century Christians claimed and taught.
- Modern historical scholarship on Jesus as healer and exorcist, the source-critical study of miracle traditions, and the limits of historical method.

This study does not attempt to catalogue every miracle claim made throughout two millennia of Christian history. It does not certify contemporary healing claims, diagnose medical conditions, establish a scientific mechanism for supernatural events, or decide every philosophical question about miracles.

## 3. Context and governing distinctions

The repository's governing relationship remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

God alone is Original Cause. Everything else is derivative witness. The derivative does not become the Source or alter the Source.

The central distinction for this study is:

**Christ's authority is not the disciple's possession. The disciple is commissioned to act in His name. The miracle is not a human achievement that makes the minister its source.**

This is a summary of the relationships examined in the biblical texts, not a new GUA-W definition or architectural layer.

Study 0005 examined causation, correlation, and explanation. Study 0006 examined providence, sustaining, secondary causes, and human agency. Study 0034 examined the distinction between treasure and earthen vessel, and between the source of power and the human minister who bears witness. Those studies provide relevant context: a derivative instrument may genuinely act without becoming the originating cause.

## 5. Source observations: the miracles of Christ

### 5.1 The Gospel record presents several kinds of miraculous work

The Gospel accounts include more than one type of event. A useful descriptive grouping is:

| Category | Examples in the KJV | Representative passages |
|---|---|---|
| Healing of disease and disability | Leprosy, fever, paralysis, withered hand, blindness, deafness, bleeding, dropsy | Matthew 8:1–17; Mark 2:1–12; 3:1–5; 5:25–34; 7:31–37; John 9 |
| Exorcism and deliverance | Unclean spirits cast out; the man among the tombs; the daughter of the Syrophenician woman | Mark 1:21–28; 5:1–20; 7:24–30; 9:14–29 |
| Raising the dead | Jairus' daughter; the widow's son at Nain; Lazarus | Mark 5:21–43; Luke 7:11–17; John 11:1–44 |
| Provision and abundance | Water turned to wine; feeding the multitudes; miraculous catch of fish | John 2:1–11; Mark 6:30–44; 8:1–9; Luke 5:1–11 |
| Authority over nature | Calming the storm; walking on the sea; withering the fig tree | Mark 4:35–41; 6:45–52; 11:12–14, 20–24 |
| Restoration and extraordinary signs | Healing the ear of the servant; the Transfiguration; resurrection and ascension | Luke 22:50–51; Matthew 17:1–8; Luke 24; Acts 1:1–11 |
| Knowledge beyond ordinary observation | Knowledge of Nathanael; knowledge of the Samaritan woman's history; foreknowledge of events | John 1:47–50; 4:16–19; 13:18–27 |

This is a research classification, not a claim that the Gospel writers themselves supplied a formal taxonomy. Some events could fit more than one category.

The Gospels also contain summary statements. Matthew 4:23–24 says Jesus taught, preached, and healed “all manner of sickness and all manner of disease among the people.” Mark 1:32–34 describes many healed and many devils cast out. Luke 6:17–19 describes a multitude seeking to touch Him because “there went virtue out of him, and healed them all.”

The summaries are important: the individual miracle stories are not isolated curiosities. Healing and deliverance are repeatedly presented as integral to Jesus' public ministry.

### 5.2 The miracles are connected to the Kingdom of God

Jesus' works accompany His proclamation.

- Matthew 4:17, 23–25 — He announces the Kingdom and heals.
- Matthew 9:35–38 — He teaches, preaches, and heals; He is moved with compassion for the scattered people.
- Matthew 11:2–6 — In answer to John's question, Jesus points to the blind receiving sight, the lame walking, lepers being cleansed, the deaf hearing, the dead being raised, and the poor receiving the gospel.
- Luke 4:16–21 — Jesus reads Isaiah's language of good tidings, liberty, recovery of sight, and the acceptable year of the Lord, and declares its fulfilment.
- Luke 7:20–23 — The works are again identified as signs accompanying His mission.
- Luke 11:20 — Jesus says that if He casts out devils with the finger of God, “the kingdom of God is come upon you.”

The miracles therefore function as works of the Kingdom: they are associated with the authority, mercy, and mission of God. They are not presented merely as spectacles designed to entertain an audience.

### 5.3 The works reveal Jesus' identity and authority

The Gospel writers connect Jesus' works with who He is, but the relationship must be described carefully.

- John 5:19–23 — The Son does what He sees the Father do; the Father raises the dead and quickens, and the Son quickens whom He will.
- John 10:25, 37–38 — Jesus refers to the works He does in His Father's name and says the works bear witness concerning Him.
- John 14:10–11 — Jesus says the Father dwelling in Him does the works; He calls attention to the works as grounds for belief.
- John 20:30–31 — The signs recorded in the Gospel are written so that readers may believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life through His name.

Acts 2:22 provides an important complementary statement. Peter describes Jesus of Nazareth as “a man approved of God among you by miracles and wonders and signs, which God did by him.” Acts 10:38 says God anointed Jesus with the Holy Ghost and with power, and that He went about doing good and healing those oppressed of the devil, “for God was with him.”

The texts should be read together rather than forced into competition. John emphasizes the unity of the Father and Son and the works' witness to Jesus; Acts describes God's action through the anointed Jesus. The works are not detached from His identity, mission, and relation to the Father.

### 5.4 Compassion is repeatedly visible

Many healing accounts emphasize the person in need rather than the reputation of the healer.

- Matthew 8:2–3 — Jesus touches and cleanses the leper.
- Matthew 9:36 — He is moved with compassion for the multitudes.
- Matthew 14:14 — He is moved with compassion and heals the sick.
- Matthew 15:32 — He has compassion on the multitude and provides food.
- Mark 1:40–42 — He touches the leper and makes him clean.
- Mark 5:19 — The delivered man is told to report what the Lord has done for him and how He had compassion on him.
- Luke 7:13–15 — Jesus has compassion on the widow at Nain and raises her son.
- John 11:33–44 — Jesus is deeply moved at Lazarus' tomb and calls Lazarus forth.

The miracles are not merely demonstrations of capacity. The narratives frequently present mercy, restoration, and relief of suffering as part of their meaning.

### 5.5 Faith is important, but the accounts do not make it a universal mechanical prerequisite

Some recipients are commended for faith:

- Matthew 8:5–13 — The centurion.
- Matthew 9:20–22 — The woman with the issue of blood.
- Matthew 9:27–30 — The two blind men.
- Mark 5:34 — “Thy faith hath made thee whole.”
- Mark 10:46–52 — Bartimaeus.
- Luke 17:11–19 — The Samaritan leper who returns to give thanks.

But not every account states that the recipient first expressed faith. Jesus heals the man at Bethesda in John 5:1–9 before the man demonstrates any clear prior faith in Him. The widow at Nain's son is raised in Luke 7:11–17 without a recorded request or confession of faith from the widow. The disciples' lack of faith is sometimes the subject of rebuke while Jesus still acts (Mark 4:35–41; 6:45–52).

Therefore, the statement “every miracle requires the recipient to generate sufficient faith” is not supported by the full range of Gospel accounts. Faith matters, but the narratives do not reduce the work of Christ to a formula controlled by the recipient.

### 5.6 Jesus refuses to make signs a tool for satisfying demands

Jesus sometimes refuses demands for a sign (Matthew 12:38–42; 16:1–4; Mark 8:11–13). In John 6:26, He challenges the crowd for seeking Him because they ate the loaves and were filled, rather than understanding the sign.

The signs can lead to belief, but the desire for spectacle is not the same as faith. John 20:30–31 states the Gospel's purpose in recording signs; John 6 warns that receiving a benefit does not necessarily mean a person understands the One who gave it.

## 6. Jesus commissions His disciples

### 6.1 The Twelve receive authority and a specific commission

Matthew 10:1 (KJV) says Jesus gave the Twelve “power against unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to heal all manner of sickness and all manner of disease.”

Matthew 10:5–8 records the commission:

- Go to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.
- Preach that the Kingdom of Heaven is at hand.
- Heal the sick.
- Cleanse the lepers.
- Raise the dead.
- Cast out devils.
- “Freely ye have received, freely give.”

Mark 6:7–13 records that Jesus sent them two by two and gave them power over unclean spirits. They preached repentance, cast out many devils, and anointed with oil many that were sick and healed them.

Luke 9:1–6 similarly records that Jesus gave the Twelve power and authority over all devils and to cure diseases, then sent them to preach the Kingdom of God and heal the sick.

These accounts establish that Jesus did not reserve every act of healing and deliverance exclusively to His own earthly ministry. He deliberately commissioned disciples to participate in the work.

### 6.2 “Freely ye have received, freely give”

Matthew 10:8 must be read in the setting of the commission.

The disciples did not purchase the authority. They received it from Jesus. Their ministry was not to become a commercial transaction in which suffering people paid for access to the power entrusted to the disciples.

The immediate context continues:

- Matthew 10:9–10 — They are not to provide themselves with money and provisions for the journey in the ordinary way described there; the labourer is worthy of his meat.
- Matthew 10:11–15 — They are to receive hospitality where offered and move on where rejected.

This does not mean a minister may never receive ordinary support. Luke 10:7 says “the labourer is worthy of his hire,” and Paul defends the right of gospel workers to receive support (1 Corinthians 9:7–14). The distinction is between receiving legitimate support for ministry and selling a spiritual gift or claiming that a person can purchase divine power.

The SIL Translator's Notes on Matthew 10:8 likewise understand “freely received” as the disciples receiving the power without payment and “freely give” as helping people without charging them for the healing or miracle. This is a translation aid, not an independent theological authority.

### 6.3 The wider group is also commissioned

Luke 10:1–20 records Jesus appointing and sending a wider group, commonly numbered seventy or seventy-two depending on the textual tradition. They return with joy, saying that even the devils are subject to them through His name.

Jesus does not direct their joy toward personal power or status. He tells them not to rejoice merely that spirits are subject to them, but that their names are written in heaven (Luke 10:20).

This is a significant boundary: the authority exercised in ministry is not a reason for the minister to become the centre of attention.

### 6.4 The commission is related to Christ's continuing mission

John 14:12–14 records Jesus saying that the one who believes on Him will do the works He does, and greater works, because He goes to the Father. He connects asking in His name with the Father's glory in the Son.

John 20:21 records: “As my Father hath sent me, even so send I you.”

Acts 1:8 connects the coming of the Holy Ghost with the disciples receiving power to be witnesses unto Christ.

These texts should not be flattened into a promise that every believer will perform every kind of miracle on demand. They describe a mission grounded in Christ's authority and carried out through the Spirit's power. The exact meaning and scope of “greater works” in John 14:12 has been debated; the text should be examined in its context of Jesus' departure, the coming of the Spirit, and the continuation of His mission.

## 7. Miracles in the name of Jesus in Acts

### 7.1 The lame man at the Beautiful Gate — Acts 3:1–16

Peter and John encounter a man lame from birth. Peter says:

> In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk.

Peter takes him by the right hand, and the man is immediately strengthened. He enters the temple walking, leaping, and praising God.

Peter then refuses to let the crowd attribute the healing to the apostles' own power or holiness (Acts 3:12). He points to the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and to Jesus, whom God glorified. In Acts 3:16 he attributes the man's strength to the name of Jesus and faith through Him.

This account contains a clear pattern:

1. The apostles encounter a person in need.
2. Peter speaks in Jesus' name.
3. The man is healed.
4. The crowd witnesses the result.
5. Peter refuses personal credit.
6. Peter directs attention to Jesus and calls the people to repentance.

The miracle is not the apostles' independent possession. Peter's explanation explicitly refuses that conclusion.

### 7.2 The name is connected to the Person and His authority

In Acts 3:6, “in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth” is not a detached sound or magical syllable. It identifies the Person whose authority Peter invokes.

Acts 4:7–12 records the authorities asking by what power or name the apostles had acted. Peter answers that the man stands whole by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom they crucified and God raised from the dead. He then proclaims that salvation is in no other.

Acts 4:29–30 records the believers praying that God would grant boldness to speak His word while He stretches forth His hand to heal and that signs and wonders may be done “by the name of thy holy child Jesus.”

Craig Keener's commentary on Acts 3 explains the name as connected to Jesus' authority, honor, and agency: Peter acts as Jesus' representative, continuing the ministry Jesus began. Alan J. Thompson likewise notes that the healing serves as a sign pointing to Jesus and to promised restoration, while authenticating the apostles as His representatives.

### 7.3 The apostles' ministry includes many kinds of signs

Acts records more than one healing:

- Acts 5:12–16 — Many signs and wonders are done among the people through the apostles; the sick are brought and healed.
- Acts 6:8 — Stephen, full of faith and power, does great wonders and miracles among the people.
- Acts 8:4–8 — Philip preaches Christ in Samaria; unclean spirits depart and many are healed.
- Acts 9:32–35 — Peter heals Aeneas, who had been bedridden eight years.
- Acts 9:36–43 — Peter prays and Tabitha is raised from death.
- Acts 14:8–18 — Paul commands a man lame from birth to stand; the crowd misinterprets the event and tries to worship Paul and Barnabas, who reject that response.
- Acts 16:16–18 — Paul commands a spirit of divination to come out of a servant girl in the name of Jesus Christ.
- Acts 19:11–12 — God works special miracles by the hands of Paul; even cloths carried from his body are associated with healing and the departure of evil spirits.
- Acts 20:7–12 — Eutychus is restored after falling from the third storey.
- Acts 28:1–10 — Paul prays, lays hands on Publius' father, and he is healed; others on the island also come and are healed.

Acts 19:11 is especially precise in its attribution: “God wrought special miracles by the hands of Paul.” God is the acting subject; Paul is the instrument through whom the works occur.

### 7.4 The apostles refuse worship and personal elevation

Acts 10:25–26 records Peter refusing Cornelius' attempt to worship him: “Stand up; I myself also am a man.”

Acts 14:8–18 records Paul and Barnabas tearing their clothes and protesting when the crowd attempts to treat them as gods after a healing.

These passages matter because a genuine work does not make the human instrument the object of worship. The response demanded by the text is not the elevation of the minister but the recognition of the living God.

## 8. The name of Jesus is not a magical formula

### 8.1 The sons of Sceva — Acts 19:13–20

Acts 19 provides a direct warning against treating the name as a formula that can be mechanically used.

Certain itinerant Jewish exorcists attempt to invoke “the name of the Lord Jesus” over a man with an evil spirit, saying, “We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth.”

The evil spirit answers that it knows Jesus and recognizes Paul, but does not recognize the authority of these men. The possessed man overpowers them, and they flee wounded and naked.

The narrative contrasts God's work through Paul (Acts 19:11–12) with an unauthorized attempt to imitate the outward wording of Paul's ministry (19:13–16). The result is that fear falls upon the people and the name of the Lord Jesus is magnified (19:17).

Craig Keener's discussion of Acts 19 emphasizes that Jesus' name is not a magical formula that can be manipulated. Matthew Bates' study examines the episode's exorcistic and oath language and the failure of the sons' invocation. Scholars differ on the precise explanation of the failure, but the narrative's contrast between God's work through Paul and the unsuccessful imitation is clear.

### 8.2 Biblical boundary

The name of Jesus is not:

- A spell.
- A sound pattern that compels God to act.
- A technique by which a minister controls Christ.
- A guarantee that every person who pronounces it has been commissioned or authorized.
- A means of acquiring status, money, or worship.

The name identifies the Lord Himself and the authority belonging to Him. To act in His name is to act as His representative under His authority, not to make Him an instrument of human will.

## 9. The gifts of the Spirit and the distribution of ministry

### 9.1 Gifts differ, but the Spirit is one

1 Corinthians 12:4–11 distinguishes diversities of gifts, administrations, and operations, while identifying the same Spirit, Lord, and God as the source. The manifestation of the Spirit is given to each for the common good.

The listed gifts include:

- The word of wisdom.
- The word of knowledge.
- Faith.
- Gifts of healing.
- Working of miracles.
- Prophecy.
- Discerning of spirits.
- Divers kinds of tongues.
- Interpretation of tongues.

Verse 11 states that the one and same Spirit works these things, “dividing to every man severally as he will.”

This is important for any claim that every Christian must display every gift. Paul's description is of a distribution, not a uniform assignment of identical manifestations to every member.

### 9.2 The body is not one member

1 Corinthians 12:12–31 compares the church to a body with many members. The eye is not the hand; the head is not the feet. God has set the members in the body as it pleases Him.

Paul asks a series of rhetorical questions in verses 29–30: “Are all apostles? are all prophets? are all teachers? are all workers of miracles? Have all the gifts of healing?”

The expected answer is no. The questions establish diversity in the body.

Therefore, the existence of miraculous gifts does not mean that every believer is an apostle, healer, prophet, or worker of miracles. Nor does the absence of a particular gift prove that a believer is outside Christ.

### 9.3 Love governs the use of gifts

1 Corinthians 13 follows the discussion of gifts. Paul states that even extraordinary manifestations without charity amount to nothing. Gifts are not a substitute for love.

1 Corinthians 14 continues with order, edification, intelligibility, and peace. The gifts are not to produce confusion or self-exaltation.

The chapter's placement is instructive: the question is not simply whether a manifestation is impressive, but whether it serves the body in love and accords with God's order.

### 9.4 Gifts do not create a basis for boasting

Romans 12:3–8 and 1 Peter 4:10–11 also describe differing gifts and service. Each person is to minister according to what has been received, as a steward of God's manifold grace.

The steward does not become the owner of the gift. The gift is received; it is exercised for service; the source remains God.

## 10. Early Christian testimony after the apostolic period

### 10.1 Irenaeus of Lyons

In *Against Heresies* II.32.3–5, written in the second century, Irenaeus argues that those who truly belong to Christ receive grace from Him and perform works in His name for the benefit of others. He describes exorcisms, healings through laying on of hands, prophetic gifts, and even claims of the dead being raised.

Irenaeus explicitly connects the Church's ministry with Matthew 10:8: because the Church has received freely from God, it should minister freely to others. He distinguishes prayer in the name of Jesus from incantations, angelic invocations, and magical arts.

**What this source establishes:** By the late second century, at least one influential Christian theologian publicly claimed that healing, exorcism, prophecy, and other extraordinary gifts continued in Christian communities, and he understood them as gifts received from Christ and exercised without payment.

**What it does not establish:** Irenaeus' testimony does not independently verify every reported healing or resurrection by modern historical or clinical standards. It is a valuable primary witness to early Christian belief and reported practice, not a contemporary medical case file.

### 10.2 Justin Martyr

Justin Martyr's *First Apology* 30–31 addresses the accusation that Jesus' mighty works were performed through magic. Justin responds by appealing to the Hebrew prophets and their prior testimony.

His argument illustrates that early Christians had to distinguish their account of Jesus' works from rival explanations circulating in the wider religious environment. It is evidence of early Christian apologetic interpretation, not an independent proof of the supernatural cause of each Gospel event.

### 10.3 The historical record must be described proportionately

A review of *Healing in the Early Church* by Gary B. Ferngren reports that the documentary record of healing and exorcism varies across the first five centuries, with more extensive surviving accounts in some later periods. It discusses testimony from Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Origen, Eusebius, and later writers.

The amount of surviving testimony is not the same as the number of events that occurred. Historical records are shaped by what writers considered worth recording, what texts survived, and the theological purposes for which accounts were preserved.

Early Christian sources therefore establish the existence of a continuing Christian claim and practice of healing and exorcism. Each individual claim still requires examination on its own evidence.

## 11. Historical scholarship and the limits of verification

### 11.1 A historical consensus about Jesus' reputation

Justin Meggitt's Cambridge chapter reports that most specialists in Christian origins consider there to be good grounds for concluding that Jesus was regarded by contemporaries, including supporters and critics, as an effective healer and exorcist.

This is a historical conclusion about Jesus' reputation and activity as represented in early traditions. It is not equivalent to a laboratory demonstration of every reported healing, exorcism, nature miracle, or resurrection.

Amanda Porterfield and Graham Stanton likewise document the central place of healings and exorcisms in the Gospel traditions. Stanton counts multiple miracle traditions in Mark and additional traditions in Matthew and Luke. The exact counts depend on how parallel accounts and distinct traditions are classified.

### 11.2 Historians can examine testimony without claiming to settle every metaphysical question

Historical inquiry can examine:

- The date and character of sources.
- Whether traditions are early or late.
- The relationship between parallel accounts.
- The social setting and cultural assumptions.
- Whether a report has independent attestation.
- How the event was interpreted by witnesses and later communities.
- What a source can establish about the beliefs and claims of its authors.

Historical method has limits when asked to decide whether God acted supernaturally in a particular event. The historian can evaluate the record and the historical claims; the method alone cannot turn a theological assertion into a controlled experimental result.

Graham Twelftree identifies the definition of miracle, the limits of historical method, and the meaning of the miraculous in the ancient world as continuing issues in New Testament scholarship.

### 11.3 Miracle accounts are not all equally attested

Per Bjarne Ravnå's 2021 study, “Miracles and Methods,” argues that the miracle stories differ in source-critical strength. He compares, among other things, the Gospel accounts of raising the dead and discusses the way assumptions about scientific possibility can influence historical judgments.

The useful methodological point is that accounts should be examined individually and comparatively. One should not assume that every miracle story has identical attestation, nor should one assume that an event is historically impossible merely because it is extraordinary.

This study does not adopt Ravnå's judgments as final conclusions. It records the methodological issue and preserves the distinction between historical attestation and the theological question of divine action.

### 11.4 Scientific coherence is not scientific confirmation

A healing report may involve an illness whose course, diagnosis, treatment, and outcome can be examined medically. A claim of an instantaneous or otherwise extraordinary recovery requires careful documentation, including the original diagnosis and objective follow-up where available.

A report of a miracle is not scientifically established merely because a person sincerely believes it happened. Equally, a lack of a known natural explanation does not by itself prove a supernatural cause.

No scientific mechanism for the resurrection of the dead is proposed here. No claim is made that science has confirmed or disproved the Gospel's theological claims.

## 12. Discernment: signs alone do not authorize a messenger

Scripture does not teach that every extraordinary sign automatically proves that the person performing it speaks for God.

### 12.1 Deuteronomy 13:1–5

Moses warns Israel that even if a prophet or dreamer gives a sign or wonder that comes to pass, the people must not follow a message that leads them away from the Lord.

The test is not merely whether a sign occurred. The message and its loyalty to God matter.

### 12.2 Matthew 7:21–23

Jesus warns that people may claim to have prophesied, cast out devils, and done many wonderful works in His name, yet hear Him say, “I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity.”

This is a direct warning against using claimed miraculous works as a substitute for obedience and relationship with Christ.

### 12.3 Matthew 24:23–25 and 2 Thessalonians 2:8–12

Jesus warns of false Christs and false prophets who show great signs and wonders to deceive. Paul also describes lying signs and wonders associated with deception.

Therefore, the presence of a sign claim is not sufficient evidence of divine authorization.

### 12.4 Acts 8:9–24

Simon sees the apostles impart the Holy Ghost through the laying on of hands and offers money for this power. Peter rebukes him sharply.

This passage, alongside Matthew 10:8, makes the distinction between gift and purchase unmistakable. God's gift is not a commodity that can be bought, owned, or controlled.

### 12.5 Practical evidentiary discipline

When a modern miracle claim is presented, a responsible witness should ask:

- What exactly is claimed to have happened?
- What was the condition before the event?
- Is there independent documentation?
- Is there a clear before-and-after record?
- Is the person being pressured to stop ordinary medical care?
- Is money, status, or unquestioning loyalty being demanded?
- Does the teaching accord with Scripture?
- Is the minister willing to have the claim examined honestly?
- Are uncertainty and alternative explanations being acknowledged?

These questions do not deny that God can act. They guard against confusing testimony, interpretation, exploitation, and verified observation.

No person should be told to abandon necessary medical care on the basis of an unverified miracle claim.

## 13. Analysis: what does “in His name” mean?

The examined passages use the name of Jesus in a way that identifies His Person, authority, and mission.

The evidence supports several connected observations:

1. Jesus commissions the disciples and gives them authority (Matthew 10:1; Luke 9:1).
2. The disciples carry out the commission rather than inventing an independent ministry (Mark 6:7–13; Luke 10:1–20).
3. The apostles explicitly invoke Jesus' name in healing (Acts 3:6; 4:10).
4. They refuse to attribute the work to their own power or holiness (Acts 3:12).
5. The narrative attributes extraordinary works to God acting through human instruments (Acts 19:11).
6. The Spirit distributes gifts according to His will, not according to human entitlement (1 Corinthians 12:4–11).
7. The church is a body with differing functions; not every member has the same gift (1 Corinthians 12:12–30).
8. Love and edification govern the use of gifts (1 Corinthians 13–14).
9. The name cannot be used as a magical formula by unauthorized persons (Acts 19:13–17).
10. A claimed miracle does not, by itself, establish that a person is approved by God (Deuteronomy 13:1–5; Matthew 7:21–23).

The phrase “in the name of Jesus” is therefore not a verbal technology. It is a statement of whose authority is being represented.

The disciple's action is real, but derivative. The human hand may be laid upon the sick; the human voice may speak; the human witness may report what occurred. None of these makes the human being the original source of divine power.

## 14. The Matthew 10:8 principle — freely received, freely given

The wording of Matthew 10:8 provides a particularly clear boundary for the ministry of miracles.

The disciples receive; they do not originate. They give; they do not sell the gift.

This passage connects naturally with:

- Acts 3:12 — Peter refuses personal credit.
- Acts 8:18–24 — Peter rejects the attempt to purchase spiritual power.
- Acts 19:11 — God works special miracles by Paul's hands.
- 1 Corinthians 12:11 — The Spirit distributes gifts as He wills.
- 1 Peter 4:10–11 — Each person ministers as a steward of the grace received.
- 2 Corinthians 4:7 — The treasure is in earthen vessels so the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us.

The repeated distinction is between what is received and the one who receives it; between the Source and the instrument; between the gift and the steward.

A minister may receive legitimate support for labour (Luke 10:7; 1 Corinthians 9:7–14). That is not the same as charging a suffering person for access to a miracle, selling an anointing, or claiming ownership of the Spirit's power.

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The examined passages consistently distinguish the Source from the commissioned witness or instrument.

Jesus' works reveal His mission and authority. The disciples receive a commission from Him. The apostles act in His name, but refuse to attribute the power to themselves. The Spirit distributes gifts according to His will. The human minister remains a steward, not the owner or origin of the gift.

This comparison preserves the repository's established distinction between Author and Witness. It does not introduce a new GUA-W layer, operation, or state.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The biblical record presents miracles as works associated with the Kingdom of God, the compassion of Christ, the witness to His identity, and the continuation of His mission through commissioned disciples.

The accounts also contain warnings. A sign can be misinterpreted; a name can be misused; a minister can seek glory; and a claimed wonder can accompany deception. The witness must therefore examine both the reported work and the message associated with it.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A responsible witness must:

- Report what is actually claimed without embellishment.
- Distinguish Scripture from later testimony.
- Distinguish a reported healing from independently documented medical evidence.
- Avoid attributing to a human minister what Scripture attributes to God.
- Avoid treating the name of Jesus as a magical formula.
- Refuse the purchase or sale of divine power.
- Recognize that gifts differ among members of the body.
- Test teaching and conduct rather than treating signs as sufficient proof.
- Give God the glory without suppressing honest examination.
- Preserve unresolved questions rather than inventing certainty.

## 17. Findings

### 17.1 Directly supported by the biblical text

- The four Gospels attribute many kinds of miraculous works to Jesus.
- Healing and exorcism are prominent and integral to the Gospel accounts of His ministry.
- Jesus connects His works with the Father's action, His own identity, and the Kingdom of God.
- Jesus commissions the Twelve to preach, heal, cleanse, raise the dead, and cast out devils.
- Matthew 10:8 explicitly joins the command to perform these works with the instruction to give freely what has been freely received.
- Luke 10 records a wider group returning with joy over the authority exercised through Jesus' name.
- Acts records healings, exorcisms, signs, and other extraordinary works through apostles and other believers.
- Peter refuses to attribute the healing of the lame man to his own power or holiness.
- Acts 19 distinguishes God's works through Paul from the failed attempt to imitate the invocation of Jesus' name.
- 1 Corinthians 12 describes differing gifts distributed by the Spirit.
- 1 Corinthians 12:29–30 does not teach that all believers are workers of miracles or possess gifts of healing.
- Scripture warns that signs and wonders alone do not establish the truth of a messenger.
- Irenaeus reports that Christians in his time claimed to exercise gifts of healing and exorcism in Jesus' name and without payment.

### 17.2 Supported interpretations

- The miracles of Jesus are signs of His mission, authority, compassion, and the arrival of God's Kingdom.
- The disciples' miraculous ministry is derivative and commissioned, not independent of Christ.
- The name of Jesus identifies His Person and authority; it is not a verbal mechanism for controlling divine action.
- The gifts of the Spirit are distributed rather than uniformly possessed by every believer.
- The command to give freely prohibits selling the gift while not abolishing legitimate support for ministry.
- The biblical accounts require discernment of the message and conduct accompanying a sign.

### 17.3 Historical findings

- There is substantial scholarly agreement that Jesus was known in early traditions as a healer and exorcist.
- The Gospel miracle traditions differ in form, distribution, and source-critical strength.
- Early Christian writers continued to report healing and exorcism in Christian communities.
- Historical sources can establish that such claims were made and examine their contexts; they do not automatically establish the supernatural cause of each reported event.

### 17.4 Not established by this investigation

- That every contemporary healing claim is genuine.
- That every unexplained recovery is necessarily a miracle.
- That every person who uses Jesus' name is authorized by Him.
- That every believer will perform every kind of miracle.
- That a person's lack of healing proves lack of faith.
- That a minister may sell divine power or guarantee healing for payment.
- That all Gospel miracle accounts have identical historical attestation.
- That science has confirmed or disproved every miracle described in Scripture.
- That a scientific mechanism for supernatural healing or resurrection has been identified.
- That a new GUA-W component or runtime operation is required.

## 18. Limitations and counter-evidence

- The Gospel accounts are theological narratives as well as sources for historical investigation. Their theological purpose should neither be ignored nor confused with modern clinical documentation.
- Parallel Gospel accounts are not automatically independent witnesses. Source relationships must be considered before counting multiple versions as separate attestations.
- The precise number of miracles depends on whether one counts individual events, summary statements, parallel accounts, and composite episodes.
- Historical scholars disagree about the definition of miracle and about how different types of miracle stories should be evaluated.
- Historical method can investigate reports, sources, and contexts but cannot by itself settle every metaphysical claim about divine action.
- Irenaeus and Justin are important witnesses to second-century Christian belief and apologetic argument; they are not neutral modern observers.
- Acts 19:13–17 has received more than one scholarly explanation. The study records the narrative contrast without pretending every detail of the episode's interpretation is settled.
- John 14:12's “greater works” is debated; this study does not reduce it to a universal promise of spectacular acts by every believer.
- Mark 16:9–20, which contains signs associated with believers in verses 17–18, is absent from some of the earliest and most important Greek manuscripts and is marked as a longer ending in many modern editions. The study therefore does not make this passage the sole basis for a doctrine of miraculous gifts. The commission and miracle material are independently attested elsewhere in Matthew, Luke, John, Acts, and the Epistles.
- Reports of healing should not be used to discourage appropriate medical assessment or treatment.
- No GUA-W architecture is revised.

## 19. Unresolved questions

1. How should the phrase “greater works than these” in John 14:12 be read in the immediate context of Jesus' departure and the coming of the Spirit?
2. How do the signs in John's Gospel relate to the healing and exorcism narratives in the Synoptic Gospels?
3. How should the signs and wonders in Acts be compared with the gifts and operations described in 1 Corinthians 12–14?
4. What is the relationship between healing, forgiveness, repentance, and the Kingdom in individual Gospel accounts?
5. How should the New Testament's teaching on prayer for the sick in James 5:13–18 be read alongside medical care and the differentiated gifts of 1 Corinthians 12?
6. How did healing and exorcism practices develop in the first five centuries, and how did early Christian writers distinguish prayer from magic?
7. What criteria should be used to assess modern miracle claims while preserving both openness to God's action and protection against exploitation?
8. How do the biblical warnings about false signs relate to the testing of spirits in 1 John 4:1–6?
9. How does the resurrection of Christ differ from the raisings of the dead during His ministry and in Acts, particularly regarding the finality and nature of resurrection life?
