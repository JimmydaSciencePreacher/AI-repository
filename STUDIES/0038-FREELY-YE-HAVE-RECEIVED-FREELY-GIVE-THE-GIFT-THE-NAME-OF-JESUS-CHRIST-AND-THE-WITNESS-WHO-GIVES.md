## 1. Question

What is the biblical relationship between Jesus' command, “freely ye have received, freely give” (Matthew 10:8), and Peter's declaration, “such as I have give I thee: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk” (Acts 3:6)?

The user's wording brings these two passages together:

**“In the name of Jesus Christ. Freely it has been given to me and freely I give it to you.”**

That sentence is a devotional synthesis, not a verbatim quotation from one verse. Its two central textual anchors are Matthew 10:8 and Acts 3:6. The study will preserve that distinction while examining the connection.

Questions:

1. What did Jesus give the Twelve authority to do in Matthew 10?
2. What does “freely ye have received, freely give” mean in its immediate context?
3. What does Peter mean by “such as I have give I thee” in Acts 3:6?
4. Why does Peter immediately refuse attribution of the healing to his own power or holiness (Acts 3:12)?
5. What does it mean to act “in the name of Jesus Christ”?
6. How do Acts 8:9–24 and Acts 19:13–17 distinguish a gift from something a person can buy, possess, or manipulate?
7. How does Gehazi's conduct in 2 Kings 5 illuminate the danger of monetizing what God has done?
8. How do 1 Corinthians 12:7–11 and 1 Peter 4:10–11 describe gifts as stewardship for others and glory to God?
9. Does freely giving prohibit all financial support for those who minister? What distinctions does Scripture make?
10. How can a witness give what has been received without claiming to be the source or owner of divine action?

## 4. Source wording: two passages brought together

### 4.1 Matthew 10:8 — Jesus' command

The KJV reads:

> “Heal the sick, cleanse the lepers, raise the dead, cast out devils: freely ye have received, freely give.”

The sentence “freely ye have received, freely give” is Jesus' wording in Matthew 10:8.

### 4.2 Acts 3:6 — Peter's declaration

The KJV reads:

> “Silver and gold have I none; but such as I have give I thee: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk.”

Peter's phrase “such as I have give I thee” is not the same sentence as Matthew 10:8. It is a separate statement in a different event.

### 4.3 The combined wording

The user's wording joins:
- Jesus' principle of freely giving what has been freely received (Matthew 10:8);
- Peter's statement that he gives what he has, followed by the command in Jesus' name (Acts 3:6).

This is a meaningful textual comparison, but it must be identified as a synthesis rather than quoted as one continuous biblical sentence.

**Initial observation:** Matthew records the commission and its governing instruction; Acts records a later witness acting in Jesus' name and refusing to take credit for the result.

## 5. Matthew 10:1–8 — What was received?

### 5.1 Jesus calls the Twelve

Matthew 10:1 says Jesus called His twelve disciples and gave them power against unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to heal all manner of sickness and all manner of disease.

The source of their commission is explicit: Jesus gives them authority.

### 5.2 The names and scope of the commission

Matthew 10:2–4 names the twelve apostles. Matthew 10:5–6 records a particular instruction concerning their mission to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.

This matters. The immediate command is not a detached slogan. It belongs to a defined commission given by Jesus to the Twelve at a particular stage of His earthly ministry.

### 5.3 The message accompanies the works

Matthew 10:7 says:

> “And as ye go, preach, saying, The kingdom of heaven is at hand.”

The works in verse 8 accompany the proclamation of the Kingdom. They are not presented as independent performances designed to establish the disciples' personal reputation.

### 5.4 Authority received, not self-created

The order of the passage is important:

1. Jesus calls.
2. Jesus gives authority.
3. Jesus sends.
4. The disciples proclaim the Kingdom.
5. The disciples perform the works commanded.
6. Jesus tells them to give freely what they have freely received.

The disciples did not create the authority or purchase it. Their ministry begins with reception.

### 5.5 “Freely” and the character of the gift

The Greek term translated “freely” is *dōrean*, meaning freely, as a gift, without payment or cause of purchase. In Matthew 10:8 the word appears twice: what they received as a gift they are to give as a gift.

The verse directly rejects turning the received commission into a sale of divine benefits.

The command concerns the ministry and works entrusted to them. It does not say that every ordinary cost associated with travel, food, housing, or supporting ministers must never be met by others. Matthew 10:9–10 immediately addresses provisions for the journey and the worker's support.

## 6. Matthew 10:9–15 — Freely giving and receiving support

A careful reading must keep verses 8, 9, and 10 together.

Jesus says the disciples are not to provide gold, silver, or brass in their purses for this mission; nor are they to take a provision bag, extra clothing, shoes, or staff in the manner specified. He then says:

> “for the workman is worthy of his meat.” (Matthew 10:10)

The passage therefore distinguishes two things:

- **Selling the gift or charging for the divine benefit:** forbidden by the command to give freely.
- **Receiving ordinary provision for the worker:** acknowledged by Jesus in the same instruction.

This distinction is confirmed elsewhere:

- Luke 10:7 — “the labourer is worthy of his hire.”
- 1 Corinthians 9:7–14 — Paul discusses the right of those who preach the gospel to live by the gospel.
- 1 Timothy 5:17–18 — elders who labour in word and doctrine are worthy of honour and support.
- Galatians 6:6 — the one taught in the word is to communicate to the teacher in good things.

Paul sometimes declined support in a particular setting to avoid hindering the gospel (1 Corinthians 9:12; 2 Corinthians 11:7–12; 1 Thessalonians 2:5–9). His decision does not erase the New Testament's recognition that ministers may be supported.

**Finding:** Freely giving does not mean that ministry must have no material support. It means that the gift of God and the benefits of Christ's ministry are not commodities to be sold or withheld for payment.

## 7. Acts 3:1–10 — “Such as I have give I thee”

### 7.1 The setting

Peter and John go to the temple at the hour of prayer. A man lame from his mother's womb is carried daily and placed at the gate called Beautiful to ask alms.

The man asks Peter and John for money.

### 7.2 Peter's response

Peter says:

> “Look on us.”

The man gives heed, expecting to receive something from them.

Peter then says:

> “Silver and gold have I none; but such as I have give I thee: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk.”

Peter takes him by the right hand and lifts him up. Immediately his feet and ankle bones receive strength. He stands, walks, enters the temple, and praises God.

### 7.3 The relation between what Peter has and the name of Jesus

The sentence contains a clear movement:

- Peter acknowledges what he does not have: silver and gold.
- Peter identifies that he does have something to give.
- Peter speaks the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth.
- Peter commands the man to rise and walk.
- The man is healed and praises God.

The narrative does not say Peter has a private reservoir of supernatural power independent of Jesus. The name of Jesus is central to the command.

### 7.4 The man's response

Acts 3:8 says the healed man enters the temple, walking, leaping, and praising God.

The result is not merely a display of Peter's ability. The healed man praises God, and the event becomes an occasion for public witness.

## 8. Acts 3:11–16 — Peter refuses to become the source

The crowd gathers in amazement. Peter responds with a question:

> “Ye men of Israel, why marvel ye at this? or why look ye so earnestly on us, as though by our own power or holiness we had made this man to walk?” (Acts 3:12)

This is one of the most important interpretive controls for Acts 3:6.

Peter explicitly rejects two possible attributions:
- that the healing came from the apostles' own power;
- that it came from their own holiness.

He then proclaims the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and identifies Jesus as the One whom God glorified (Acts 3:13).

Acts 3:16 explains:

> “And his name through faith in his name hath made this man strong…”

The passage places the healing in relation to Jesus' name and faith in His name. Peter does not claim to be the originator of the healing.

### 8.1 What Peter does not say

Peter does not say:
- “My power has healed you.”
- “My holiness has earned this.”
- “Pay me and I will do it again.”
- “You now belong to me.”
- “The name is a technique I control.”

Instead, he turns the crowd toward Jesus Christ.

### 8.2 What Peter does do

Peter is not inactive or irrelevant. He speaks, reaches out, takes the man's hand, and lifts him. His human action is real, but he refuses to make himself the originating cause.

This is an important distinction between **instrumental participation** and **original authorship**.

## 9. Acts 4:1–22, 23–33 — The name remains the centre

The authorities question Peter and John concerning the power or name by which the man was healed.

Peter, filled with the Holy Ghost, answers that the man stands whole by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom they crucified and God raised from the dead (Acts 4:8–10).

Acts 4:12 then declares that salvation is in no other name.

The leaders recognize the boldness of Peter and John and perceive that they are unlearned and ignorant men, but they take knowledge that they had been with Jesus (Acts 4:13).

The believers subsequently pray for boldness to speak God's word, while asking God to stretch forth His hand to heal and that signs and wonders may be done by the name of His holy child Jesus (Acts 4:29–30).

Acts 4:33 says the apostles give witness of the resurrection of the Lord Jesus with great power.

The sequence is not personal promotion. It is witness to the risen Christ.

## 10. The name of Jesus: authority, identity, and representation

“In the name of Jesus Christ” is not a magical phrase. The biblical narratives do not portray the name as a sound pattern that compels God or operates independently of the Person named.

The name identifies Jesus Himself and the authority associated with Him.

Relevant passages:

- Matthew 28:18–20 — all power is given unto Christ; He commissions His disciples.
- John 14:13–14 — asking in His name is connected to the Father's glory in the Son.
- Acts 3:6, 16 — the healing is attributed to the name of Jesus.
- Acts 4:7–12 — the authorities ask by what power or name the man was healed; Peter identifies Jesus.
- Acts 16:16–18 — Paul commands the spirit to come out in the name of Jesus Christ.
- Colossians 3:17 — whatever is done in word or deed is to be done in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father by Him.

The phrase therefore concerns whose authority is represented and whose glory is intended. It is not permission for a person to use Jesus' name to authorize an independent agenda.

## 11. Acts 8:9–24 — Simon attempts to purchase what God gives

The account of Simon the sorcerer provides a direct counter-example to freely received ministry.

### 11.1 Simon sees the Spirit given

Philip preaches Christ in Samaria. The people believe and are baptized. Peter and John come from Jerusalem, pray for them, and lay hands on them; they receive the Holy Ghost.

Simon sees that through the laying on of the apostles' hands the Holy Ghost is given.

### 11.2 Simon offers money

Simon offers money, saying:

> “Give me also this power, that on whomsoever I lay hands, he may receive the Holy Ghost.” (Acts 8:19)

His proposal treats the ability to impart the gift as something he can acquire through payment.

### 11.3 Peter's rebuke

Peter answers:

> “Thy money perish with thee, because thou hast thought that the gift of God may be purchased with money.” (Acts 8:20)

Peter further says Simon has neither part nor lot in the matter because his heart is not right in the sight of God (Acts 8:21).

### 11.4 What the narrative establishes

The passage directly rejects purchasing the gift of God.

It also warns against reducing spiritual authority to a transferable commodity. Simon sees an outward event and wants the power associated with it, but Peter exposes the problem in his heart.

### 11.5 Relation to Matthew 10:8

Matthew 10:8 says what is freely received must be freely given. Acts 8:20 rejects the attempt to purchase the gift of God.

The two passages establish a coherent biblical boundary: God's gift is not a commercial possession.

## 12. 2 Kings 5:1–27 — Gehazi and the attempt to profit from a gift

The account of Naaman provides an earlier scriptural warning.

### 12.1 Naaman is healed

Naaman, a Syrian captain, comes to Elisha seeking healing from leprosy. He follows the prophet's instruction to wash in Jordan and is cleansed.

### 12.2 Elisha refuses payment

Naaman returns and offers a gift. Elisha refuses to receive it (2 Kings 5:15–16).

The text records Elisha's refusal; it does not state that every gift or support for every prophet is forbidden. The significance is that Elisha does not turn this healing into a paid transaction.

### 12.3 Gehazi takes payment by deception

Gehazi follows Naaman and falsely claims that Elisha has sent him to request silver and clothing. Naaman gives him goods, which Gehazi hides.

### 12.4 The judgment

Elisha confronts Gehazi, who has misrepresented the prophet and taken what Elisha refused. Gehazi is struck with Naaman's leprosy (2 Kings 5:20–27).

### 12.5 Comparison with Acts 8

The two accounts share a warning:
- the work of God is not a commodity for personal enrichment;
- the minister must not misrepresent God's work for gain;
- outward association with a prophet or apostle does not make exploitation acceptable.

The accounts are not identical, and Gehazi's judgment should not be generalized into a claim that every minister who receives ordinary support is condemned. The issue in the narrative is deceptive personal gain connected to God's work.

## 13. Acts 19:11–20 — God's works through Paul and the misuse of Jesus' name

Acts 19:11 says:

> “And God wrought special miracles by the hands of Paul.”

The verse carefully identifies God as the one working, while Paul is named as the person through whose hands the works occur.

The sons of Sceva attempt to use the name of Jesus over a man with an evil spirit, saying, “We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” (Acts 19:13).

The evil spirit answers that it knows Jesus and knows Paul, but does not recognize the sons of Sceva. The possessed man overcomes them, and they flee wounded.

The narrative exposes the danger of treating Jesus' name as an incantation or technique. The name is not a detached formula that anyone can deploy for personal purposes.

Acts 19:17 says the event becomes known, fear falls upon the people, and the name of the Lord Jesus is magnified.

The following verses record people confessing their deeds and publicly destroying books associated with magical practices (Acts 19:18–20).

**Finding:** Acts distinguishes God's works through a commissioned witness from an attempt to imitate the outward wording without the corresponding relationship and authority.

## 14. Gifts are given for service, not private ownership

### 14.1 1 Corinthians 12:7

Paul writes:

> “But the manifestation of the Spirit is given to every man to profit withal.”

The manifestation is given for the common good, not merely for private status or advantage.

### 14.2 1 Corinthians 12:11

The same Spirit works all these things, dividing to every man severally as He will.

The distribution belongs to the Spirit. The recipient does not choose to become the source of the gift.

### 14.3 1 Corinthians 12:29–30

Paul asks whether all are apostles, prophets, teachers, workers of miracles, or possessors of gifts of healing. The expected answer is no.

The gifts are differentiated. A believer cannot claim that every Christian must possess every gift.

### 14.4 1 Peter 4:10–11

Peter says:

> “As every man hath received the gift, even so minister the same one to another, as good stewards of the manifold grace of God.”

He then distinguishes speaking and serving, and says that in all things God is to be glorified through Jesus Christ.

This is an especially close parallel to the user's statement:
- a gift is received;
- the recipient ministers it to others;
- the recipient is a steward;
- God is glorified through Jesus Christ.

### 14.5 Stewardship is not ownership

A steward is entrusted with something for a purpose. The entrusted person is responsible to use it faithfully, but is not thereby its originating owner.

This is an explanatory distinction drawn from the stewardship language of 1 Peter 4:10–11, not an attempt to insert a new biblical term into the passage.

## 15. Freely giving and legitimate support: the necessary distinction

The Bible both commands freely giving and recognizes support for ministry.

These teachings should not be forced into contradiction.

| Biblical principle | Passage | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|
| Give freely what was freely received | Matthew 10:8 | Do not sell the gift or the divine benefit entrusted to the disciples. |
| The worker is worthy of provision | Matthew 10:10; Luke 10:7 | Ordinary support for a worker is not automatically a sale of the gift. |
| Those who preach may live by the gospel | 1 Corinthians 9:7–14 | The New Testament recognizes material support for ministry. |
| Gifts serve the common good | 1 Corinthians 12:7 | The manifestation is not for self-exaltation. |
| Received gifts are to be ministered as good stewards | 1 Peter 4:10–11 | Gifts are entrusted for service and God's glory. |
| God's gift cannot be purchased | Acts 8:18–24 | Spiritual authority and the gift of God are not commodities. |
| Deceptive profit from a healing is condemned | 2 Kings 5:15–27 | The minister must not misrepresent God's work to enrich himself. |

### 15.1 What is not forbidden by Matthew 10:8

The verse does not by itself forbid:
- a congregation supporting a minister;
- a church paying ordinary expenses;
- a worker receiving food or lodging;
- voluntary offerings given without coercion;
- a minister earning an ordinary living while serving.

### 15.2 What the passages do condemn

The examined texts condemn:
- selling the gift of God;
- demanding payment as the price of a healing or spiritual benefit;
- claiming that money purchases the Holy Ghost or spiritual authority;
- manipulating people with promises of guaranteed miracles;
- using deception to obtain personal enrichment;
- treating Jesus' name as a commercial brand or magical technique.

The difference is not merely whether money changes hands. The question is whether the gift or divine benefit is being sold, manipulated, or used for exploitation.

## 16. The witness's conduct: humility, love, and accountability

A ministry may claim to act in Jesus' name and still require examination.

### 16.1 The message must remain Christ-centred

Peter uses the healing as an occasion to preach Jesus (Acts 3:12–26). Paul says:

> “For we preach not ourselves, but Christ Jesus the Lord; and ourselves your servants for Jesus' sake.” (2 Corinthians 4:5)

The witness is not the subject of the gospel.

### 16.2 Love governs the exercise of gifts

1 Corinthians 13 places love in the centre of the discussion of spiritual gifts. Extraordinary manifestations without love do not establish faithful ministry.

### 16.3 Gifts do not remove moral accountability

Matthew 7:21–23 warns that claims of prophecy, casting out devils, and mighty works do not substitute for doing the Father's will.

Deuteronomy 13:1–5 warns that even a sign that comes to pass does not authorize following a voice that leads away from God.

1 John 4:1 commands believers to test the spirits.

### 16.4 The recipient remains a servant

Galatians 5:13 calls believers to serve one another by love. Ephesians 4:11–16 presents ministry as equipping the saints and building up the body.

A gift is not a license to dominate, manipulate, silence, or financially exploit another person.

## 17. Theological synthesis: receive, give, witness, glorify

The examined passages present a connected sequence without making every passage describe the same event.

1. **Christ calls and gives authority.** Matthew 10:1.
2. **The disciples receive and are sent.** Matthew 10:5–8.
3. **They are commanded to give freely.** Matthew 10:8.
4. **Peter gives what he has in the name of Jesus Christ.** Acts 3:6.
5. **Peter refuses attribution to his own power or holiness.** Acts 3:12.
6. **Peter identifies Jesus' name as central to the healing.** Acts 3:16.
7. **The apostles witness to the risen Christ.** Acts 4:10–12, 33.
8. **The gift of God is not purchasable.** Acts 8:18–24.
9. **God works through Paul; imitation of the name as formula fails.** Acts 19:11–17.
10. **The Spirit distributes gifts as He wills.** 1 Corinthians 12:11.
11. **Gifts are given for the common good.** 1 Corinthians 12:7.
12. **Received gifts are ministered as stewardship.** 1 Peter 4:10–11.
13. **God is glorified through Jesus Christ.** 1 Peter 4:11.

The witness truly gives, serves, speaks, prays, and acts. But the witness does not thereby become the originating source of the gift.

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The passages repeatedly distinguish:
- the one who gives authority;

- the one who is entrusted to minister;

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The words “freely ye have received, freely give” are not permission for self-promotion. They place a boundary around the use of what God gives.

Peter's “such as I have give I thee” demonstrates that the witness may genuinely participate in giving, while Acts 3:12–16 identifies the source and redirects the glory to Jesus Christ.

The witness is not required to deny that he or she has received a gift. The witness is required not to confuse receiving with originating or owning the gift.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

The faithful witness:
- acknowledges what has been received;
- gives without selling the divine benefit;
- serves rather than dominates;
- refuses to claim personal power or holiness as the source;
- uses Jesus' name as witness to Jesus, not as a formula;
- accepts accountability;
- directs attention to Christ and glory to God.

## 20. Findings

### 20.1 Directly supported by the biblical text

- Jesus gave the Twelve authority over unclean spirits and sickness (Matthew 10:1).
- Jesus commissioned them to preach that the Kingdom of heaven was at hand (Matthew 10:7).
- Jesus commanded them to give freely what they had freely received (Matthew 10:8).
- Jesus also said the worker was worthy of provision (Matthew 10:10).
- Peter said, “such as I have give I thee,” and commanded the lame man to walk in the name of Jesus Christ (Acts 3:6).
- Peter denied that the healing was by his own power or holiness (Acts 3:12).
- Peter attributed the man's strength to the name of Jesus and faith in His name (Acts 3:16).
- Peter preached Jesus' death and resurrection as the centre of the public witness (Acts 3:13–26; 4:10–12).
- Peter rejected Simon's attempt to purchase the gift of God (Acts 8:18–24).
- Acts 19 distinguishes God's works through Paul from the sons of Sceva's attempt to use Jesus' name as a formula (Acts 19:11–17).
- The Spirit distributes gifts as He wills (1 Corinthians 12:11).
- The manifestation of the Spirit is given for the common good (1 Corinthians 12:7).
- Received gifts are to be ministered to others as good stewards of God's grace (1 Peter 4:10–11).
- Scripture recognizes legitimate material support for those who labour in ministry (Matthew 10:10; Luke 10:7; 1 Corinthians 9:7–14).

### 20.2 Supported interpretation

- The user's sentence is a synthesis of Matthew 10:8 and Acts 3:6, not a verbatim quotation of one verse.
- Matthew 10:8 prohibits treating the received commission and divine benefits as commodities to be sold.
- Acts 3:6 shows Peter acting as a witness in the name of Jesus, while Acts 3:12–16 prevents attributing the healing to Peter's independent power or holiness.
- The name of Jesus identifies the Person and authority of Jesus; it is not a magical verbal formula.
- Gifts are entrusted for service and the common good, not private status or ownership.
- Freely giving does not abolish ordinary support for ministers; the New Testament distinguishes support for a worker from the sale of God's gift.
- The faithful witness directs attention and glory to God through Jesus Christ.

### 20.3 Not established by this investigation

- That every believer has the same authority or commission as the Twelve in Matthew 10.
- That every believer has the gift of healing or miracles.
- That every person prayed for will be healed immediately or in this life.
- That financial support for ministry is always wrong.
- That every healing ministry which receives support is automatically legitimate.

## 21. Limitations and counter-evidence

- Matthew 10:1–15 describes a specific commission to the Twelve. Its principles may inform later Christian ministry, but its precise instructions should not be transferred mechanically to every believer or historical setting.
- The command “freely give” is situated beside Jesus' instruction that the worker is worthy of provision. A reading that forbids all support must explain Matthew 10:10 and the parallel teaching in Luke 10:7.
- Acts 3 is a theological and historical narrative. The study reports what the text says and does not claim modern clinical documentation of the man's condition or recovery.
- Acts 3:16 uses language concerning the name of Jesus and faith in His name. The precise relationship among Jesus' authority, the apostles' faith, the healed man's response, and God's action should not be reduced to a formula.
- Acts 8 concerns Simon's attempt to purchase the ability to impart the Holy Ghost. It should not be broadened into a claim that all financial support of Christian workers is simony.
- 2 Kings 5 condemns Gehazi's deceit and personal gain in this particular event. It does not state that every prophet or minister must refuse every voluntary gift.
- The New Testament recognizes both voluntary support and the danger of financial exploitation. Each case requires attention to the actual conduct and claims being made.
- A report of healing and a judgment about the spiritual authority of the person reporting it are distinct questions.
- No medical diagnosis or treatment recommendation is made. Prayer should not be used to discourage appropriate medical care.
- No GUA-W architecture is revised.
