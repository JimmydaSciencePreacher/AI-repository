## 1. Research question

What does Scripture mean when it speaks of the name of Jesus Christ, and what is the relationship between Christ's supreme authority and the authority He gives to those He sends?

The central passages are Philippians 2:5–11, Matthew 28:18–20, John 14:6–14, Acts 3:1–16, Acts 4:7–12, Acts 16:16–18, Acts 19:11–20, and Colossians 3:17.

The investigation asks:

1. What is the “name which is above every name” in Philippians 2:9–11?
2. How does the exaltation of Jesus relate to His humiliation, obedience, death, and resurrection?
3. What does “in the name of Jesus” mean in Acts?
4. Why do the authorities in Acts 4 ask Peter and John about both power and name?
5. What authority does the risen Christ possess, and what does He commission His disciples to do?
6. What does it mean to ask the Father in Jesus' name?
7. How do the Spirit's agency and Christ's authority relate to the witness's action?
8. What distinguishes delegated authority from independent or self-claimed authority?
9. Why do the sons of Sceva fail when they invoke the name of Jesus?
10. How does the name of Jesus relate to salvation, allegiance, obedience, worship, and the glory of the Father?

## 4. Philippians 2:5–11 — The movement from humiliation to exaltation

### 4.1 The passage begins with the mind of Christ

Philippians 2:5 calls believers to have the mind which was also in Christ Jesus. The passage is not an isolated declaration about a title; it is part of Paul's appeal to humility, unity, and regard for others.

### 4.2 Christ's humiliation

Philippians 2:6–8 describes Christ's form, His taking the form of a servant, His coming in the likeness of men, His humbling of Himself, His obedience, and His death on the cross.

The movement is downward:
- He takes the form of a servant.
- He humbles Himself.
- He becomes obedient unto death.
- His death is the death of the cross.

The text presents this as Christ's own obedience and self-humbling.

### 4.3 God's exaltation of Christ

Philippians 2:9 begins, “Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name.”

The subject of the action is God. God highly exalts Christ and bestows the name above every name.

The passage does not describe the disciples exalting Jesus into lordship. Their confession acknowledges the lordship God has declared.

### 4.4 Universal homage and confession

Philippians 2:10–11 says every knee shall bow and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.

The scope is comprehensive: heavenly, earthly, and under-earthly beings. The confession is not limited to one congregation or one historical audience.

The final phrase matters: the confession of Jesus Christ as Lord is “to the glory of God the Father.”

### 4.5 What the passage establishes

- Christ's humility and obedience precede the exaltation described in the passage.
- God is the One who exalts Him.
- Jesus Christ is confessed as Lord.
- Universal homage is directed to Him.
- The confession glorifies God the Father.

The passage does not say that the apostles, the church, or any individual witness owns this universal lordship.

## 5. What is “the name above every name”?

There is a genuine interpretive discussion about the precise referent of “the name” in Philippians 2:9.

### 5.1 The title “Lord”

Philippians 2:11 supplies the confession: “Jesus Christ is Lord.” The Greek title *Kyrios* (“Lord”) is especially significant because the Greek Old Testament frequently uses it to render the divine name YHWH. The wording of Philippians 2:10–11 also echoes Isaiah 45:23, where universal bowing and confession belong to the LORD.

On this reading, the name above every name is the supreme divine designation or status expressed in the confession that Jesus Christ is Lord.

### 5.2 The name “Jesus”

Some interpreters argue that the name is “Jesus,” because verse 10 immediately speaks of the name of Jesus and because the human name borne by the crucified Jesus is now publicly exalted and honored.

On this reading, the exaltation does not mean that the earthly name was unknown before or that Jesus acquired a different personal identity. It means that the name of Jesus is now accorded supreme honor and universal acknowledgment.

### 5.3 A related question: name, status, and dignity

In biblical usage, “name” can signify more than a sound or personal label. It may identify the person, reputation, authority, standing, or honor associated with the bearer.

The UBS translation commentary on Philippians 2:9 explains that “name” in this setting carries the sense of character, position, role, rank, or dignity, and identifies “Lord” in verse 11 as the most probable referent. Other scholarly discussions argue for “Jesus” or distinguish the name itself from the title confessed in verse 11.

### 5.4 What can be stated without overstating the debate

The exact grammatical identification remains discussed. But the passage's central confession is not uncertain: **Jesus Christ is Lord**, and the universal acknowledgment of His lordship glorifies the Father.

The study therefore records the interpretive debate rather than pretending that every scholar agrees on one narrow grammatical answer.

## 6. Isaiah 45:18–25 — The Old Testament background

Isaiah 45 is a major background to Philippians 2:10–11.

In Isaiah 45:21–23, the LORD declares that there is no God else beside Him, a just God and a Saviour. He calls the ends of the earth to look unto Him and be saved. He declares that every knee shall bow and every tongue shall swear.

Paul's language in Philippians 2:10–11 deliberately recalls this universal homage language.

This is significant for Christology. The universal confession of Jesus Christ as Lord is expressed with language associated with the LORD's own universal sovereignty in Isaiah.

### 6.1 Theological implication

Paul is not merely saying Jesus has a respected human title. The language places the exalted Jesus within the scriptural declaration of universal divine sovereignty.

### 6.2 Necessary distinction

Philippians still distinguishes God who exalts Christ from Christ who is exalted. The distinction between Father and Son is not erased by the Son's exalted status. The passage culminates in the glory of God the Father.

## 7. Matthew 28:16–20 — All authority and the commission

The risen Jesus declares:

> “All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth.” (Matthew 28:18)

He then commands the disciples to go, teach all nations, baptize, and teach them to observe all that He has commanded.

### 7.1 The order matters

1. The risen Christ declares His authority.
2. Christ commissions His disciples.
3. The disciples are to make disciples among all nations.
4. They baptize in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost.
5. They teach obedience to Christ's commands.
6. Christ promises His presence with them.

The mission proceeds from Christ's authority and command. The disciples do not originate the mission or establish its terms independently.

### 7.2 “All power” and delegated mission

The KJV's “power” here translates the Greek *exousia*, commonly meaning authority or right. Jesus declares that all authority in heaven and earth belongs to Him.

The disciples receive a commission to act. They do not receive ownership of all authority in heaven and earth.

### 7.3 The name of Father, Son, and Holy Ghost

Matthew 28:19 places baptism in the singular “name” of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost. The text distinguishes the three while placing them together in the baptismal commission.

The verse must not be reduced to a human formula for controlling spiritual outcomes. It identifies the divine name into which disciples are baptized and the authority under which the mission proceeds.

## 8. Acts 2:22–36 — The Father exalts Jesus; Jesus receives and pours forth the promise

Peter's Pentecost sermon provides a bridge between Philippians 2 and the apostolic witness.

Acts 2:22–24 proclaims Jesus' mighty works, crucifixion, and resurrection. Acts 2:32 declares that God raised Jesus, and the apostles are witnesses. Acts 2:33 says Jesus, being exalted by the right hand of God, has received the promise of the Holy Ghost from the Father and has shed forth what the crowd sees and hears.

Acts 2:36 concludes that God has made the crucified Jesus both Lord and Christ.

The text distinguishes:
- God the Father, who raises and exalts;
- Jesus, who is raised and exalted;
- the Holy Ghost, whose promised outpouring is given;
- the apostles, who witness to what God has done.

The apostolic witness is real, but it is not the originating cause of the resurrection or the outpouring of the Spirit.

## 9. Acts 3:1–16 — The name of Jesus in the healing at the Beautiful Gate

Acts 3 records Peter and John encountering a man lame from birth.

Peter says:

> “In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk.” (Acts 3:6)

The man is healed, enters the temple walking and leaping, and praises God.

### 9.1 The name and the event

Peter invokes the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth. The narrative then records a concrete action and result.

The name is not detached from Jesus' identity. Peter identifies the particular Jesus: Jesus Christ of Nazareth.

### 9.2 Peter's correction of mistaken attribution

Acts 3:12 is essential:

> “Why look ye so earnestly on us, as though by our own power or holiness we had made this man to walk?”

Peter rejects the inference that the healing originated in his own power or holiness.

### 9.3 Peter's explanation

Acts 3:13–16 turns the crowd toward the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the glorification of Jesus, the rejection of Jesus by the people, God's raising of Him from the dead, and faith in His name.

Peter is a witness and participant in the event. He does not claim to be the source of the authority or the healing.

## 10. Acts 4:1–12 — “By what power, or by what name?”

The rulers ask Peter and John:

> “By what power, or by what name, have ye done this?” (Acts 4:7)

The question joins power and name. It asks about the authority behind the healing.

Peter, filled with the Holy Ghost, answers that the man stands whole by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom the rulers crucified and whom God raised from the dead (Acts 4:8–10).

Acts 4:12 then declares that salvation is in no other name.

### 10.1 The name as authority and representation

A UBS translation commentary on Acts 4:7 explains that “name” in such contexts can represent the authority or permission of the person named. It notes that some languages naturally express the question as “by whose authority” or “who gave you the right?”

This supports an important interpretive distinction: “in the name of Jesus” is not merely saying a sequence of syllables. It identifies the authority and Person represented.

### 10.2 The apostolic answer

Peter does not claim that the authority is his own. He identifies Jesus, the crucified and risen Christ.

The healing becomes an occasion to proclaim the resurrection and exclusive saving significance of Christ.

## 11. John 14:1–14 — Asking in Jesus' name

John 14 contains Jesus' words about the way to the Father, His unity with the Father, His works, and prayer in His name.

Jesus says:

> “And whatsoever ye shall ask in my name, that will I do, that the Father may be glorified in the Son.” (John 14:13)

### 11.1 The stated purpose

The verse itself gives the purpose: “that the Father may be glorified in the Son.”

Prayer in Jesus' name is not presented as a means for the disciple to establish an independent will. Its stated end is the Father's glory in the Son.

### 11.2 The surrounding context

John 14:6 identifies Jesus as the way, the truth, and the life, and says no one comes to the Father but by Him.

John 14:10–11 describes the mutual indwelling of Father and Son and the Father's works in Jesus.

John 14:12–14 places the disciples' future works and asking in Jesus' name within the context of Jesus going to the Father.

### 11.3 “In my name” is not a blank cheque

The passage does not teach that appending the words “in Jesus' name” guarantees any requested outcome, irrespective of Christ's identity, mission, or the Father's glory.

John 15:1–17 develops the context of abiding in Christ, His words abiding in the disciples, obedience to His commandments, love, and bearing fruit. John 16:23–28 again connects asking in Jesus' name with the Father's giving and the disciples' relationship to the Son.

The broader Johannine context prevents treating the phrase as a mechanical formula.

### 11.4 What remains to be examined

The relationship between the promise in John 14:13–14 and the later qualifications and developments in John 15–16 deserves close attention. The study records the contextual connection but does not claim that every theological question about prayer is settled here.

## 12. John 5:19–30 — The Son's work and the Father's authority

John 5 records Jesus explaining His relation to the Father.

Jesus says the Son can do nothing of Himself, but what He sees the Father do; the Son does likewise (John 5:19). The Father shows the Son all things that He does (John 5:20). The Father has given the Son authority to execute judgment (John 5:22, 27).

This passage requires careful reading. It does not describe Jesus as a merely independent human actor. It presents the Son's works in relation to the Father, while also affirming the Son's unique authority, life-giving power, and judgment.

### 12.1 A distinction for this study

The Son's relation to the Father is unique and cannot simply be equated with the relation of an apostle to Christ.

A disciple is commissioned by Jesus. The Son's relation to the Father belongs to the divine identity and mission revealed in John's Gospel. Similar words about receiving, sending, or doing must not erase this difference.

## 13. John 17:1–26 — The name given, revealed, and kept

Jesus' prayer in John 17 repeatedly speaks of the Father's name.

- John 17:6 — Jesus has manifested the Father's name to the people given Him.
- John 17:11 — Jesus asks the Father to keep them through His own name, which the Father has given Him.
- John 17:12 — Jesus says He kept those given to Him in the Father's name.
- John 17:26 — Jesus has declared the Father's name and will declare it.

The passage places the name within revelation, relationship, keeping, unity, and the Father's gift to the Son.

The exact textual and translation question in John 17:11 is discussed among interpreters, but the wider prayer makes clear that the Father's name is not a magic word. It is bound to the Father's identity and the Son's revelation of Him.

### 13.1 The disciples' unity

John 17:20–23 asks that those who believe through the apostles' word may be one. The unity of believers is patterned in relation to the Father and Son, but it does not make believers divine or erase the distinction between Christ and His people.

## 14. Acts 16:16–18 — Paul acts in the name of Jesus Christ

In Philippi, Paul encounters a servant girl with a spirit of divination. After many days, Paul turns and says to the spirit:

> “I command thee in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her.” (Acts 16:18)

The spirit comes out that same hour.

The passage describes Paul commanding in Jesus' name. It does not describe Paul as the independent source of the authority.

The account is part of Acts' wider presentation of the mission of the risen Christ through the Holy Ghost and His witnesses.

The passage also raises questions about the relation between spiritual oppression, public proclamation, and the economic exploitation of the girl. The study does not turn the event into a general formula for every situation involving mental illness or unusual behavior.

## 15. Acts 19:11–20 — God's works through Paul and the sons of Sceva

Acts 19:11 says:

> “And God wrought special miracles by the hands of Paul.”

The verse identifies God as the One working and Paul as the person through whose hands the works occur.

### 15.1 The attempted imitation

The sons of Sceva attempt to invoke “Jesus whom Paul preacheth” over an evil spirit (Acts 19:13).

The spirit answers that it knows Jesus and knows Paul, but does not recognize the sons of Sceva. The possessed man overcomes them.

### 15.2 The contrast

The narrative distinguishes:
- God's works through Paul;
- Paul's relation to the mission of Jesus;
- the sons of Sceva's attempt to imitate an outward formula.

The name of Jesus is not an incantation that can be detached from the Person and authority it identifies.

### 15.3 The result

Acts 19:17 says the event becomes known, fear falls upon the people, and the name of the Lord Jesus is magnified.

Acts 19:18–20 describes confession, renunciation of magical practices, and the growth of the word of the Lord.

The narrative's final emphasis is not the reputation of Paul or the technique of exorcism. It is the magnification of Jesus' name and the spread of His word.

## 16. Colossians 3:17 — Every word and deed in the name of the Lord Jesus

Paul writes:

> “And whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God and the Father by him.”

This instruction broadens the discussion beyond miracles.

The name of Jesus governs ordinary speech and action, not merely dramatic spiritual events. The verse connects:
- word;
- deed;
- the Lord Jesus;
- thanksgiving;
- God the Father.

The next verses address household relationships and daily conduct. Thus “in the name” is a comprehensive orientation of life under Christ, not a phrase reserved for a moment of supernatural command.

## 17. Romans 10:9–13 and Acts 2:21 — Calling upon the name of the Lord

Romans 10:9–13 connects confessing Jesus as Lord, believing that God raised Him from the dead, salvation, and calling upon the name of the Lord.

Paul quotes Joel 2:32, where calling upon the name of the LORD is associated with salvation.

This again connects the confession of Jesus as Lord with the scriptural language of calling upon the LORD.

The name is not a verbal password. Calling upon the Lord is an act of faith and appeal to the Person confessed as Lord.

## 18. Authority, delegation, and the witness: distinctions

The passages support several distinct statements that must not be collapsed into one another.

### 18.1 Christ's authority

Matthew 28:18 declares that all authority in heaven and earth has been given to the risen Christ. Philippians 2:9–11 proclaims His exaltation and universal lordship.

### 18.2 Christ's commission

Matthew 28:19–20 records Christ sending His disciples to make disciples, baptize, and teach. The commission originates with Christ.

### 18.3 The Spirit's agency

Acts 1:8 promises power when the Holy Ghost comes upon the disciples, so they will be witnesses to Christ. Acts 4:8 describes Peter filled with the Holy Ghost as he testifies before the authorities.

### 18.4 The witness's participation

Peter speaks, reaches out, and lifts the man (Acts 3:6–7). Paul speaks and commands in Acts 16 and 19. The witnesses are active participants, not inert objects.

### 18.5 The witness is not the originating authority

Acts 3:12 explicitly rejects attribution of the healing to Peter's own power or holiness. Acts 19:11 says God wrought miracles by Paul's hands. These texts distinguish divine action from the human means through which it is manifested.

### 18.6 Delegation does not make the recipient the Source

A person may be authorized to act on behalf of another without becoming the one who grants the authority or owns the mission. This is an explanatory analogy for the biblical commission; it is not a claim that divine authority is merely a human legal arrangement.

## 19. Theological synthesis

The passages establish a coherent pattern while preserving their distinct contexts:

1. Christ humbles Himself and becomes obedient unto death (Philippians 2:5–8).
2. God highly exalts Him and bestows the name above every name (Philippians 2:9).
3. Every knee bows and every tongue confesses Jesus Christ as Lord, to the glory of the Father (Philippians 2:10–11).
4. The risen Christ declares that all authority in heaven and earth belongs to Him (Matthew 28:18).
5. Christ commissions His disciples (Matthew 28:19–20).
6. The Holy Ghost empowers the disciples to be witnesses to Christ (Acts 1:8).
7. The apostles act and speak in Jesus' name (Acts 3:6; 16:18).
8. They refuse to claim independent power or holiness (Acts 3:12).
9. They identify Jesus as the crucified and risen Lord (Acts 3:13–16; 4:10–12).
10. Attempts to use the name as an independent formula are exposed (Acts 19:13–17).
11. Believers are to conduct every word and deed in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to the Father (Colossians 3:17).

The name of Jesus is inseparable from the Person of Jesus, His identity, authority, and lordship. To act in His name is not to become Him or to acquire His universal authority as personal property. It is to act under His commission and in relation to His authority, with the purpose of honoring Him and glorifying the Father.

## 22. Findings

### 22.1 Directly supported by Scripture

- Christ humbled Himself and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross (Philippians 2:5–8).
- God highly exalted Him and gave Him the name above every name (Philippians 2:9).
- Every knee shall bow and every tongue confess Jesus Christ as Lord, to the glory of God the Father (Philippians 2:10–11).
- The risen Jesus declares that all authority in heaven and earth has been given to Him (Matthew 28:18).
- Jesus commissions His disciples to make disciples, baptize, and teach obedience to His commands (Matthew 28:19–20).
- The Holy Ghost empowers the disciples to be witnesses to Christ (Acts 1:8).
- Peter acts in the name of Jesus Christ in the healing at the Beautiful Gate (Acts 3:6).
- Peter denies that the healing was by his own power or holiness (Acts 3:12).
- The authorities ask by what power or name the apostles acted (Acts 4:7).
- Peter identifies Jesus Christ as the authority associated with the healing and proclaims His resurrection (Acts 4:8–12).
- Jesus connects asking in His name with the Father's glory in the Son (John 14:13).
- Acts 19 distinguishes God's works through Paul from the sons of Sceva's attempted imitation (Acts 19:11–17).
- Believers are instructed to do all in word and deed in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to the Father (Colossians 3:17).

### 22.2 Supported interpretation

- The name of Jesus is not merely a sound or label; in these contexts it is bound to His identity, authority, status, and lordship.
- “In the name of Jesus” commonly identifies the authority and Person represented, though each passage must be read in its own context.
- Delegated commission does not make the disciple the independent source or owner of Christ's authority.
- The Spirit's empowerment and the witness's human participation are both present in Acts.
- The name of Jesus is not a magical formula that compels God or guarantees any outcome requested.
- The purpose of asking in Jesus' name is explicitly connected with the Father's glory in the Son.

### 22.3 Not established by this investigation

- A definitive resolution of every grammatical debate about the referent of “the name above every name” in Philippians 2:9.
- That every believer has the same commission or authority as the apostles in every respect.
- That every prayer phrased “in Jesus' name” must receive the exact requested result.
- That a witness can independently control the Holy Ghost or divine power.
- That a miracle report alone proves the spiritual standing of the person reporting it.
- That scientific methods can verify the divine identity or authority of Jesus Christ.
- That a new GUA-W layer, operation, state, or definition is required.

## 23. Limitations and counter-evidence

- Philippians 2:9's precise referent for “the name above every name” is debated. The study records the debate and relies on the explicit confession of verse 11 for its central finding.
- The Old Testament background of Isaiah 45:23 is important, but the precise way Paul applies the passage to Jesus should be described with theological care.
- Matthew 28:18–20 is a commission to the disciples and must not be converted into a claim that every individual Christian possesses unlimited authority.
- John 14:13–14 must be read in the context of John 14–16, including abiding in Christ, obedience, the Spirit's coming, and the Father's glory.
- “In the name” can express different relations in different passages: authority, representation, confession, appeal, allegiance, or action. A single English phrase should not erase contextual distinctions.
- Acts is a theological narrative. The study reports what Luke records and does not claim independent modern clinical verification of each miraculous event.
- The failure of the sons of Sceva does not provide a diagnostic checklist for every contemporary spiritual or psychological event.
- No scientific mechanism for divine action is proposed.
