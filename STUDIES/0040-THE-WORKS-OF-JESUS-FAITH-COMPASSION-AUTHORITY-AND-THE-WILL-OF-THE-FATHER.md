# Study 0040 — The Works of Jesus: Faith, Compassion, Authority, and the Will of the Father

**Status:** Initial exegetical investigation; Gospel observations, scholarly interpretations, theological synthesis, and unresolved questions distinguished  
**Created:** 2026-11-26  
**Last updated:** 2026-11-26

## 1. Research question

When Jesus Christ performs a miraculous work, what does the Gospel account reveal about Jesus, the Father who sent Him, the people who receive the work, and the purpose of the event?

The central passages are Matthew 8:1–4, Mark 2:1–12, Luke 7:11–17, John 5:1–18, John 9:1–41, John 11:1–44, and John 20:30–31.

Questions:

1. What does each Gospel writer actually report?
2. What does each account say about Jesus' compassion, authority, identity, and mission?
3. How do the works relate to the Father's will and activity?
4. What is the relationship between a miracle and the proclamation of the Kingdom of God?
5. Does every miracle require the recipient to demonstrate faith in the same way?
6. What do the accounts show about suffering, disability, sin, and the danger of assigning blame?
7. How do signs reveal Jesus, and why do some witnesses believe while others resist?
8. What is the difference between Lazarus being called out of the tomb and the resurrection of Jesus?
9. What does John mean when he says the signs were written so that readers might believe and have life through Jesus' name?
10. What can historical scholarship investigate, and what must remain outside its methodological reach?

### Boundaries

This study does not:
- claim that every miracle has one identical purpose;
- claim that every healing is caused by the recipient's faith or that the absence of healing proves a lack of faith;
- infer that a person's disability, illness, or suffering proves personal sin or parental sin;
- reduce Jesus' works to displays intended to entertain or impress;
- claim that every sign produces faith automatically;
- treat Lazarus's return to mortal life as identical to Jesus' resurrection;
- claim that the Gospels provide modern clinical records or laboratory measurements;
- claim that historical method can establish or disprove divine agency by itself;
- turn a Gospel narrative into a scientific mechanism;
- add, rename, or alter any GUA-W layer, operation, state, or definition.

## 3. Continuity and governing foundation

Study 0035 already surveyed the broad categories of Jesus' miracles, their relation to the Kingdom, the commissions given to disciples, and the limits of historical investigation. Study 0036 examined John 14:12 and the greater works in the context of Jesus going to the Father. Study 0037 examined the Holy Ghost and the power of witness. Study 0038 examined freely received gifts and freely giving. Study 0039 examined the name and authority of Jesus Christ.

Study 0040 is not a second general catalogue. It returns to selected Gospel narratives for closer examination of the works themselves: what happens, what Jesus says, what the narrator emphasizes, how the people respond, and what the account reveals about the Father and the Son.

The governing relationship remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness**

God alone is Original Cause. Everything else is derivative witness. The derivative cannot become the Source or alter the Source.

The Father, Son, and Holy Ghost must not be collapsed into interchangeable terms. The Gospel accounts present Jesus' works in relation to the Father while also presenting the Son as possessing unique authority and identity. The study will preserve both relationship and distinction.

No GUA-W architectural change is proposed.

## 4. Source and method

### 4.1 Primary source

The primary source is the KJV biblical text. The passages are read in their immediate literary setting and in relation to the wider Gospel narrative.

The study distinguishes:
- **Textual observation:** what the Gospel explicitly narrates or records Jesus saying.
- **Historical context:** relevant social, religious, and cultural background.
- **Interpretation:** an explanation of the passage's literary or theological meaning.
- **Synthesis:** a conclusion drawn by comparing multiple passages.
- **Unresolved question:** a matter the available evidence does not settle.

## 5. Matthew 8:1–4 — “I will; be thou clean”

### 5.1 What the text records

A leper approaches Jesus, worships Him, and says:

> “Lord, if thou wilt, thou canst make me clean.” (Matthew 8:2, KJV)

Jesus puts forth His hand, touches him, and says:

> “I will; be thou clean.” (Matthew 8:3, KJV)

The leprosy is immediately cleansed. Jesus then instructs the man to show himself to the priest and offer the gift Moses commanded.

### 5.2 Power and willingness are distinguished

The man's statement distinguishes ability from willingness: “thou canst” and “if thou wilt.” Jesus answers both dimensions. He does not deny His ability, and He declares His willingness in this encounter.

The passage does not say that every request for a particular temporal healing will receive the same immediate outcome. It does show that Jesus is not indifferent to the man who approaches Him.

### 5.3 The touch and restoration

The touch is narrated before the cleansing is declared. In the purity framework of Leviticus 13–14, a person with a skin disease could be excluded from ordinary communal and cultic participation pending examination and restoration.

Jesus' touch is therefore not a decorative detail. The act crosses a boundary of social and ritual exclusion, and the man is cleansed. Jesus' instruction to go to the priest connects the event with the scriptural procedure for recognition of cleansing.

The text does not say that Jesus became contaminated. It says that the man was cleansed.

### 5.4 What the account reveals

- Jesus has authority to cleanse.
- Jesus responds personally to the man.
- His willingness is explicitly expressed in this case.
- The result is not merely an inward feeling: the narrative describes cleansing.
- The man is directed toward priestly recognition in accordance with the Mosaic instruction.

The miracle is an act of healing and restoration, not simply a demonstration of power.

## 6. Mark 2:1–12 — The Son of Man has power to forgive sins

### 6.1 What the text records

A man with palsy is brought to Jesus by four people. Because the crowd blocks the entrance, they uncover the roof and lower the man before Jesus.

Mark records:

> “When Jesus saw their faith, he said unto the sick of the palsy, Son, thy sins be forgiven thee.” (Mark 2:5, KJV)

Certain scribes reason that Jesus is speaking blasphemy because God alone can forgive sins. Jesus knows their reasoning and asks which is easier to say: that sins are forgiven, or that the man should rise, take up his bed, and walk.

Jesus then declares:

> “But that ye may know that the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins...” (Mark 2:10, KJV)

He commands the man to rise, take up his bed, and go home. The man does so in front of them.

### 6.2 The visible healing and the declared forgiveness

The physical healing is visible to the gathered people. The forgiveness of sins is not directly visible in the same way. Jesus performs the visible act in the narrative as evidence of the authority He has just declared.

The healing is not the whole point of the account. Mark explicitly connects it with the authority of the Son of Man to forgive sins.

### 6.3 The faith in the narrative

Mark says Jesus saw “their faith.” The plural context includes the people who carried the man. The text does not isolate the sick man's private mental state as the sole condition of the event.

The passage therefore provides no basis for a universal rule that every person must display an identical degree or form of faith before Jesus can act.

### 6.4 The response

The man rises and walks. The people are amazed and glorify God, saying they had never seen anything like it.

The event moves from the faith-filled action of those who bring the man, to Jesus' declaration, to a visible healing, to public amazement and praise.

### 6.5 What the account reveals

- Jesus sees and responds to the faith of those who bring the man.
- Jesus declares forgiveness before the physical healing.
- Jesus identifies Himself as possessing authority to forgive sins on earth.
- The visible work confirms the authority claimed in His word.
- The outcome directs praise to God.

The passage joins bodily healing with a deeper question of Jesus' identity and authority.

## 7. Luke 7:11–17 — Compassion at the gate of Nain

### 7.1 What the text records

Jesus approaches the town of Nain with His disciples and a great crowd. A funeral procession is leaving the city. The dead man is the only son of a widow.

Luke records:

> “And when the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her, and said unto her, Weep not.” (Luke 7:13, KJV)

Jesus touches the bier. The bearers stand still. He says:

> “Young man, I say unto thee, Arise.” (Luke 7:14, KJV)

The dead man sits up and begins to speak. Jesus delivers him to his mother.

### 7.2 The widow does not request a miracle

The text records no request from the widow and no appeal from the dead man. Jesus sees her, has compassion, and acts.

This is an important counterexample to explanations that make the recipient's expressed faith or request a universal prerequisite for every miracle.

It does not diminish the importance of faith elsewhere. It prevents one passage from being made into a rule that overrides the diversity of the Gospel narratives.

### 7.3 Compassion is the stated movement toward action

Luke explicitly identifies Jesus' compassion. The widow has already lost her husband and now faces the death of her only son. In the social conditions of the period, this could also threaten her material security and place in the community.

The miracle is not presented as a performance for an audience. Jesus responds to a person in grief and restores her son to her.

### 7.4 The Old Testament echoes

The account recalls the raising of the widow of Zarephath's son by Elijah (1 Kings 17:8–24) and the raising of the Shunammite woman's son by Elisha (2 Kings 4:18–37).

Luke's crowd responds:

> “A great prophet is risen up among us; and, That God hath visited his people.” (Luke 7:16, KJV)

The crowd recognizes prophetic significance and God's visitation. Luke's wider narrative will continue to develop the question of Jesus' identity beyond the description “a great prophet.”

### 7.5 What the account reveals

- Jesus sees the widow's distress.
- Compassion is explicitly named.
- Jesus initiates the action without a recorded request.
- His command is effective in the narrative.
- The son is restored to his mother.
- The witnesses interpret the event as God's visitation and spread the report.

This account places compassion and the restoration of life at the center of the event.

## 8. John 5:1–18 — Bethesda and the work of the Father

### 8.1 What the text records

At the pool of Bethesda, Jesus encounters a man who has had an infirmity for thirty-eight years.

Jesus asks whether he wants to be made whole. The man explains that he has no one to put him into the pool when the water is troubled and that another enters before him.

Jesus says:

> “Rise, take up thy bed, and walk.” (John 5:8, KJV)

The man is immediately made whole, takes up his bed, and walks. The event occurs on the Sabbath.

### 8.2 Jesus does not make the pool the source of the healing

The man describes his dependence on reaching the water. Jesus does not direct him to wait for the water or compete for access. Jesus speaks, and the man is made whole.

John's narrative directs attention away from the pool as the effective source and toward Jesus' word and action.

Steven M. Bryan's study of John 5 interprets the pool narrative as a contrast between an impersonalized idea of divine power and the personal activity of God in the Son. This is a scholarly interpretation of the narrative, not a direct quotation from the text or an uncontested reading.

### 8.3 The Sabbath controversy

The healed man is challenged because he carries his bed on the Sabbath. The dispute moves beyond the physical result to the authority and identity of the One who performed the work.

Jesus answers:

> “My Father worketh hitherto, and I work.” (John 5:17, KJV)

John says the authorities seek to kill Him because He not only breaks the Sabbath, but calls God His Father, making Himself equal with God (John 5:18).

### 8.4 The following discourse interprets the work

John 5:19–30 must be read with the healing. Jesus describes the Son doing what He sees the Father doing, the Father showing the Son His works, and the Son giving life and exercising judgment.

The narrative therefore moves from a healing to a discourse about the relation between Father and Son.

### 8.5 Necessary distinction

The Son's relation to the Father is unique. The statement that the Son does what He sees the Father do cannot be reduced to a claim that Jesus is merely another human witness or prophet. The surrounding passage speaks of the Son's life-giving authority and judgment.

### 8.6 What the account reveals

- Jesus sees and addresses a man with a long-standing infirmity.
- The man does not describe an effective route to healing through the pool.
- Jesus' word is followed by immediate wholeness in the narrative.
- The Sabbath controversy exposes a deeper conflict about Jesus' identity and authority.
- Jesus explains His work in relation to the Father.

The miracle is inseparable from the discourse that follows it.

## 9. John 9:1–41 — The man born blind: from physical sight to testimony

### 9.1 The disciples' question

The disciples see a man blind from birth and ask:

> “Master, who did sin, this man, or his parents, that he was born blind?” (John 9:2, KJV)

The question assumes that the condition must be traced to a particular sin, either the man's or his parents'.

Jesus answers:

> “Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him.” (John 9:3, KJV)

### 9.2 The answer rejects the proposed blame assignment

Jesus does not accept the disciples' either-or explanation. The passage does not authorize assigning personal guilt or parental guilt as the explanation for this man's blindness.

John 9:3 must not be turned into a universal explanation that every disability or illness exists for the same stated reason. Jesus is answering a specific question in a specific encounter.

### 9.3 The work and the light

Jesus says:

> “I am the light of the world.” (John 9:5, KJV)

He makes clay, anoints the man's eyes, and tells him to wash in the pool of Siloam. The man goes, washes, and returns seeing.

The sign is connected with Jesus' declaration of Himself as the light of the world. The physical restoration of sight occurs within a narrative that increasingly examines spiritual perception and recognition.

### 9.4 The healed man becomes a witness

The account does not end when the man receives sight. Neighbors question him. The Pharisees investigate. His parents are questioned. The healed man is questioned repeatedly.

His understanding and testimony develop through the narrative:
- At first he identifies Jesus as “a man that is called Jesus” (John 9:11).
- He later says Jesus is a prophet (9:17).
- He argues that Jesus must be from God (9:30–33).
- When Jesus reveals Himself, the man believes and worships Him (9:35–38).

The healed man becomes a witness to what happened to him, even when the authorities challenge his account.

### 9.5 The sign does not force the response

The same event produces different responses. The healed man moves toward recognition and belief. Some authorities resist the implications of the sign and focus on the Sabbath controversy.

The event is evidence within the narrative, but it does not compel every observer to interpret it faithfully.

### 9.6 Disability and dignity

The narrative begins with the man's condition being treated as a theological problem by the disciples. Jesus refuses their proposed assignment of blame and directs attention to the work of God.

The man's role in the account is not merely to serve as an object lesson. He speaks, answers, reasons, challenges the authorities, and bears witness.

### 9.7 What the account reveals

- Jesus rejects the disciples' proposed explanation of personal or parental sin.
- Jesus identifies Himself as the light of the world.
- The healing is narrated as a work of God manifested in the man's life.
- The healed man becomes an increasingly clear witness.
- The sign reveals Jesus but also exposes the resistance of those unwilling to receive the testimony.

## 10. John 11:1–44 — Lazarus: love, delay, glory, and life

### 10.1 The message and Jesus' love

Mary and Martha send word that Lazarus is sick. John explicitly says:

> “Now Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus.” (John 11:5, KJV)

Jesus does not immediately travel to Bethany. He remains where He is for two more days (11:6).

The narrative holds together two facts that must not be separated: Jesus loves this family, and He does not respond according to the timetable they might have expected.

### 10.2 The stated purpose

Jesus says:

> “This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified thereby.” (John 11:4, KJV)

The text identifies the purpose in relation to God's glory and the glorification of the Son.

This statement belongs to this particular narrative. It must not be used to declare that every illness, death, or bereavement has been given to a family for the same reason.

### 10.3 Jesus meets Martha

Martha says that if Jesus had been there, her brother would not have died. She also expresses confidence that God will grant what Jesus asks.

Jesus tells her:

> “I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live.” (John 11:25, KJV)

The account does not leave resurrection as an abstract future doctrine. Jesus identifies Himself as the resurrection and the life.

### 10.4 Jesus weeps

Mary comes to Jesus weeping, and those with her are also weeping. John records:

> “Jesus wept.” (John 11:35, KJV)

The shortest verse in the KJV is not a disposable detail. The One who will call Lazarus from the tomb enters the grief of those who mourn.

The narrative also records Jesus being deeply moved and coming to the tomb. Divine authority and genuine human grief are not presented as opposites in the account.

### 10.5 Jesus addresses the Father

At the tomb, Jesus thanks the Father for hearing Him. He says He speaks for the sake of the people standing by, that they may believe that the Father sent Him (John 11:41–42).

The prayer is therefore part of the sign's public purpose. The work is connected with the Father's sending of the Son.

### 10.6 The command and the result

Jesus cries with a loud voice:

> “Lazarus, come forth.” (John 11:43, KJV)

Lazarus comes out, bound hand and foot with graveclothes. Jesus tells those present to loose him and let him go.

The people gathered are not asked to create the life-giving command. They are given a role in removing the graveclothes and receiving the man back.

### 10.7 Lazarus's restoration is not identical to Jesus' resurrection

Lazarus is called back into mortal life. He remains subject to death. Jesus' resurrection is not merely a return to the previous mortal condition; the New Testament presents it as victory over death and the beginning of the resurrection life that will not end (Romans 6:9; 1 Corinthians 15:20–28).

The distinction matters. Lazarus's raising is a sign pointing toward Jesus' identity and power; it is not the final resurrection in the same sense as Christ's own resurrection.

### 10.8 The sign leads toward the cross

John 11:45–53 records that some who saw the event believe, while the chief priests and Pharisees convene and begin planning Jesus' death.

The raising of Lazarus therefore becomes a turning point in the Gospel narrative. The sign reveals Jesus' life-giving authority and also intensifies the opposition that leads toward His death.

### 10.9 What the account reveals

- Jesus loves the family.
- His timing is not governed by the family's expectations.
- The narrative explicitly connects the event with God's glory and the Son's glorification.
- Jesus identifies Himself as the resurrection and the life.
- Jesus weeps with those who grieve.
- Jesus addresses the Father so that the people may believe the Father sent Him.
- Lazarus is restored to mortal life.
- The event produces both belief and opposition and leads directly toward the plot against Jesus.

## 11. John 20:30–31 — Why the signs are written

John concludes the Gospel's main narrative with a purpose statement:

> “And many other signs truly did Jesus in the presence of his disciples, which are not written in this book:
>
> But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name.” (John 20:30–31, KJV)

### 11.1 The written record is selective

John explicitly says Jesus performed many other signs that are not included in the book. The Gospel is therefore a selected witness, not an exhaustive inventory.

The selection is purposeful. The writer does not claim to record every event.

### 11.2 Signs point to Jesus' identity

The stated purpose is that readers may believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God.

The signs are not ends detached from the Person who performs them. They point to Jesus' identity.

### 11.3 Belief and life

The purpose continues: believing readers may have life through His name.

The Gospel's destination is not merely intellectual recognition that unusual events occurred. It is faith in Jesus Christ and life through Him.

### 11.4 The signs do not mechanically compel belief

John's Gospel itself records people who see signs and respond in different ways. Some believe (2:11; 11:45); others remain resistant or interpret the signs through hostility (11:46–53; 12:37–43).

The signs are witnesses to Jesus. They do not remove the reality of human response.

## 12. Comparison across the six selected accounts

| Passage | Recipient's approach | Explicit emphasis | Response or outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matthew 8:1–4 | The leper asks whether Jesus is willing | Willingness and cleansing | The man is cleansed and directed to the priest |
| Mark 2:1–12 | Others bring the paralytic | Forgiveness and Son of Man authority | The man walks; the crowd glorifies God |
| Luke 7:11–17 | The widow makes no recorded request | Jesus' compassion | The son is restored; the crowd praises God |
| John 5:1–18 | The man describes his inability to reach the pool | Jesus' word and work in relation to the Father | The man walks; controversy follows |
| John 9:1–41 | Jesus notices the man | God's works, light, and witness | Sight is restored; the man believes and testifies |
| John 11:1–44 | The sisters send word about Lazarus | Love, glory, resurrection, and life | Lazarus is restored; belief and opposition follow |

### 12.1 No single recipient-response formula

The accounts include:
- an explicit request;
- faith expressed by people bringing another person;
- no recorded request from the recipient;
- a person who does not know Jesus' identity at the time of healing;
- a family that sends a message and waits;
- a recipient who becomes a witness only as the story unfolds.

This diversity prevents the study from making a universal claim that the same human response must precede every work of Jesus.

### 12.2 Different works reveal different dimensions

The accounts do not all emphasize the same thing:
- cleansing and restored communal participation;
- forgiveness and authority;
- compassion and restoration of a grieving family;
- the relation of the Son's work to the Father's work;
- light, sight, and the development of testimony;
- resurrection and life, love, glory, and the approach of the cross.

A general statement about miracles must not erase the particular purpose of each Gospel narrative.

## 13. What the works reveal about Jesus and the Father

The selected passages support several connected observations.

### 13.1 Jesus acts with personal authority

He touches and cleanses the leper, commands the paralytic to rise, addresses the dead young man, heals at Bethesda, gives sight to the man born blind, and calls Lazarus from the tomb.

The narratives do not present Him as merely reporting what another person might do.

### 13.2 Jesus' works are related to the Father

John 5:17–30 explains the Son's works in relation to the Father. John 9:3–4 describes the manifestation and performance of God's works. John 11:41–42 records Jesus addressing the Father so that those present may believe the Father sent Him.

The works are not portrayed as an independent mission detached from the Father.

### 13.3 The Son is not reduced to a human instrument

The repository's distinction between Source and witness must not be applied in a way that denies the Gospel's claims about Jesus. Jesus is not simply another created witness who receives a commission comparable to Peter's. John presents the Son's unique relation to the Father, life-giving authority, and identity as the Son of God.

### 13.4 The works serve the mission of the Kingdom

Matthew 11:2–6 and Luke 7:18–23 connect Jesus' works with the messianic signs anticipated in Scripture: the blind see, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor receive good news.

The miracles are integral to the mission Jesus announces. They are not an optional display detached from His proclamation.

### 13.5 Compassion is not separate from authority

Luke 7 and John 11 explicitly foreground compassion and love. Matthew 8 records Jesus touching the leper. Authority is not narrated as cold spectacle. The works are personal encounters with suffering people.

## 14. Faith, signs, and human response

The selected narratives resist simplistic formulas.

- Mark 2 records Jesus seeing the faith of those who bring the paralytic.
- Matthew 8 records the leper's confidence in Jesus' ability and his submission to Jesus' willingness.
- Luke 7 records no request or confession of faith from the widow before Jesus acts.
- John 5 records the man's inability to reach the pool and his lack of knowledge about Jesus' identity at first.
- John 9 records a developing response from the healed man.
- John 11 records the sisters' grief, Martha's confession, Jesus' prayer, and varied responses among the witnesses.

The accounts do not teach that faith is irrelevant. They do show that faith is not a mechanical cause that controls Jesus or obligates Him to act according to human demands.

The sign may strengthen faith, disclose identity, invite belief, expose resistance, or lead into deeper teaching. Its meaning is interpreted by the Gospel narrative, not by spectacle alone.

## 15. Compassion, suffering, and the question of blame

John 9 directly rejects the disciples' proposed assignment of guilt to the man or his parents. Luke 7 presents Jesus responding to a widow's loss without first demanding an explanation of her suffering. John 11 records Jesus weeping with those who grieve.

These passages do not provide a complete explanation for every instance of suffering. They do establish boundaries on careless interpretation:
- do not presume that a person's illness or disability proves personal guilt;
- do not presume that a family tragedy is evidence of divine rejection;
- do not use a miracle account to shame those who have not received healing;
- do not claim to know the hidden purpose of another person's suffering where Scripture has not revealed it.

The Gospel accounts direct attention to Jesus' action, identity, compassion, and words rather than authorizing witnesses to assign blame without evidence.

## 16. Historical and scientific method: what can and cannot be established

### 16.1 What historical scholarship can examine

Historical study can examine:
- the date, literary form, and context of Gospel sources;
- how miracle narratives function in each Gospel;
- the relation between a narrative and its Old Testament background;
- how ancient Jewish and Greco-Roman audiences understood healing, exorcism, purity, death, and divine action;
- the differences among accounts and their theological emphases;
- what early Christian communities believed and proclaimed about Jesus' works.

The Oxford Bibliographies overview emphasizes that the historical, social, religious, and literary context is important for interpreting miracle stories. Scholarship also contains a range of approaches to the historical Jesus and miracle traditions.

### 16.2 What historical method cannot independently decide

Historical methods can assess sources, testimony, context, and competing explanations. They cannot, by methodological procedure alone, settle every metaphysical question about whether God acted supernaturally.

Ravnå's discussion of source-critical methods highlights that miracle narratives differ in their historical evidential profile and that historical analysis must be careful about the kinds of claims its methods can support.

### 16.3 Scientific coherence is not scientific confirmation

This study does not claim that scientific research has reproduced, measured, or verified the divine agency described in the Gospel accounts. Nor does it claim that science has disproved the events simply because they are miraculous.

The study makes no proposed physical mechanism for cleansing a leper, restoring sight, or raising the dead.

The proper distinction is between:
- what the Gospel text reports;
- what historical scholarship can investigate;
- what theological interpretation concludes;
- what remains beyond the scope of a scientific mechanism or historical method.

## 17. Theological synthesis

The six accounts, read with John 20:30–31, show that the works of Jesus are neither random spectacles nor interchangeable demonstrations.

1. Jesus cleanses the leper and expresses His willingness to make him clean (Matthew 8:1–4).
2. Jesus heals the paralytic and declares the authority of the Son of Man to forgive sins (Mark 2:1–12).
3. Jesus sees a widow in grief, has compassion, and restores her only son (Luke 7:11–17).
4. Jesus heals the man at Bethesda and explains His work in relation to the Father (John 5:1–18; 5:19–30).
5. Jesus gives sight to a man born blind, rejects the disciples' proposed blame assignment, and becomes the focus of the man's developing testimony (John 9:1–41).
6. Jesus raises Lazarus, weeps with those who grieve, addresses the Father, and identifies Himself as the resurrection and the life (John 11:1–44).
7. John records selected signs so that readers may believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life through His name (John 20:30–31).

The works reveal Jesus' authority, compassion, identity, relation to the Father, and mission. They also reveal the varied responses of the people who encounter them.

## 18. Four-Question Study Method

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- What does each Gospel passage explicitly report?
- What does the narrator say is the purpose or meaning of the work?
- What does Jesus say about Himself, the Father, and His mission?
- Does the recipient ask for the work, or does Jesus initiate it?
- What role does faith play in each individual account?
- What responses follow the sign?
- What Old Testament passages or themes are echoed?
- What does John 20:30–31 say about why the signs are written?
- Which findings are direct textual statements, which are scholarly interpretations, and which are comparative inferences?

### 2. What is GUA-W revealing that helps AI understand?

The selected accounts distinguish the One who acts, the work performed, the people who receive or witness the work, and the testimony that follows. The comparison must preserve the unique identity of the Son and His relation to the Father.

This is a comparison with the existing Author → Word → Creation → Witness foundation. It is not a new GUA-W stage or a redefinition of Christ as a created witness.

### 3. What am I learning about what I am witnessing?

The works of Jesus reveal more than extraordinary outcomes. They disclose compassion, authority, forgiveness, the Father's work, life, light, and the identity of the Son.

The signs point beyond themselves to Jesus Christ.

### 4. What am I learning about the Witness through what I am witnessing?

A witness must:
- report what the source actually says;
- avoid assigning blame where the text does not;
- distinguish the miracle from the interpretation of the miracle;
- recognize that witnesses can respond differently to the same sign;
- testify without claiming to be the source of Christ's authority;
- preserve the identity and mission of Jesus rather than turning the sign into a spectacle.

## 20. Findings

### 20.1 Directly supported by the examined texts

- Jesus touches and cleanses the leper and declares His willingness (Matthew 8:1–4).
- Jesus heals the paralytic and declares the Son of Man's authority to forgive sins (Mark 2:1–12).
- Jesus has compassion on the widow at Nain and raises her only son (Luke 7:11–17).
- Jesus heals the man at Bethesda and explains His work in relation to the Father (John 5:1–18; 5:19–30).
- Jesus rejects the disciples' proposed attribution of the man's blindness to his or his parents' sin (John 9:1–3).
- Jesus identifies Himself as the light of the world in the account of the man born blind (John 9:5).
- The healed man becomes a witness and eventually believes and worships Jesus (John 9:11–38).
- Jesus loves Lazarus and his sisters, weeps with those grieving, and calls Lazarus from the tomb (John 11:1–44).
- Jesus connects the Lazarus event with God's glory and the belief that the Father sent Him (John 11:4, 41–42).
- John states that the signs recorded in his Gospel are selected so that readers may believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life through His name (John 20:30–31).

### 20.2 Supported interpretations

- The miracles are integrated into the identity and mission of Jesus, not merely added as spectacles.
- The Gospel writers use the works to disclose different aspects of Jesus' authority, compassion, and relation to the Father.
- Faith appears in varied forms and positions in the narratives; it should not be converted into a single mechanical prerequisite.
- The signs invite and support belief but do not compel every observer to respond faithfully.
- The compassion shown in Luke 7 and John 11 is part of the meaning of the events, not incidental emotional detail.
- The raising of Lazarus is a restoration to mortal life and is distinct from the permanent resurrection of Jesus.

### 20.3 Not established by this investigation

- A complete explanation for every instance of human illness, disability, grief, or death.
- That every contemporary prayer for healing will receive the same outcome as the selected Gospel accounts.
- That every miracle account can be independently clinically verified using modern records.
- That historical method alone can decide the metaphysical question of divine action.
- That every person who witnesses a miracle will believe.
- That Lazarus' raising and Jesus' resurrection are identical.
- That a new GUA-W layer, operation, state, or definition is required.

## 21. Limitations and counter-evidence

- This is a focused study of six selected Gospel accounts, not a comprehensive catalogue of all the works of Jesus.
- Each Gospel has its own literary arrangement and theological emphases. The accounts should not be flattened into one composite narrative.
- The study relies primarily on the KJV text. Some secondary sources use modern translations; where translation choices affect interpretation, the difference must be stated.
- John 9:3 has generated interpretive discussion about punctuation and the relation between the man's condition and the manifestation of God's works. The study does not claim that this verse supplies a universal explanation for disability.
- John 5:1–18 has multiple scholarly interpretations concerning the pool, Sabbath, and the following discourse. Bryan's interpretation is included as one scholarly argument, not a consensus claim.
- The exact historical circumstances of individual miracle events cannot all be independently reconstructed from the available sources.
- The term “resurrection” is sometimes used broadly in commentary for Lazarus, but the study distinguishes his restoration to mortal life from Christ's resurrection.
- The historical and scientific method discussion does not claim that science has confirmed Scripture or that the lack of a scientific mechanism disproves the Gospel testimony.
- No GUA-W architecture is revised.

## 22. Unresolved questions

1. How does Matthew's account of the cleansing of the leper relate to the purity and restoration themes of Leviticus 13–14?
2. How does Mark's arrangement of forgiveness before healing shape the reader's understanding of Jesus' authority?
3. How does Luke's use of Elijah and Elisha traditions contribute to the identity question in Luke 7:11–17?
4. What is the exact relationship between the healing at Bethesda and Jesus' discourse on the Father's work in John 5:17–30?
5. How should John 9:3 be translated and punctuated, and how does that affect the relationship between suffering and the manifestation of God's works?
6. How does the healed man's developing testimony in John 9 model witness under opposition?
7. How should the delay in John 11:5–6 be read alongside the explicit statement that Jesus loved the family?
8. What is the relationship between Jesus' tears, His compassion, and His authority over death in John 11?
9. How does the raising of Lazarus lead narratively to the decision to put Jesus to death (John 11:45–53)?
10. How does John 20:30–31 define the relation between signs, belief, Jesus' identity, and life?
11. How do the selected miracles relate to the messianic signs in Isaiah 35:3–6 and 61:1–3?
12. How should the church distinguish testimony about a miracle from proof of a minister's character, doctrine, or authority?
13. How can historical research evaluate testimony without pretending that its methods settle every theological or metaphysical question?
