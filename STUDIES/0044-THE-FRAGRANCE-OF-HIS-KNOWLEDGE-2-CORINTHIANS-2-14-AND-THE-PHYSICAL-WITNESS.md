## 1. Why this verse enters the investigation

The question carried forward from Study 0043 is:

> **Are natural laws the keyholes through which we investigate the physical expression of the Word, even when the process itself is beyond ordinary scientific explanation?**

The user now directs attention to 2 Corinthians 2:14:

> “Now thanks be unto God, which always causeth us to triumph in Christ, and maketh manifest the savour of his knowledge by us in every place.” (KJV)

This verse introduces a particularly useful relationship for GUA-W investigation. Paul describes God's knowledge being made manifest “by us,” using the language of savour or fragrance. The verse places together:
- God as the one to whom thanks are given and who leads the work;
- Christ, in whom the triumph occurs;
- human witnesses as the means “by” whom the knowledge is made manifest;
- knowledge of God becoming known in every place;
- fragrance as the sensory image through which Paul describes this manifestation.

The verse does not say that the Gospel is literally a chemical scent. It uses a physical sensory experience as an image for the public spread and recognition of the knowledge of God in Christ.

That distinction is the first key to this study.

## 3. Context: the triumph and the fragrance

In 2 Corinthians 2:12–13 Paul describes his arrival at Troas, an open door for the Gospel, his lack of rest because he did not find Titus, and his departure for Macedonia. Verse 14 then turns to thanksgiving to God.

The language of triumph evokes the imagery of a public victory procession. Ancient triumphal processions could include incense and aromatic substances. The image gives Paul's readers a public, sensory picture: a victory is being displayed, and its fragrance spreads beyond the immediate participants.

Commentators differ over how precisely Paul positions himself in the triumphal procession—whether emphasizing the victorious procession, the captive led by the victor, or the paradoxical combination of suffering apostolic service and Christ's victory. The important point for this investigation is that Paul attributes the triumph to God and places the manifestation of God's knowledge through human ministry.

The fragrance image continues in verses 15–16. Paul says that the apostolic witnesses are a sweet savour of Christ to God, while the same proclamation is encountered differently by those who are being saved and those who are perishing.

This is not a claim that the Gospel has two chemical compositions. The difference concerns the response and significance of the message to its hearers.

Verse 17 then draws an ethical boundary: Paul rejects corrupting or peddling God's Word and describes speaking sincerely, as from God, in God's sight, in Christ.

The passage therefore concerns more than the spread of information. It concerns the Source, the message, the means of its manifestation, the response of hearers, and the integrity of the witness.

## 4. The wording: “maketh manifest” and “by us”

Two expressions in verse 14 deserve attention.

### 4.1 “Maketh manifest”

The Greek verb translated “maketh manifest” is *phaneroō* (φανερόω), commonly used for making something visible, evident, or known. In this context it describes the making-known of the savour of God's knowledge.

The word does not require that the knowledge itself become a material object. The phrase describes manifestation: what was not being perceived or recognized is made known.

This connects with the broader GUA-W interest in the difference between a Source and the witness through whom something is revealed. The manifestation is real, but the instrument through which it is manifested is not thereby its originating Source.

### 4.2 “By us”

The Greek phrase is *di’ hēmōn* (δι’ ἡμῶν), “through us” or “by us.” The preposition *dia* with the genitive commonly expresses agency or means.

The wording matters. Paul does not say that the knowledge originates in the apostles. He says God makes the savour of His knowledge manifest through them.

This supports a distinction already central to GUA-W:

**Source ≠ instrument.  
Author ≠ witness.  
The means through which something is manifested does not become the originating cause of what is manifested.**

The human witness is involved. The human witness is not the Author.

## 5. The physical keyhole: what is fragrance?

Paul's metaphor draws upon a physical phenomenon that can be investigated.

In ordinary physical terms, an odor is perceived when volatile chemical molecules enter the air, reach the nasal cavity, and interact with olfactory sensory receptors. Those receptors initiate cellular signaling, which is transmitted through neural pathways and processed by the nervous system.

The process includes several distinguishable physical events:

1. A substance contains or releases molecules with suitable volatility.
2. Some molecules enter the surrounding air.
3. Molecules are transported through the air and reach the nose.
4. Odorant molecules interact with olfactory receptor proteins in sensory neurons.
5. Receptor activation initiates intracellular signaling.
6. Electrical activity is transmitted through olfactory pathways.
7. The nervous system processes the signal as a smell.

This is a physical sensory chain. Chemistry describes odorant molecules and their properties. Physics describes such matters as molecular motion, diffusion, transport, and interactions. Biology and neuroscience describe receptors, cellular signaling, neural transmission, and perception.

The physical process gives us a legitimate keyhole through which to investigate the word-picture Paul uses.

It does not establish that Paul or the Gospel literally emits a measurable odor.

## 6. What the metaphor does—and does not—authorize

### What the passage does say

- God is thanked as the one who leads the work in Christ.
- God makes the savour of His knowledge manifest through the apostolic witnesses.
- The manifestation extends “in every place.”
- Paul describes the witnesses as a sweet savour of Christ to God.
- The message is encountered in relation to life and death.
- Those who speak must not corrupt God's Word; they must speak sincerely and in God's sight.

### What the passage does not say

- That the Gospel is a volatile chemical compound.
- That every Christian emits a measurable spiritual fragrance.
- That a particular olfactory receptor detects the knowledge of God.
- That the natural process of smelling explains spiritual revelation.
- That the physical mechanism of odor perception is the cause of the spiritual message.
- That the metaphor can be used as scientific proof of a spiritual claim.

The physical process illuminates the metaphor's sensory force. It does not replace the metaphor's theological meaning.

This is the same discipline applied in Study 0043: distinguish the reported or intended meaning from a mechanism that the text does not claim.

## 7. A deeper connection: one fragrance, different reception

Paul's language in verses 15–16 is especially important:

> “To the one we are the savour of death unto death; and to the other the savour of life unto life.”

A fragrance is encountered by a perceiver, but Paul's point is not that the same physical odor chemically changes into two different substances depending on the observer. The message of Christ is proclaimed; its significance is encountered differently by those who are being saved and those who are perishing.

The passage therefore distinguishes:
- the message being made manifest;
- the witness through whom it is made manifest;
- the hearer who encounters it;
- the response or significance of that encounter.

This is relevant to the repository's investigations of reception, attention, expectation, memory, and mistaken witness. A witness may faithfully communicate what has been entrusted to him, while the reception and response of hearers differ.

The difference in reception must not be confused with a change in the Source or corruption of the message.

Verse 17 supplies the safeguard: the witness must not corrupt the Word to produce a preferred response.

## 8. The keyhole proposition, refined through 2 Corinthians 2:14

The earlier working hypothesis was:

> “Anytime that the Word interacted with the Physical World at least ‘a’ Natural Law was used.”

This passage requires us to distinguish two kinds of manifestation.

### 8.1 Physical manifestation

A physical fragrance is manifested through physical properties and processes. Its molecules, transport, receptor interactions, and neural signaling are open to scientific investigation.

### 8.2 Testimonial or communicative manifestation

Paul's phrase “maketh manifest the savour of his knowledge by us” describes God's knowledge being made known through human witnesses. The fragrance is a metaphor for the spread and character of that knowledge, drawing upon a familiar physical sensory experience.

The passage therefore gives us two related but non-identical levels:
- a physical phenomenon used as the image;
- a spiritual and communicative reality described through that image.

We must not collapse the two.

### 8.3 Revised investigative proposition

**When Scripture describes the Word's interaction with physical Creation, investigate the physical manifestation through the relevant sciences. When Scripture uses a physical phenomenon as a metaphor for spiritual manifestation, investigate both the physical phenomenon and the textual meaning of the metaphor, without treating the metaphor as a claim that the spiritual reality is physically identical to its image.**

This is a methodological refinement, not a replacement of the user's original hypothesis. The universal claim that every interaction involves “a natural law” remains open for investigation.

## 10. The vessel and the witness: “through us” is not “from us”

The phrase “by us” deserves to remain in the center of the study.

A vessel can carry something without originating what it carries. A messenger can communicate a word without becoming its author. A witness can make something known without becoming the source of the truth witnessed.

This does not make the vessel irrelevant. The vessel's condition and conduct matter because the message can be misrepresented by a corrupt witness. Paul's warning in verse 17 shows that the integrity of the means is a real responsibility.

The relationship is therefore not:
- the human witness creates the knowledge;
- the human witness controls the Source;
- the human witness changes the Word to secure a desired response.

Rather, the passage describes God making His knowledge manifest through witnesses who must speak sincerely in Christ and in God's sight.

This is consistent with the GUA-W distinction: **the derivative may participate in manifestation, but participation does not confer authorship.**

## 15. Conclusion

The user's question opens an important distinction.

**Natural laws are keyholes for investigating physical Creation. They are not keys that unlock authority over the Creator.**

In 2 Corinthians 2:14, Paul uses fragrance—a physical sensory phenomenon—to describe the manifestation of the knowledge of God through human witnesses. The physical science of smell can explain the sensory image. It cannot turn the Gospel into a chemical odor or explain the spiritual origin of the knowledge being proclaimed.

The most important GUA-W correspondence in this passage may be the words **“by us.”**

God makes manifest. The witness participates. The witness does not originate.

The next study should examine 2 Corinthians 4:6, where Paul moves from the language of fragrance to the language of light:

> “For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.”

That passage brings Genesis 1:3, physical light, inner illumination, knowledge, and witness into one investigative field. It must be studied carefully without confusing created light with the knowledge of God's glory.
