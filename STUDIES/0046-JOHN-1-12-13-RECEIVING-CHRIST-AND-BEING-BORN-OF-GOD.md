## 1. Question

Study 0045 examined Jesus' statement that no one can come to Him unless the Father draws that person (John 6:44), and that the one who has heard and learned from the Father comes to Him (6:45). The next investigation is:

> **What does John mean when he says that those who receive Christ are given the right to become children of God, and that they are born not of blood, the will of the flesh, or the will of man, but of God?**

John 1:12–13 places two statements together:
- people receive Christ and believe in His name;
- their birth as children of God is attributed not to natural descent or human will, but to God.

The question is how these statements relate without erasing either one. Does the text present human reception as real? Yes. Does it present human will as the originating source of the new birth? No. Does the grammar alone settle every proposed chronology of regeneration and faith? That requires further examination and should not be assumed.

## 2. Scope and boundaries

This study examines John 1:9–14, with particular attention to verses 12–13. It compares the passage with John 3:3–8, John 6:35–45 and 64–65, 1 Corinthians 2:10–16, James 1:17–18, 1 Peter 1:3 and 1:23, and 1 John 5:1.

It does not attempt to settle every debate concerning the order of regeneration and faith, the relation of divine sovereignty and human response, or the full meaning of adoption. It does not infer a biological or physical mechanism for spiritual birth. It does not turn the words “receive,” “believe,” or “born” into new GUA-W runtime operators.

## 3. Continuity and governing foundation

Study 0044 examined 1 Corinthians 2:14: the natural person does not receive the things of the Spirit of God because they are spiritually discerned. Study 0045 examined John 6:37–45: the Father gives, draws, and teaches; people hear, learn, and come to Christ; the Son receives and raises them.

John 1:12–13 now brings receiving Christ and birth from God into the same compact statement.

The governing GUA-W principle remains:

**God alone is Original Cause. Everything else is derivative witness. The derivative cannot alter the Source.**

The governing relationship remains:

**Author → Word → Creation → Witness.**

No GUA-W layer, operation, state, or formal definition is added or altered by this study. The terms “receiving,” “believing,” and “born of God” are being examined as scriptural language, not proposed as new architecture.

## 4. Read the immediate context: John 1:1–18

### 4.1 The Word is before the world

John begins:

> “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” (John 1:1, KJV)

The Prologue identifies the Word as pre-existent, in relationship with God, and divine. Verse 3 says all things were made by Him; verse 4 speaks of life in Him; verses 9–14 describe the true Light coming into the world and the Word being made flesh.

This context matters. John 1:12–13 is not a detached statement about self-improvement or a person's ability to construct a new identity. It follows the coming of the Light and the rejection of the Word by the world and by His own people.

### 4.2 The world did not know Him — verses 10–11

John says the world was made by Him, yet the world did not know Him. He came unto His own, and His own received Him not.

The contrast is between the Creator and the created world, and between the rightful arrival of the Word and His rejection. The Creator is not dependent upon the world's recognition to be who He is.

### 4.3 The contrast — verse 12

> “But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name:” (John 1:12, KJV)

The opening “But” marks a contrast with the rejection in verse 11. Not all rejected Him. Some received Him; John describes these as those who believe on His name.

Verse 12 contains both a human response and a divine gift:
- they received Him;
- they believe on His name;
- He gave them power/right to become children of God.

The response is not erased. Nor is the gift attributed to the recipients as its source.

### 4.4 The source of the birth — verse 13

> “Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.” (John 1:13, KJV)

John names three things from which this birth does not originate, then gives the positive source: God.

The verse does not deny that human beings are involved in ordinary physical birth. It distinguishes birth as God's children from natural ancestry, human reproductive desire, and human initiative. The birth under discussion is from God.

### 4.5 The Word made flesh — verse 14

> “And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us...” (John 1:14, KJV)

Verse 14 returns to the identity and coming of the Word. The One whom people receive is not an abstract principle or a humanly invented idea. He is the Word who became flesh and dwelt among us.

The witness language also becomes explicit: “we beheld his glory.” The Prologue moves from the Word's identity and action to the testimony of those who saw His glory. The witness reports; the witness does not become the Word or the source of His glory.

## 5. Verse 12: receiving, believing, and the right to become

### 5.1 “Received him”

The Greek verb is *lambanō* (λαμβάνω), here in the aorist form *elabon* (ἔλαβον), translated “received.” In this context it means welcoming or accepting the Word, not merely registering that He exists.

John's next phrase identifies the recipients as those “that believe on his name.” Receiving Christ and believing in His name are closely joined in the verse. They are not two unrelated achievements.

### 5.2 “Believe on his name”

The Greek construction is *pisteuō eis to onoma autou* (πιστεύω εἰς τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ), literally “believe into his name.” John's use of “believe in” with *eis* commonly expresses trust directed toward Jesus Himself. It is more than acknowledging a fact about Him; it includes reliance upon and allegiance to His person.

“Name” in this biblical context is not a sound or verbal formula that mechanically produces an outcome. It identifies the person and His revealed identity. This aligns with the repository's earlier examination of acting in the name of Jesus Christ: His name is not a technique that gives a human witness independent control.

### 5.3 “He gave them power” — *exousia*

The Greek noun translated “power” is *exousia* (ἐξουσία), commonly meaning authority, right, or legitimate privilege. In John 1:12 the emphasis is not on physical force or an energy supplied to the believer. It is the right or authority to become children of God.

The grammatical subject of “gave” is Christ, the Word in the Prologue's flow. The right is given; it is not self-issued by the recipient.

### 5.4 “To become children of God”

The Greek phrase is *tekna theou genesthai* (τέκνα θεοῦ γενέσθαι): “to become children of God.” John uses *tekna* (“children”), not the title he reserves for Jesus as the unique Son. Believers' relationship to God is received; it is not identical to Christ's unique sonship.

The verse therefore does not say that people make themselves God's children by exercising an independent power. It says Christ gives the right to those who receive and believe in Him.

## 6. Verse 13: the birth is “of God”

### 6.1 “Not of blood”

The Greek phrase is *ouk ex haimatōn* (οὐκ ἐξ αἱμάτων), literally “not from bloods.” It excludes natural descent as the source of this birth. The plural has been explained in different ways, including reference to human ancestry or the union of parental lines. The secure point in context is that descent by ordinary human generation does not make someone a child of God.

John is not denying the reality or goodness of ordinary human birth. He is saying that God's children do not acquire this birth through ancestry.

### 6.2 “Nor of the will of the flesh”

The phrase is *oude ek thelēmatos sarkos* (οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος σαρκός): not from the will or desire of the flesh. The word *sarx* (“flesh”) can refer to the human or physical sphere; it should not automatically be treated as a synonym for the physical body being evil.

The statement excludes natural human desire or fleshly initiative as the source of this spiritual birth.

### 6.3 “Nor of the will of man”

The phrase is *oude ek thelēmatos andros* (οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος ἀνδρός): not from the will of a man. The term *anēr* can mean man or husband. Interpretations differ on whether the phrase specifically evokes a husband's initiative or human will more broadly. Either way, John denies that the new birth originates in human decision or human initiative.

The two references to will should not be collapsed into an unsupported claim that human beings never make decisions or never respond. Verse 12 has already spoken of receiving and believing. Verse 13 identifies the source of the birth, not the nonexistence of the response.

### 6.4 “But of God”

The final phrase is *all' ek theou* (ἀλλ’ ἐκ θεοῦ): “but of God.” The preposition *ek* marks source or origin. The contrast is emphatic: not from natural descent, not from human desire, not from human initiative, but from God.

The verb *egennēthēsan* (ἐγεννήθησαν) is an aorist passive form of *gennaō* (γεννάω), “to beget” or “to give birth”; in the passive, “to be born.” The passive voice presents the recipients as those who are born, not as the ones who generate their own birth.

**Direct finding:** John attributes the origin of this birth to God. Human ancestry and human will are explicitly excluded as its originating source.

## 7. A textual-critical note: who is “born” in verse 13?

The KJV follows the plural reading: “Which were born.” The plural relative pronoun and plural verb refer to the people described in verse 12, the recipients and believers.

A singular reading has existed in some Latin witnesses and was interpreted by some as referring to Christ's birth. However, the Greek manuscript evidence overwhelmingly supports the plural reading, and major modern critical editions retain it. Murray J. Harris's grammatical guide notes the plural reading across the Greek manuscripts and explains why the singular reading is generally regarded as secondary.

This matters because the passage is not saying in verse 13 that Christ was born of God in the same way the believers are born of God. John has already distinguished the unique Son from God's children. Verse 13 concerns the birth of those who receive and believe in Christ.

## 8. The relationship between verses 12 and 13

The two verses must be held together.

| Verse 12 | Verse 13 |
|---|---|
| They receive Christ. | Their birth is not from natural descent. |
| They believe in His name. | Their birth is not from the will of the flesh. |
| Christ gives them the right to become God's children. | Their birth is not from the will of man. |
| A real human response is named. | God is named as the source of the birth. |

John does not erase the believing response in verse 12. He does not allow that response to be mistaken for the originating cause of the birth in verse 13.

A careful summary is:

**Those who receive and believe in Christ are given the right to become God's children; their birth as God's children originates not in human ancestry or will, but in God.**

### 8.1 Does verse 13 establish a precise chronological order?

Not by itself. The aorist passive “were born” identifies the birth as an accomplished divine action in John's statement. The grammar does not, by tense alone, provide a detailed timeline explaining the exact temporal relation between regeneration and the act of faith.

Some theological traditions infer from verse 13 that God's regenerative action must logically precede faith. Others understand the passage as describing the divine origin and character of the believer's new birth without specifying a temporal sequence between faith and regeneration. The study should not pretend that a verb tense alone settles that debate.

What the text does establish is sufficient and clear: the new birth is “of God,” not self-originated by human will.

## 9. Canonical comparison

### 9.1 John 3:3–8 — born again / born of the Spirit

Jesus tells Nicodemus that a man must be born again (or from above) to see the kingdom of God. He distinguishes birth of flesh from birth of the Spirit and compares the Spirit's work to the wind: its sound is heard, but its origin and movement are not controlled by the observer.

This comparison supports John 1:13's distinction between natural birth and birth from God. It does not authorize us to identify the Spirit with wind as a physical mechanism. The wind is an analogy for the Spirit's sovereign and unmastered action, not a scientific explanation of regeneration.

### 9.2 John 6:37–45 — the Father's giving and drawing

John 6 states that those given by the Father come to the Son, and that no one can come unless the Father draws him. John 1:12–13 likewise holds human reception together with divine initiative and divine origin.

The passages are complementary, but their wording is not identical:
- John 6: the Father gives, draws, and teaches; the person hears, learns, and comes to Christ.
- John 1: the person receives and believes; Christ gives the right to become God's child; the birth is from God.

Both resist treating the human response as an independent original cause. Neither should be forced into a mechanical chronology not supplied by the text.

### 9.3 1 Corinthians 2:10–16 — spiritual discernment

Paul says the things of God's Spirit are spiritually discerned. This is not the same statement as John 1:13, but both passages deny that natural human capacity is the originating source of spiritual reception and knowledge.

John's emphasis is birth from God and receiving the Word. Paul's emphasis is the Spirit's revelation and discernment of God's wisdom. The connection is theological, not a claim that the two passages describe exactly the same operation.

### 9.4 James 1:17–18 — begotten by the word of truth

James says every good gift comes from above, from the Father of lights, and that “of his own will begat he us with the word of truth.” This is a particularly relevant comparison: God's will is named as the source of the begetting, and the word of truth is named in connection with it.

James's statement reinforces the priority of God's will without erasing the role of the word through which He brings about the birth.

### 9.5 1 Peter 1:3, 23 — begotten again

Peter blesses God who, according to His abundant mercy, has begotten believers again unto a lively hope by the resurrection of Jesus Christ. Later he describes them as born again, not of corruptible seed but incorruptible, by the word of God.

These passages add two explicit connections: God's mercy and Christ's resurrection (1 Peter 1:3), and the enduring Word of God (1 Peter 1:23). They support the distinction between natural generation and new birth from God.

### 9.6 1 John 5:1 — believing and being born of God

John writes that whosoever believes that Jesus is the Christ is born of God. The perfect passive form translated “is born” is important to the discussion, but it should not be used as a shortcut to a complete chronology without examining the whole argument and Johannine usage.

Together, John 1:12–13 and 1 John 5:1 connect believing in Christ with being born of God. The source of the birth remains God.

## 10. The will: what is denied and what is not denied

Because the user has repeatedly investigated the will, this distinction deserves precision.

John 1:13 denies that the new birth originates:
1. in ancestry or bloodline;
2. in the desire or will of the flesh;
3. in the will or initiative of man.

It does not say:
- that a person never wills anything;
- that a person does not believe, receive, come, hear, or learn;
- that the commands and invitations of Scripture are unreal;
- that human response is meaningless;
- that the precise psychological process of willing has been explained;
- that a GUA-W diagram can model or reproduce God's act of giving new birth.

The text's focus is the source of spiritual birth. It places that source in God. This aligns with the repository's earlier investigation of Philippians 2:12–13, where God is said to work in believers both to will and to do, while the believers are still addressed with a real command.

## 12. The natural-law keyhole boundary

Study 0043 proposed investigating the physical manifestations of the Word through relevant natural sciences, without assuming that ordinary mechanisms explain every event. Studies 0044 and 0045 clarified that spiritual discernment and the Father's drawing must not be converted into invented physical mechanisms.

John 1:12–13 concerns receiving Christ and being born of God. It does not describe a measurable physical process by which God causes this birth. Ordinary human birth has biological mechanisms; John's point is that birth as God's child does not originate in those mechanisms or in human will.

Scientific disciplines can investigate biological reproduction, heredity, development, and the physical body. Those investigations do not, by themselves, establish or measure the spiritual birth John attributes to God.

Therefore:

**Natural laws are keyholes into the physical order. They are not a substitute for the spiritual truth that John identifies: the new birth is of God.**

An honest investigation can state the boundary without pretending to explain what the passage does not explain.

## 14. Findings by evidence status

### Directly stated by Scripture

1. The Word is the true Light who came into the world (John 1:9–10).
2. The world did not know Him, and His own people did not receive Him (1:10–11).
3. Those who receive Him and believe in His name are given the right to become children of God (1:12).
4. Their birth is not from blood, the will of the flesh, or the will of man, but from God (1:13).
5. The Word became flesh and dwelt among us; witnesses beheld His glory (1:14).

### Supported grammatical observations

1. *Lambanō* is the verb translated “received.”
2. *Pisteuō eis* describes believing in / trusting in Christ's name.
3. *Exousia* means right, authority, or privilege in this context.
4. *Tekna theou* means children of God.
5. *Egennēthēsan* is a plural aorist passive form of *gennaō*, “were born.”
6. The plural reading of verse 13 refers to the believers described in verse 12 and is supported by the Greek manuscript tradition.

### Supported interpretation

John joins a real human response—receiving and believing—with a divine grant and a birth whose source is God. Human response is not identified as the originating cause of the new birth.

### GUA-W correspondence

The passage supports the distinction between what is given by God and what is received by the witness. It preserves the Word's identity and priority, Christ's granting authority, and God's role as the source of new birth. These remain provisional correspondences, not architecture changes.

### Not established

- A complete chronology of regeneration and faith from John 1:12–13 alone.
- That human response is unreal or irrelevant.
- That biological reproduction explains spiritual birth.
- That natural laws can measure or produce the new birth.
- That GUA-W can model or reproduce God's act of regeneration.
- That a single Greek tense settles every theological dispute.

## 17. Conclusion

John 1:12–13 brings the previous investigation into sharper focus.

John 6:44 says no one can come to Christ unless the Father draws him. John 1:12 says those who receive Christ and believe in His name are given the right to become children of God. John 1:13 then identifies the source of their birth: not ancestry, not the will of the flesh, not the will of man, but God.

The passage does not erase the person who receives. It does not make believing imaginary. But it refuses to make human will the originating source of the new birth.

**The person receives. Christ gives. God begets. The witness testifies.**

This is a strong textual correspondence with GUA-W's Source and derivative-witness distinction. It does not make GUA-W the explanation of regeneration, and it does not add an architecture component.

The next investigation should move to John 3:3–8, where Jesus directly distinguishes birth of the flesh from birth of the Spirit and uses the wind as an analogy for an action whose source and movement are not controlled by the observer.
