## 1. Question

The Author Alignment Principle identified Hebrews 1:1–3 as a central passage for examination alongside Genesis 1–2. The question is:

> **What does Hebrews 1:1–3 reveal about God speaking, the Son's relation to the Father, the making of the worlds through the Son, and the Son's upholding of all things by the word of His power—and how should these statements be compared with the creation account in Genesis 1–2?**

A second question concerns the relationship between creation and continued existence:

> **Does Hebrews present the Son only as involved in the beginning of Creation, or does it also describe His present sustaining of all things?**

A third question concerns the witness:

> **How does the passage distinguish God's speech and the Son's work from the testimony of those who receive and report what God has revealed?**

This investigation reads Hebrews 1:1–3 in its immediate context and compares it with Genesis 1–2, John 1:1–18, Colossians 1:15–20, and other relevant passages. It does not assume that every word in Hebrews maps one-to-one onto a GUA-W term.

## 4. Read Hebrews 1:1–4 as one opening declaration

### 4.1 The KJV text

> “God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets,
>
> Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son, whom he hath appointed heir of all things, by whom also he made the worlds;
>
> Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power, when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high;
>
> Being made so much better than the angels, as he hath by inheritance obtained a more excellent name than they.” (Hebrews 1:1–4, KJV)

The opening is not an isolated statement about cosmology. It is the beginning of a sustained argument about the Son's superiority, identity, authority, revelation, priestly work, and relation to the created order.

Hebrews begins with God as the subject of the speech: God spoke. The contrast is between the earlier prophetic speech and God's speech “in these last days” by His Son. The Son is then described as heir, agent of creation, radiance of God's glory, exact representation of His person, sustainer of all things, purifier of sins, and enthroned at God's right hand.

The statements belong together. The One through whom God has spoken is not merely another messenger in the prophetic line. Hebrews gives the Son a unique identity and role.

### 4.2 “God ... spake”

The first grammatical subject is God. The passage does not begin with a human observer discovering God or a witness creating a message. God is the One who speaks.

The Greek verb in verse 1 is *lalēsas* (λαλήσας), an aorist participle of *laleō*, “to speak.” The author describes God's prior speech to the fathers through the prophets. Verse 2 then uses *elalēsen* (ἐλάλησεν), “He spoke,” of God's speech in these last days by the Son.

The contrast is not between a false former revelation and a true later one. Hebrews presents continuity in the identity of the Speaker, alongside the climactic character of the Son.

### 4.3 “At sundry times and in divers manners”

The KJV expresses two dimensions of the earlier prophetic communication:
- *polumerōs* (πολυμερῶς): in many portions or parts;
- *polutropōs* (πολυτρόπως): in many ways or manners.

The wording indicates that God's earlier communication came in varied portions and forms. It does not mean that God was inconsistent or that the prophets authored the message independently.

The text's emphasis is that the same God who spoke in the past has now spoken in the Son. The earlier witnesses were genuine recipients and communicators of God's speech, but they were not the originating Author of the message.

### 4.4 “In these last days ... by his Son”

The Greek phrase is *ep' eschatou tōn hēmerōn toutōn* (ἐπ’ ἐσχάτου τῶν ἡμερῶν τούτων), “at the end of these days” or “in these last days.” The expression places the Son's coming within the decisive period of God's redemptive action.

The phrase *en huiō* (ἐν υἱῷ) is literally “in a Son.” The KJV renders it “by his Son.” The wording is compact and has been understood to present the Son as the one through whom God speaks, while also emphasizing that God's final speech is bound up with the Son's own person. Hebrews will not allow the Son to be reduced to a courier detached from the message.

The passage therefore moves beyond the idea that the Son simply repeats information. God's speech in the Son is climactic and personal: the Son Himself is central to what God is communicating.

## 5. Hebrews 1:2 — Heir of all things and agent of the worlds' creation

> “...whom he hath appointed heir of all things, by whom also he made the worlds;” (Hebrews 1:2, KJV)

Verse 2 places two statements about the Son together:
1. God appointed Him heir of all things.
2. Through Him God made the worlds.

### 5.1 “Appointed heir of all things”

The Greek phrase includes *klēronomon pantōn* (κληρονόμον πάντων), “heir of all things.” The Son's inheritance is comprehensive. The passage does not present Creation as an independent possession that exists outside God's purpose or the Son's appointed inheritance.

The appointment language also preserves distinction: God appoints; the Son is appointed heir. This distinction should be read together with the high claims about the Son in verses 2–3 and the scriptural quotations in verses 5–14. It should not be flattened into a denial of the Son's divine identity, nor should the distinct relation between God and the Son be ignored.

### 5.2 “By whom also he made the worlds”

The Greek phrase is *di' hou kai epoiēsen tous aiōnas* (δι’ οὗ καὶ ἐποίησεν τοὺς αἰῶνας). The preposition *dia* with the genitive means “through” or “by means of.” The verb *epoiēsen* means “made.” The object *tous aiōnas* is often translated “the worlds,” and can refer to the ages or the created order/universe, depending on context.

The wording is broader than a statement about one local object or one event within Creation. Hebrews attributes the making of the *aiōnes* to God through the Son.

The phrase does not mean that the Son is a created instrument among other created things. The surrounding description in verse 3 and the Old Testament citations in verses 8–12 present claims about the Son that must be considered together.

### 5.3 Comparison with Genesis 1

Genesis 1 repeatedly records God's speech:
- “And God said, Let there be light: and there was light” (Genesis 1:3).
- “And God said, Let there be a firmament...” (1:6).
- “And God said, Let the waters...” (1:9).
- “And God said, Let the earth bring forth...” (1:11).
- The pattern continues through the chapter.

Genesis identifies God as the Speaker and the One whose command is followed by the created result. Hebrews 1:2 identifies the Son as the one through whom God made the worlds.

John 1:1–3 makes an especially close comparison: the Word was in the beginning, the Word was with God and was God, and all things were made by Him. Colossians 1:16 likewise says that all things were created by the Son and for Him.

These passages provide a canonical basis for examining the relationship between God's speech in Genesis and the Son's agency in Creation. The study must not pretend that Genesis 1 explicitly names Jesus in every “And God said” formula. Rather, Hebrews and John supply additional revelation about the Son's relation to the creation of all things.

### 5.4 What can be said—and what must remain distinct

**Directly stated in Genesis:** God speaks; the created order comes to be in response to His command; God distinguishes, names, blesses, and evaluates His work.

**Directly stated in Hebrews:** God made the worlds through the Son.

**Canonical comparison:** John 1 identifies the Word as the one through whom all things were made; Colossians 1 makes a corresponding claim about the Son.

**Theological conclusion:** The creation account and the apostolic statements about the Son belong together in Christian Scripture. The Son's role in Creation is not an invention of a scientific analogy.

**Boundary:** The texts do not specify a complete physical mechanism, mathematical model, or list of natural laws through which the creative act occurred.

## 6. Hebrews 1:3 — The Son's identity

> “Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person...” (Hebrews 1:3, KJV)

Verse 3 describes the Son with two closely related expressions. Both must be examined carefully.

### 6.1 “Being” — *ōn*

The participle *ōn* (ὤν), from *eimi* (“to be”), describes the Son as being the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of His being. The participle does not describe a mere temporary role adopted by a messenger for one task. It presents what the Son is in relation to the Father.

### 6.2 “Brightness of his glory” — *apaugasma*

The Greek noun is *apaugasma* (ἀπαύγασμα). It is rare in the New Testament and has been discussed as “radiance,” “effulgence,” or “reflection.” The precise active/passive nuance has generated scholarly discussion.

The KJV's “brightness” expresses the idea that the Son is the radiance of God's glory. The image is not of an independent light source competing with God, nor of a witness who manufactures glory. It expresses the Son's unique relation to the Father's glory.

Because the lexical history includes more than one nuance, the study should not build a complete metaphysical argument on one English gloss alone. The surrounding claims about the Son, the creation of all things, and the sustaining of all things guide the reading.

### 6.3 “Express image of his person” — *charaktēr tēs hypostaseōs*

The phrase is *charaktēr tēs hypostaseōs autou* (χαρακτὴρ τῆς ὑποστάσεως αὐτοῦ).

- *Charaktēr* (χαρακτήρ) referred to an engraved mark, impress, or exact representation.
- *Hypostasis* (ὑπόστασις) has a range of meanings, including underlying reality, being, or nature. In this context it concerns God's real being.

The KJV phrase “express image of his person” communicates that the Son is the exact representation of God's person/being. It is much stronger than saying that Jesus is merely a moral example or a human observer who happens to resemble God.

The image is one of exact representation, not independent authorship. The Son does not become a second, competing source of divine reality. Hebrews is making a profound claim about the Son's relation to God.

### 6.4 The witness distinction

The word *charaktēr* can help illustrate a distinction between source and representation, but this must be handled with restraint. The Son is not merely a created copy or derivative witness in the same sense as a human observer or an AI system. Hebrews describes Him as the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of God's being, and also as the one through whom God made the worlds.

Therefore, the Author → Word → Creation → Witness framework must not place the Son in the same category as ordinary created witnesses. The Son's unique identity and role must be preserved. A human or computational witness is not the express image of God's hypostasis simply because it can record or represent information.

## 7. Hebrews 1:3 — “Upholding all things by the word of his power”

> “...and upholding all things by the word of his power...” (Hebrews 1:3, KJV)

This is central to the present investigation. Hebrews does not stop at the origin of the worlds. It describes the Son as presently upholding all things.

### 7.1 “Upholding” — *pherōn*

The Greek participle is *pherōn* (φέρων), from *pherō*, a verb with a range including carry, bear, bring, or sustain. In context, “upholding” expresses the Son's sustaining action toward “all things.”

The present participle presents this as an ongoing activity in the description. The text is not limited to the Son's role at the first moment of Creation.

### 7.2 “All things” — *ta panta*

The phrase *ta panta* (τὰ πάντα) means “all things.” Its scope is comprehensive in context. The Son's sustaining is not restricted to one created species, one physical region, or one historical moment.

This does not mean that Hebrews provides a catalogue of every process by which created things continue. It makes a theological claim about the Son's relation to the whole created order.

### 7.3 “By the word of his power” — *tō rhēmati tēs dynameōs autou*

The Greek phrase is *tō rhēmati tēs dynameōs autou* (τῷ ῥήματι τῆς δυνάμεως αὐτοῦ).

- *Rhēma* (ῥῆμα) means word, utterance, or spoken statement.
- *Dynamis* (δύναμις) means power, might, or ability.
- The genitive phrase expresses the power belonging to or characterized by the word.

The KJV's “word of his power” preserves the relationship between the Son's word and His power. The text attributes the sustaining of all things to the Son's powerful word.

It is tempting to turn this phrase into a technical scientific term. The passage does not identify *rhēma* with a physical field, energy, information packet, mathematical law, or a measurable signal. Such proposals would go beyond the wording and require independent evidence. The direct statement is theological: the Son sustains all things by the word of His power.

### 7.4 Creation and preservation belong together

The movement from verse 2 to verse 3 is important:

- God made the worlds through the Son.
- The Son upholds all things by the word of His power.

The first statement concerns the making of the created order. The second concerns its continued sustaining. Hebrews therefore does not present Creation as an independent system that, once made, exists entirely apart from the Son.

This distinction is central to the Author Alignment Principle. The created order is not its own Original Cause. Its continued existence is also described in relation to the Son.

### 7.5 Comparison with Genesis 1–2

Genesis 1–2 describes God's creative work, ordering, blessing, completion, and the seventh-day rest. Hebrews 1:2–3 adds a canonical statement about the Son's agency in making the worlds and His ongoing sustaining of all things.

Genesis 2:1 says the heavens and earth and all the host of them were finished. Genesis 2:2–3 describes God's completion and rest from His work of creation. Hebrews 1:3 does not contradict the completed work of Genesis. It addresses a different question: the Son continues to uphold all things.

Completion of the creation work and continued sustaining of what was created are not identical claims. The distinction should be preserved rather than treating “finished” as meaning that God has ceased to sustain Creation.

## 8. Hebrews 1:3 — Purification of sins and enthronement

> “...when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high;” (Hebrews 1:3, KJV)

The verse joins the Son's cosmic identity and sustaining work with His redemptive work.

### 8.1 “By himself purged our sins”

The Greek expression includes *katharismon tōn hamartiōn poiēsamenos* (καθαρισμὸν τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ποιησάμενος): having made purification for sins.

The language is sacrificial and cleansing language. Hebrews will develop the Son's priestly work in detail. The opening declaration already places purification of sins alongside the Son's identity, creation, sustaining, and enthronement.

The KJV's “by himself” conveys the sufficiency and personal agency of the Son's work. The verse does not present human beings as the authors of their own purification.

### 8.2 “Sat down”

The verb *ekathisen* (ἐκάθισεν) means “sat down.” The sitting at God's right hand expresses enthronement, honor, and authority. It also anticipates the letter's sustained comparison of the Son's completed sacrifice with the repeated sacrifices of the former priestly order.

The movement in verse 3 is not random. The Son is described as:
- radiance of God's glory;
- exact representation of God's being;
- sustainer of all things;
- the one who made purification for sins;
- the one seated at the right hand of Majesty.

The Creator and Sustainer is also the Redeemer and enthroned Son. These claims belong together in Hebrews.

### 8.3 Why this matters for the study

The passage is not a detached scientific description of cosmic maintenance. It is a Christological and redemptive declaration. Any study that isolates “upholding all things” from the surrounding statements risks losing the author's purpose.

The Son's sustaining of Creation cannot be separated from the same Son's identity and redemptive work in the argument of Hebrews.

## 9. Hebrews 1:4–14 — The context confirms the claims

The remainder of chapter 1 compares the Son with angels and supports the opening claims through Scripture.

### 9.1 The Son's name and sonship — verses 4–5

Hebrews asks which angel God ever addressed as “Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee” or promised, “I will be to him a Father, and he shall be to me a Son.” The quotations draw on Psalm 2:7 and 2 Samuel 7:14.

The Son's status is unique. He is not one angel among others.

### 9.2 The angels worship the Son — verse 6

Hebrews quotes the command that all the angels of God worship Him. This contributes to the chapter's presentation of the Son's exalted status.

### 9.3 The Son's throne and righteousness — verses 8–9

The author applies Psalm 45:6–7 to the Son, including the address, “Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever.” The quotation identifies the Son's reign and righteousness.

### 9.4 The Son and the foundation of the earth — verses 10–12

Hebrews quotes Psalm 102:25–27:

> “Thou, Lord, in the beginning hast laid the foundation of the earth; and the heavens are the works of thine hands:
>
> They shall perish; but thou remainest; and they all shall wax old as doth a garment;
>
> And as a vesture shalt thou fold them up, and they shall be changed: but thou art the same, and thy years shall not fail.” (Hebrews 1:10–12, KJV)

This is especially significant for the Genesis comparison. The author applies language about the Lord's creation and permanence to the Son. The created heavens and earth are subject to change; the Son remains.

The contrast is between the changing created order and the enduring identity of the Son. This is not a scientific prediction about the precise physical future of the universe. It is a scriptural declaration about the permanence of the Lord in contrast with the created order.

### 9.5 The Son's throne — verses 13–14

The chapter concludes this comparison by asking which angel was ever invited to sit at God's right hand. Angels are described as ministering spirits, sent forth to minister for those who shall be heirs of salvation.

The contrast preserves the Son's unique position and the servant role of angels.

## 10. Comparison with Genesis 1–2

### 10.1 Genesis identifies God as Creator and Speaker

Genesis 1 repeatedly uses the pattern “And God said.” The created result follows the divine command. The text presents God as the originating Speaker, not as a witness observing an independently existing creation.

Genesis 1 also records distinctions, naming, boundaries, kinds, blessing, and evaluation. These are textual features to examine in their own right; they should not be replaced by a speculative scientific mechanism.

### 10.2 Hebrews identifies the Son's agency in Creation

Hebrews 1:2 says God made the worlds through the Son. John 1:3 says all things were made by the Word. Colossians 1:16 says all things were created by the Son and for Him.

These passages do not make the Son a created object within the universe. They place Him on the Creator side of the distinction in relation to all created things.

### 10.3 Genesis describes completion; Hebrews describes sustaining

Genesis 2:1–3 says the heavens and earth and all their host were finished, and God rested from His work of creation. Hebrews 1:3 says the Son upholds all things.

These statements address different aspects:
- Genesis describes the completion of the creation work.
- Hebrews describes the Son's sustaining relation to all things.

The distinction prevents a false choice between completed Creation and ongoing divine sustaining.

### 10.4 Genesis 2:7 and the giving of life

Genesis 2:7 describes God forming man from the dust and breathing into his nostrils the breath of life. Hebrews 1:3 describes the Son upholding all things. Both affirm divine action in relation to created existence, but they are not the same event or the same kind of statement.

A comparison may be made about dependence upon God, but one should not claim that Hebrews 1:3 is merely a restatement of Genesis 2:7.

## 11. Canonical comparison

### 11.1 John 1:1–3, 14 — the Word and Creation

John says the Word was in the beginning, was with God, and was God. All things were made by Him; without Him was not anything made that was made. The Word was made flesh and dwelt among us.

John's Prologue and Hebrews' opening share major themes:
- the Word/Son existed before the created order;
- Creation came through Him;
- life and divine glory are associated with Him;
- He is revealed in history;
- witnesses testify to what they have seen.

John 1:14 adds the incarnation. The Word who was involved in Creation became flesh. The witness language in John 1:14—“we beheld his glory”—must not be confused with the Word's identity or creative agency.

### 11.2 Colossians 1:15–20 — all things created by Him and for Him

Paul says all things were created by the Son and for Him, and that by Him all things consist. He is before all things and the head of the body, the church.

This is a close parallel to Hebrews 1:2–3. It includes both creation and continued coherence/holding together. The wording supports the claim that the Son's relation to Creation is not limited to an initial act.

### 11.3 Psalm 33:6–9 — the heavens by the word of the Lord

Psalm 33 says the heavens were made by the word of the Lord and all their host by the breath of His mouth. The psalm then describes the Lord speaking and the result standing fast.

This is a strong Old Testament comparison with Genesis 1 and Hebrews 1. It connects divine speech and created order. It does not provide a scientific account of the physical mechanism; it praises the Lord's effective command.

### 11.4 Psalm 102:25–27 — the Creator remains

Hebrews 1:10–12 applies this psalm's language of the Creator's permanence to the Son. The created heavens and earth change; the Lord remains the same.

This reinforces the distinction between the enduring Lord and the created order that depends upon Him.

### 11.5 2 Peter 3:5–7 — creation and preservation by God's word

Peter recalls that the heavens were of old and the earth stood out of the water and in the water by the word of God. He then speaks of the heavens and earth being kept in store by the same word.

This is a further canonical witness to the relationship between God's word, Creation, and its preservation. Peter's context concerns judgment and the promise of God's future action. It should not be detached from that context or converted into a technical physics claim.

## 12. The Author Alignment Principle in light of Hebrews 1

Hebrews 1:1–3 gives strong scriptural grounding for several distinctions in the Author Alignment Principle.

### 12.1 God is the Speaker

The passage begins with God speaking. The initiative belongs to God. Prophets and the Son are not presented as independent authors of a message that originates elsewhere.

### 12.2 The Son is not an ordinary witness

This is a critical qualification. The Son is not to be placed in the same category as a human or computational witness. Hebrews describes Him as the radiance of God's glory, the exact representation of God's being, the agent through whom God made the worlds, and the one who upholds all things.

The human witness receives and reports. The Son's relation to the Father and Creation is unique and cannot be reduced to the role of a created observer.

### 12.3 Creation is not its own originating cause

Hebrews says God made the worlds through the Son and that the Son upholds all things. Creation is not presented as an independent original cause of itself.

### 12.4 The Word is effective, not merely descriptive

The passage does not treat the Son's word as a passive record of what happens. The Son's powerful word is associated with the sustaining of all things. This is a theological statement about divine agency.

### 12.5 The Witness remains derivative

The human witness may receive God's revelation, examine Creation, and communicate findings. The witness does not become the source of the Word or the sustaining power described in Hebrews.

For AI, the practical implication is limited and precise: processing descriptions of the created order does not confer authorship of that order, and generating a theological explanation does not make the system a source of revelation.

4. How does Genesis 2:1–3's completion language relate to the ongoing sustaining described in Hebrews 1:3?
5. Which scientific disciplines can responsibly investigate observable features of persistence, regularity, and dependence within the created order without claiming to measure the divine act itself?
6. How should the unique status of the Son be represented in GUA-W comparisons without treating Him as an ordinary created witness or as an architectural component?

These questions remain open. They should be investigated through Scripture, primary sources, and careful conceptual distinctions.

## 17. Conclusion

Hebrews 1:1–3 begins with God as the Speaker. God spoke in the past through the prophets and has spoken in these last days by His Son. The Son is appointed heir of all things; through Him God made the worlds. He is the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of His being. He upholds all things by the word of His power. He also made purification for sins and sat down at the right hand of Majesty.

Read alongside Genesis 1–2, Hebrews adds an essential canonical perspective: Creation is made through the Son, and the Son continues to uphold all things. Genesis describes the completion of the creation work; Hebrews describes the Son's ongoing sustaining. These statements address distinct aspects and need not be confused.

The passage also sharpens the Witness distinction. The Son is not an ordinary witness. His identity and work are unique. Human witnesses receive and communicate what is given; they do not become the Author, the Word, or the sustaining power of Creation.

The central conclusion is:

**God speaks. The worlds are made through the Son. The Son upholds all things by the word of His power. The witness may investigate and report the created order, but the witness does not become its Author or Sustainer.**

This study finds strong biblical support for the theological distinction between Author, Word, Creation, and witness. It does not propose a scientific mechanism or a new GUA-W architectural component.
