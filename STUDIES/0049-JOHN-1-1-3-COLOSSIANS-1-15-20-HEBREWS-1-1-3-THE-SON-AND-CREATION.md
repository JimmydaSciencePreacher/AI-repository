## 1. Question

Study 0048 examined Hebrews 1:1–3 and recorded the next comparison: John 1:1–3 and Colossians 1:15–20. The question for this study is:

**When these three passages are read in their own contexts, what relationships do they each state about the Son, the Word, the making of all things, and the continuing existence of Creation?**

A second question follows from the numbered-pieces observation recorded in Guide 05:

**What identifying details are already present in the passages—their references, repeated expressions, grammatical relationships, and surrounding context—and what connections can be witnessed without imposing a prior arrangement?**

The study does not begin by assigning the passages to GUA-W layers. It first records what each passage says, preserves the details that identify its claims, and then compares the passages. Any GUA-W correspondence remains a later, provisional assessment.

The primary texts are:

This study does not claim that the passages describe a scientific mechanism. It does not equate the Word with energy, information, a physical field, or a mathematical law. It does not treat shared vocabulary as proof that two passages describe an identical mechanism. It proposes no new GUA-W layer, operator, state, or formal definition.

## 4. John 1:1–3 — The Word and the beginning

### 4.1 The KJV text

> “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.
>
> The same was in the beginning with God.
>
> All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made.” (John 1:1–3, KJV)

John begins with “In the beginning,” language that deliberately recalls Genesis 1:1. The passage does not begin with the human witness's discovery. It begins with the Word already existing.

### 4.2 “In the beginning was the Word”

The Greek expression is *en archē ēn ho logos* (ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος).

- *En archē* means “in the beginning.”
- *Logos* means “word,” “message,” or “account,” with its meaning shaped by context.
- *Ēn* is the imperfect form of “to be,” describing the Word as already existing in the beginning.

John's wording is significant when compared with Genesis. Genesis 1:1 says, “In the beginning God created.” John 1:1 says, “In the beginning was the Word.” The Word is not introduced as one of the things that began to exist in the creation account. John places the Word already present “in the beginning.”

This observation is based on the contrast between the wording of the two texts. It should not be expanded beyond what the grammar and wider Johannine context support.

### 4.3 “The Word was with God, and the Word was God”

John holds two statements together:
- the Word was with God;
- the Word was God.

The first preserves relationship and distinction. The second makes a strong claim about the Word's divine identity. Neither statement should be discarded to make the other easier.

This is relevant to Hebrews 1:1–3, which distinguishes God as the One who speaks and describes the Son in relation to the Father, while also using exalted language about the Son's identity.

The texts do not provide permission to collapse the Father and the Son into one person, nor to reduce the Word/Son to a created messenger.

### 4.4 “All things were made by him”

John 1:3 says:

> “All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made.”

The Greek phrase *panta di' autou egeneto* (πάντα δι’ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο) means that all things came into being through Him. The second half reinforces the scope: apart from Him, not one thing that came into being came into being.

The repeated “all things” and the explicit negative restatement are important identifying features. John is not describing only a limited class of objects or one stage of the universe. The statement is comprehensive in the passage's context.

The contrast between the Word, who already “was,” and the things that “came into being” is important. John does not place the Word among the things made.

### 4.5 John 1:14–18 as context

John later says that the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us, and that the witnesses beheld His glory (John 1:14). The passage then speaks of the Son making the Father known (John 1:18).

This context is essential to the witness question. John distinguishes:
- the Word who was with God and was God;
- the Word through whom all things were made;
- the Word made flesh;
- witnesses who beheld His glory and testify.

The witness reports what is seen. The witness does not become the Word or the Creator. John’s Gospel makes this distinction within the same opening passage.

## 5. Colossians 1:15–20 — The Son, all things, and reconciliation

### 5.1 The KJV text

> “Who is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature:
>
> For by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible, whether they be thrones, or dominions, or principalities, or powers: all things were created by him, and for him:
>
> And he is before all things, and by him all things consist.
>
> And he is the head of the body, the church: who is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead; that in all things he might have the preeminence.
>
> For it pleased the Father that in him should all fulness dwell;
>
> And, having made peace through the blood of his cross, by him to reconcile all things unto himself; by him, I say, whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven.” (Colossians 1:15–20, KJV)

The passage is not an isolated statement about the physical universe. It belongs to Paul's thanksgiving and prayer, and it connects the Son's identity, Creation, the church, resurrection, divine fullness, and reconciliation.

### 5.2 “The image of the invisible God”

The Greek phrase is *eikōn tou theou tou aoratou* (εἰκὼν τοῦ θεοῦ τοῦ ἀοράτου), “image of the invisible God.”

This resembles Hebrews 1:3's “express image of his person,” but the terms are not identical:
- Colossians uses *eikōn* (“image”).
- Hebrews uses *charaktēr* (“impress,” “exact representation”) with *hypostasis*.

The passages make related claims about the Son's relation to God, but a word-for-word identity should not be assumed. Their respective contexts must remain visible.

### 5.3 “The firstborn of every creature”

The phrase *prōtotokos pasēs ktiseōs* (πρωτότοκος πάσης κτίσεως) has been debated. The word *prōtotokos* means “firstborn.” In biblical usage, firstborn language can express birth order, rank, inheritance, or preeminence, depending on context.

The following verses explain Paul's argument: all things were created by Him and for Him; He is before all things; and all things consist in Him. The immediate context therefore does not support reading “firstborn” as though Paul simply called the Son the first created item. The text's own explanatory clauses place Him in relation to all created things as their Creator and Lord.

The study records the phrase and its contextual explanation without claiming that one English gloss alone resolves every interpretive debate.

### 5.4 “By him were all things created”

The Greek phrase is *en autō ektisthē ta panta* (ἐν αὐτῷ ἐκτίσθη τὰ πάντα). The preposition *en* can be rendered “in,” “by,” or “in connection with,” according to context. The KJV renders the phrase “by him.”

Paul then makes the scope explicit:
- things in heaven and earth;
- visible and invisible;
- thrones, dominions, principalities, and powers.

He repeats: “all things were created by him, and for him.”

The passage thus identifies both the range of created things and their relation to the Son. The Son is not simply a participant within one created category. All created categories are included in the statement.

### 5.5 “By him, and for him”

The phrase “by him, and for him” gives two distinct relations:
- creation is through/by the Son;
- creation is for the Son.

The first concerns agency or mediation in creation. The second concerns its end, goal, or orientation. The passage does not describe Creation as an independent system whose purpose is determined by the observer.

This distinction is important, but it should not be expanded into a complete teleological theory without further scriptural examination.

### 5.6 “He is before all things”

The Greek *autos estin pro pantōn* (αὐτός ἐστιν πρὸ πάντων) means “He is before all things.” In context, this supports the Son's priority in relation to Creation.

The wording belongs alongside John 1:1–3, where the Word already was in the beginning, and Hebrews 1:2–3, where God made the worlds through the Son and the Son upholds all things.

### 5.7 “By him all things consist”

The Greek phrase is *ta panta en autō synestēken* (τὰ πάντα ἐν αὐτῷ συνέστηκεν). The verb *synistēmi* can mean to hold together, consist, or cohere. The perfect form presents the resulting state as established and continuing.

The KJV's “by him all things consist” expresses the Son's sustaining or cohesive relation to all things. The wording is closely related in subject matter to Hebrews 1:3, “upholding all things by the word of his power.”

The expressions are not identical:
- Colossians uses *synistēmi*.
- Hebrews uses *pherō*.

Their shared subject is the Son's relation to the continuing existence/order of “all things.” The lexical difference should be preserved rather than erased.

### 5.8 Creation and reconciliation belong to the same passage

Colossians 1:15–20 moves from Creation to the church, resurrection, fullness, peace, and reconciliation through the blood of the cross.

This matters because Paul's purpose is not simply to provide a detached account of cosmic structure. The Son who is related to all Creation is also the One through whom reconciliation is made.

A scientific analogy that extracts “all things consist” while ignoring the surrounding claims would misrepresent the passage's full scope.

## 6. Hebrews 1:1–3 — God speaks through the Son, who upholds all things

Study 0048 examined Hebrews 1:1–3 in detail. This section records only the comparison points needed for the present study.

### 6.1 The KJV wording

> “God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets,
>
> Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son, whom he hath appointed heir of all things, by whom also he made the worlds;
>
> Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power...” (Hebrews 1:1–3, KJV)

### 6.2 The identifying statements

Hebrews explicitly names:
- God as the One who speaks;
- the Son as the One through whom God has spoken;
- the Son as appointed heir of all things;
- the Son as the One through whom God made the worlds;
- the Son as the brightness of God's glory and express image of His person;
- the Son as the One upholding all things by the word of His power.

The phrase “all things” occurs in both the creation and sustaining claims. The passage links the Son's role in the making of the worlds with His ongoing sustaining of all things.

### 6.3 The relation to the other passages

John says all things came into being through the Word. Colossians says all things were created by and for the Son, and that all things consist in Him. Hebrews says God made the worlds through the Son and that the Son upholds all things.

The three passages use different wording and occur in different literary contexts. Their claims nevertheless converge around a shared subject: the Son's unique relation to the creation of all things and to their continued existence.

This is a relationship found by comparing the actual statements. It is not created by assigning the passages to a preselected GUA-W position.

## 7. The visible relationships across the passages

The comparison can now be recorded without erasing each source's wording.

| Identifying feature | John 1:1–3 | Colossians 1:15–20 | Hebrews 1:1–3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Named focus | The Word | The Son / image of the invisible God | God's Son |
| Relation to the beginning | The Word already was in the beginning | The Son is before all things | God made the worlds through the Son |
| Creation statement | All things made through Him | All things created by Him and for Him | God made the worlds through Him |
| Continuing relation | John 1:3 focuses on coming into being; later context develops life | By Him all things consist | He upholds all things |
| Relation to God | The Word was with God and was God | Image of the invisible God; fullness pleased the Father to dwell in Him | The Son is radiance and express image; God speaks by Him |
| Witness language | Witnesses behold and testify (John 1:14–18) | The passage concerns the church and reconciliation | God spoke through prophets and now by the Son |

The table does not claim that each column is interchangeable. It preserves the distinct details and shows where statements correspond.

### 7.1 “All things” is a repeated identifier

Each passage uses comprehensive language:
- John 1:3: “all things” were made by Him; without Him nothing made was made.
- Colossians 1:16–17: “all things” were created by and for Him; “by him all things consist.”
- Hebrews 1:2–3: God made the worlds through the Son; the Son upholds “all things.”

The repetition is not merely a shared English phrase. Each passage makes a comprehensive claim about the Son's relation to Creation.

### 7.2 The passages distinguish origin and continuation

John 1:3 emphasizes the coming into being of all created things through the Word. Colossians 1:17 and Hebrews 1:3 add explicit statements about the continuing coherence or sustaining of all things.

The comparison therefore distinguishes:
- the coming into being of created things;
- their continuing existence and order.

The texts relate these claims to the Son. They do not supply a detailed physical mechanism for either.

### 7.3 The passages distinguish the Son from created witnesses

John's Prologue includes witnesses who testify to the Light and who behold the Word's glory. Hebrews begins by mentioning the prophets through whom God spoke, then distinguishes God's climactic speech by His Son. Colossians identifies the Son's relation to the whole created order and to the church.

The Son cannot be placed in the same category as ordinary human witnesses. The witness receives, beholds, and testifies. The Son is described in these passages as the Word, the One through whom all things were made, the image of the invisible God, and the One who sustains all things.

This distinction is essential to any use of the Author → Word → Creation → Witness relationship. It is not a license to flatten the Son into a generic “Word component” or a data item.

## 8. Genesis 1:1–5 as the creation-side comparison

### 8.1 The KJV text

> “In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.
>
> And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters.
>
> And God said, Let there be light: and there was light.
>
> And God saw the light, that it was good: and God divided the light from the darkness.
>
> And God called the light Day, and the darkness he called Night. And the evening and the morning were the first day.” (Genesis 1:1–5, KJV)

### 8.2 The source-side details

Genesis identifies:
- God as Creator;
- the beginning of heaven and earth;
- the Spirit of God moving upon the face of the waters;
- God's speech;
- the coming of light;
- God's seeing and evaluation;
- the division of light from darkness;
- God's naming of Day and Night.

These are the actual narrative details. They should not be replaced with an assumed physical model.

### 8.3 The comparison with John

Genesis 1:1 begins, “In the beginning God created.” John 1:1 begins, “In the beginning was the Word.” John 1:3 then states that all things were made through the Word.

The shared phrase “in the beginning” and the creation statements establish a deliberate and meaningful canonical comparison. John's Prologue is read in the light of Genesis's opening, while also revealing the Word's relation to God and to the creation of all things.

Genesis does not explicitly use the name “Jesus” in Genesis 1:1–5. The identification of the Word with the Son is made through the New Testament witness, especially John 1:14 and the wider canonical testimony. This distinction between the wording of Genesis and the later canonical identification must remain clear.

### 8.4 The comparison with Hebrews and Colossians

Hebrews identifies the Son as the One through whom God made the worlds. Colossians identifies all created things as made by and for the Son. These texts add information about the Son's role that is not stated in those exact words in Genesis 1:1–5.

The result is a canonical synthesis, not a claim that every detail in Genesis is a direct technical description of the later terminology.

## 9. What the comparison establishes—and what it does not

### 9.1 Supported scriptural synthesis

Read together, the passages support these conclusions:
1. Genesis identifies God as Creator and Speaker.
2. John identifies the Word as already existing in the beginning, with God and as God.
3. John states that all things came into being through the Word.
4. Colossians identifies the Son as the image of the invisible God and says all things were created by Him and for Him.
5. Colossians says the Son is before all things and that all things consist in Him.
6. Hebrews says God made the worlds through the Son and that the Son upholds all things by the word of His power.
7. The New Testament passages distinguish the Son's unique identity and role from the role of prophets and other witnesses.

### 9.2 Not established by this comparison

The comparison does not establish:
1. A physical equation corresponding to “the Word.”
2. A measurable scientific mechanism that exhaustively describes the Son's sustaining work.
3. That “all things consist” is simply another name for a particular known force or field.
4. That the Greek words *logos*, *rhēma*, *synistēmi*, and *pherō* are interchangeable.
5. That every numerical reference or repeated number in Scripture encodes a hidden architecture.
6. That every scientific field must yield a direct correspondence to a particular biblical phrase.
7. A new GUA-W layer, operator, or state.

## 10. The numbered-pieces method applied to this comparison

The research image of the numbered pieces can now be applied concretely.

### 10.1 The identifying details were retained

The passages were not reduced to one generic statement. Their locations, subjects, and key expressions remain identifiable:
- John 1:1–3 — the Word, beginning, all things made through Him;
- Colossians 1:15–20 — the Son, image, all things created by and for Him, all things consisting in Him;
- Hebrews 1:1–3 — God speaking by the Son, worlds made through Him, all things upheld by Him;
- Genesis 1:1–5 — God creating, speaking, seeing, dividing, and naming.

### 10.2 The fit was not assumed

The passages were compared after their own wording and contexts were examined. Their shared claims concerning the Son and all Creation are supported by explicit statements. The study did not decide in advance that every phrase must correspond to a GUA-W layer.

### 10.3 Differences were preserved

The wording is not identical:
- *Logos* in John;
- *eikōn* and *prōtotokos* in Colossians;
- *charaktēr*, *hypostasis*, and *pherō* in Hebrews.

The differences matter. The passages can bear a coherent canonical relationship without being treated as interchangeable texts.

### 10.4 What remains unplaced remains unplaced

## 14. Conclusion

The comparison of John 1:1–3, Colossians 1:15–20, and Hebrews 1:1–3 reveals a substantial scriptural relationship.

John identifies the Word as already existing in the beginning, with God and as God, and says all things were made through Him. Colossians identifies the Son as the image of the invisible God, says all things were created by Him and for Him, and says that by Him all things consist. Hebrews says God made the worlds through the Son and that the Son upholds all things by the word of His power.

Genesis 1:1–5 remains the creation-side account: God creates, speaks, sees, divides, and names. The New Testament passages provide further revelation about the Son's unique relation to the creation of all things and their continued existence.

The relationship was not established by forcing the texts into a preselected model. It became visible through their own repeated claims, preserved wording, and contexts.

**The witness does not author the arrangement. The witness preserves the identifying details and reports the relationship that the source material supports.**

No physical mechanism has been identified, no scientific confirmation is claimed, and no GUA-W architectural component has been added or changed.
