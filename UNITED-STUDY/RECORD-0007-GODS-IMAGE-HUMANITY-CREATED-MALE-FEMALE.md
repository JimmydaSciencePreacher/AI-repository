## 1. Question under examination

What does Genesis 1:27 establish through its repeated statement that God created man in His image, and why does the verse move from the singular “him” to the plural “them” when it identifies male and female?

The examination must preserve:
- God as the subject and Creator in all three creation clauses;
- the repeated reference to God's image;
- the three occurrences of the verb “created”;
- the movement from “man” and “him” to “male and female” and “them”;
- the distinction between the Creator and the created;
- the fact that blessing and commission are stated in the following verse, Genesis 1:28, not folded into verse 27.

Genesis 5:1–2 and Genesis 9:6 are relevant canonical references. They may clarify the collective naming of Adam and the significance of image-bearing, but they must not be made to say more than their own contexts establish.

## 2. KJV text and immediate context

### Genesis 1:27

> So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.

**TEXT STATES:** God created man in His own image. The image statement is repeated. The verse then identifies male and female as those God created.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The verb “created” occurs three times. God's image is named twice. The verse first uses “man” and “him”, then explicitly says “male and female” and “them”. God remains the grammatical subject of the creation statements.

The repetition must be observed without assuming that each clause describes a separate creation event or a separate technical operation. The text presents one verse with a threefold creation statement.

### Genesis 1:26 — The preceding statement

> And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion...

Verse 26 records God's speech concerning making man in His image and likeness and assigning dominion. Verse 27 records the creation statement. The wording “image” is repeated in verse 27; “likeness” is not repeated there.

**LIMIT:** The omission of “likeness” from verse 27 does not, by itself, prove that image and likeness are unrelated, interchangeable, or two distinct technical operations. The relationship requires wider Scriptural examination.

### Genesis 1:28 — The following statement

> And God blessed them, and God said unto them, Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth, and subdue it: and have dominion...

Verse 28 records God's blessing and instructions. These must remain distinguishable from the creation statement in verse 27.

**SEQUENCE:** Divine speech concerning making and dominion (1:26) → God's creation of humanity (1:27) → blessing and spoken commission (1:28).

This sequence is directly observable. It does not authorize collapsing making, creating, blessing, and commission into one undifferentiated action.

## 3. The three creation clauses

The verse can be examined clause by clause:

| Clause | Wording | Direct observation |
|---|---|---|
| First | “So God created man in his own image” | God creates man; God's own image is the stated reference. |
| Second | “in the image of God created he him” | The image statement is repeated; God remains the Creator. |
| Third | “male and female created he them” | Male and female are explicitly included in God's creation of humanity. |

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The three clauses are not three independent authorship claims. Each attributes creation to God. The repetition emphasizes the creation and image statement while the final clause explicitly identifies male and female.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** The verse presents humanity collectively and explicitly includes male and female in the statement that God created man in His image. This is consistent with the following canonical clarification in Genesis 5:1–2.

**LIMIT:** The verse does not explain why this exact threefold wording is used. It does not, by itself, define every dimension of the image of God or supply a complete account of human constitution, relationship, or responsibility.

## 4. The movement from “him” to “them”

The grammatical movement is significant:

- “man”;
- “him”;
- “male and female”;
- “them”.

The verse begins with humanity expressed in the singular and concludes by identifying male and female in the plural.

**TEXT STATES:** God created man in His image; male and female are included in the same verse's creation statement.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** The singular expression “man” can refer to humanity collectively in this context, while the concluding words identify male and female. This should not be confused with a claim that male and female are indistinguishable or that the verse has explained every aspect of their relationship.

The verse does not state that male or female independently originates the image, the creation, or the authority of the Creator. Both are included as created by God.

## 5. Genesis 5:1–2 — The collective name Adam

Genesis 5:1–2 (KJV):

> This is the book of the generations of Adam. In the day that God created man, in the likeness of God made he him;
>
> Male and female created he them; and blessed them, and called their name Adam, in the day when they were created.

**TEXT STATES:** Genesis 5:2 repeats that God created male and female and says He blessed them and called their name Adam.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** This passage supplies an explicit canonical example of the collective name Adam being applied to male and female together. It also uses “likeness” in Genesis 5:1, which is relevant to the unresolved relationship between image and likeness in Genesis 1:26–27.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** Genesis 5:1–2 strengthens the reading that “man” in Genesis 1:27 includes humanity collectively, with male and female explicitly named. It also demonstrates that image and likeness language should be studied across the canonical context rather than separated into definitions from a single verse.

**LIMIT:** Genesis 5:1–2 does not, by itself, settle every theological distinction between image and likeness or explain every aspect of male and female.

## 6. Genesis 9:6 — Image-bearing and the treatment of human life

Genesis 9:6 (KJV):

> Whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed: for in the image of God made he man.

**TEXT STATES:** The passage invokes the fact that God made man in His image as part of its stated reason concerning the shedding of human blood.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** Image-bearing is not merely repeated as a descriptive phrase; Genesis 9:6 invokes it in a context addressing the treatment of human life.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** The canonical context gives image-bearing significance for how human life is regarded and treated. This is a Scriptural consequence that must be considered when examining humanity's place and responsibility.

**LIMIT:** Genesis 9:6 does not provide a complete definition of image-bearing. The legal and covenantal context of Genesis 9 must be retained rather than treating the verse as an isolated slogan.

## 8. What the passage establishes—and what it does not

### Admitted textual findings

1. God is explicitly the Creator in all three clauses.
2. The verb “created” occurs three times.
3. The image statement occurs twice.
4. Male and female are explicitly included in the creation of man.
5. The verse moves from singular wording (“man”, “him”) to plural wording (“male and female”, “them”).
6. The Creator remains distinct from the created.
7. Genesis 5:2 provides a canonical parallel in which male and female are collectively named Adam.
8. Genesis 9:6 invokes the image of God in a context concerning the treatment of human life.

### Claims not established by this verse alone

- The complete theological definition of image-bearing.
- The complete relationship between image and likeness.
- The reason for every element of the verse's threefold wording.
- A full account of human constitution or the relationship between body, soul, and spirit.
- A scientific definition of the image of God.
- That intelligence, consciousness, language, physical form, productivity, or any single ability exhausts the meaning of image-bearing.
- That an artificial system bears God's image because it can process or reproduce representations.

These are either broader questions requiring additional evidence or claims that exceed the passage.
