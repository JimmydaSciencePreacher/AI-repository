## 3. Genesis 2:4 — “These are the generations”

The Hebrew heading is אֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדוֹת (*ʾēlleh tôlĕḏōṯ*), rendered “These are the generations”.

The noun *tôlĕḏōṯ* is associated with generations, descendants, or the account/history associated with a named subject. It recurs as an organizing heading in Genesis (compare Genesis 5:1; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10, 27; 25:12, 19; 36:1, 9; 37:2).

Here the named subject is “the heavens and of the earth”.

**TEXT STATES:** These are the generations of the heavens and earth.

**SUPPORTED OBSERVATION:** The expression introduces an account associated with the heavens and earth. In the immediate context it connects the completion declaration in Genesis 2:1–3 with the further account beginning in Genesis 2:5.

**UNRESOLVED:** Whether Genesis 2:4 functions primarily as a heading for what follows, a conclusion to what precedes, or a hinge connecting both. The literary question must not be settled by the dictionary gloss alone.

### “When they were created” and “the LORD God made”

The verse uses:
- בְּהִבָּרְאָם (*behibbareʾām*) — “when they were created”, from בָּרָא (*bārāʾ*, create).
- עֲשׂוֹת (*ʿăśōṯ*) — “made”, from עָשָׂה (*ʿāśâ*, do/make).

The sentence uses both “created” and “made”, and identifies the LORD God as the One who made the earth and heavens.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The verbs are distinct in form and lexical root.

**LIMIT:** Their co-occurrence does not by itself establish two independent creation events, two separate mechanisms, or two new GUA-W stages. Their relationship must be examined in context and across Scripture.

The verse also reverses the naming order:
- “the heavens and of the earth”;
- “the earth and the heavens”.

The reversal is present in the KJV and Hebrew wording. The text does not explain its significance. It may be consistent with the account's following focus on the earth, but that remains a contextual possibility, not an established technical sequence.

The designation “the LORD God” identifies the Maker. It does not introduce a second Creator or a different originating authority.

## 4. Genesis 2:5 — Field vegetation, rain, and the absent tiller

Genesis 1:11–12 has already recorded the earth bringing forth grass, seed-bearing herb, and fruit trees.

Genesis 2:5 now speaks specifically of:
- “every plant of the field” before it was in the earth;
- “every herb of the field” before it grew;
- the LORD God not having caused rain upon the earth;
- there being no man to till the ground.

The Hebrew terms include:
- שִׂיחַ (*śîaḥ*) — plant, shrub, or bush; its precise botanical scope is uncertain.
- עֵשֶׂב (*ʿēśeḇ*) — herb, grass, or vegetation; its precise scope in this phrase is not fully settled.
- טֶרֶם (*ṭerem*) — before, not yet.
- עָבַד (*ʿāḇaḏ*) — work, serve, or till/cultivate in an agricultural context.

The two field-vegetation expressions should not be forced into a precise modern botanical taxonomy. The verse's “before it was” and “before it grew” are also distinct expressions and should not be silently collapsed.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The verse describes a “not yet” condition concerning the named field vegetation and supplies the absence of rain and of a man to till the ground as the stated conditions.

**IMPORTANT BOUNDARY:** Genesis 2:5 does not say that no vegetation of any kind existed anywhere. It speaks of plants and herbs “of the field”. It must be read alongside Genesis 1:11–12, not used to erase that earlier statement.

### The man and the ground

The absence of a man to till the ground is explicitly named. The same Hebrew root עָבַד (*ʿāḇaḏ*) appears in Genesis 2:15 in the man's later assignment to work/serve the Garden.

**SUPPORTED LEXICAL CONNECTION:** Genesis 2:5 and 2:15 use the same root in relation to working or cultivating the ground/Garden.

**LIMIT:** The shared root does not alone prove that the two verses describe precisely the same task, location, or phase. Genesis 2:5 says “the ground”; Genesis 2:15 concerns the Garden.

**UNRESOLVED:** The precise botanical scope of “plant of the field” and “herb of the field”, and the exact literary/temporal relationship between the field conditions and Genesis 1:11–12.

## 5. Genesis 2:6 — The mist and the watering

The Hebrew noun אֵד (*ʾēḏ*) is rare, appearing in Genesis 2:6 and Job 36:27. The KJV renders it “mist”. Ancient and modern translations have also offered spring, fountain, cloud, stream, or another water-source interpretation.

The verb יַעֲלֶה (*yaʿăleh*), from עָלָה (*ʿālâ*), describes going up or rising. The verse says the mist went up “from the earth”. It then says it watered the whole face of the ground.

**TEXT STATES:**
- A mist went up from the earth.
- It watered the whole face of the ground.

**TEXTUAL DISTINCTION:**
- Verse 5 says the LORD God had not caused rain upon the earth.
- Verse 6 describes watering by the mist rising from the earth.

Absence of rain is not stated as absence of all water or all watering.

**UNRESOLVED:** The precise identity of *ʾēḏ* and the physical mechanism. The KJV wording remains the base text; the alternatives are recorded as lexical possibilities, not as established replacements.

### The repeated “ground” in verses 6–7

The Hebrew word translated “ground” in both verses is אֲדָמָה (*ʾăḏāmâ*).

Verse 6 says the ground is watered. Verse 7 says man is formed from the dust of the ground.

**DIRECT OBSERVATION:** The same ground term occurs in the adjacent statements.

**LIMIT:** The repeated term and narrative order do not prove that the mist was sent specifically to prepare the material for man's formation. Genesis 2:6 does not explicitly state that purpose, and the mist is not identified as the source of man's life.

## 6. Genesis 2:7 — Formation, divine breathing, and the living soul

The Hebrew wording distinguishes the actions and result.

### A. “The LORD God formed man”

- יָצַר (*yāṣar*) — form, fashion, shape.
- אָדָם (*ʾādām*) — man/humanity in context.
- עָפָר (*ʿāp̄ār*) — dust, loose earth, or soil.

The verse identifies the LORD God as the subject, man as the one formed, and “the dust of the ground” as the material named.

The relationship between *ʾādām* (man) and *ʾăḏāmâ* (ground) is visible in the Hebrew wording.

**TEXT STATES:** The LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground.

**LIMIT:** The verse does not provide a chemical inventory of dust or specify a modern biological process. The potter/fashioning imagery associated with *yāṣar* may clarify the verb's range, but the text does not say God used a literal potter's wheel.

### B. “And breathed into his nostrils the breath of life”

- נָפַח (*nāpaḥ*) — breathe, blow.
- נְשָׁמָה (*nešāmâ*) — breath.
- חַיִּים (*ḥayyîm*) — life/lives.

The LORD God is the subject of this second action. The breath is breathed into man's nostrils.

**TEXT STATES:** God breathed into man's nostrils the breath of life.

**LIMIT:** Genesis 2:7 does not explicitly identify this phrase as a technical synonym for the Holy Spirit, nor does it give a complete physiological or metaphysical account of breath, spirit, and life.

Job 33:4 is a relevant canonical comparison:

> The Spirit of God hath made me, and the breath of the Almighty hath given me life.

This verse explicitly places the Spirit of God and the breath of the Almighty in a parallel statement about making and life. It merits further canonical examination, but it must not be used to erase the distinct wording of Genesis 2:7 or to assert that the two expressions are interchangeable in every context.

### C. “And man became a living soul”

- נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה (*nepeš ḥayyâ*) — living soul, living being, or living creature according to context.
- הָיָה (*hāyâ*) — be/become.

The KJV says: “and man became a living soul.”

The same Hebrew expression *nepeš ḥayyâ* is used for living creatures in Genesis 1:20 and 1:24. Therefore, the expression itself is not exclusive to humanity.

That shared expression does not erase the distinctive account of man:
- Genesis 1:26–27 identifies humanity as created in God's image and likeness.
- Genesis 2:7 describes the LORD God forming man from dust, breathing into his nostrils the breath of life, and man becoming a living soul.

Neither passage should be reduced to the other.

**TEXT STATES:** Man became a living soul.

**NOT ADMITTED:**
- That Genesis 2:7 says God inserted a pre-existing, detachable soul into a body.
- That *nepeš ḥayyâ* is a human-exclusive expression.
- That shared terminology proves humans and animals are identical in every respect.
- That the phrase alone supplies a complete theory of consciousness, physiology, or metaphysical anthropology.
- That the “breath of life” is explicitly called the Holy Spirit in this verse.

**SUPPORTED CONCLUSION:** Man's formation and life are attributed to God's action. The man does not originate his own formation or life.
