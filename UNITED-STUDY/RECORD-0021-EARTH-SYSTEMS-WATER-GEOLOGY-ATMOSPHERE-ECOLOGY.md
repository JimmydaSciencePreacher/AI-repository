## 3. Disciplinary findings

### A. Hydrology — water is stored in multiple places and moves between them

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** USGS describes water as stored in the atmosphere, on the land surface, and below ground, in liquid, solid, and gaseous forms. Its described stores include oceans, lakes, rivers, wetlands, glaciers, snowpack, soil moisture, permafrost, atmospheric moisture, and groundwater in aquifers.

Water moves between these stores through processes including evaporation, evapotranspiration, precipitation, snowmelt, runoff, streamflow, infiltration, groundwater recharge, groundwater flow, and discharge.

USGS identifies solar energy and gravity as major drivers of this movement. NOAA likewise describes the water cycle as a complex set of stores and flows connecting air, clouds, oceans, lakes, vegetation, snowpack, and glaciers. NOAA cautions that the familiar simplified circular diagram does not capture all the complexity of real water pathways.

**Scope:** These descriptions establish multiple stores and pathways, not one uniform route followed by every water molecule. Timing, location, phase, and pathway vary.

Sources: USGS Water Cycle; NOAA The Water Cycle; USGS What is Hydrology?

### B. Geology and geomorphology — water interacts with earth materials

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** USGS describes groundwater as occupying pores and fractures in soil and rock. Its movement depends in part on the permeability and porosity of subsurface materials, and groundwater can discharge into springs, rivers, and oceans.

USGS also describes runoff as interacting with the physical geology and topography of a landscape. Runoff can carry particulate matter and sediment into streams. The amount and effect of runoff vary with such conditions as slope, soil type, vegetation, rainfall intensity and duration, and prior soil moisture.

USGS research on sediment transport and deposition reports that changes in sediment supply and movement can affect river-channel form, aquatic habitat, water quality, and reservoir storage. Sediment is therefore not merely an inert visual feature; its movement has consequences in both physical and ecological settings.

**Scope:** These findings do not mean water alone determines landform or sediment movement. Gravity, material properties, topography, climate, vegetation, and other conditions also matter. The detailed processes and rates are site-specific.

Sources: USGS Groundwater Flow and the Water Cycle; USGS Surface Runoff and the Water Cycle; USGS Sediment Transport and Deposition.

### C. Soil science — soil links water movement and plant availability

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** USDA-NRCS describes infiltration as water entering soil. Soil texture, structure, pore continuity, vegetation, slope, and surface conditions influence infiltration and runoff. Soil can store water available for plant growth.

The USDA-NRCS source also describes a linked sequence: reduced infiltration can increase runoff; runoff can cause erosion; erosion can carry sediment, nutrients, and organic matter into waterways; and the loss or redistribution of soil material can affect soil condition and plant communities.

The NRCS soil-health material describes relationships among soil structure, water retention, runoff, erosion, organisms, organic matter, and nutrient cycling.

**Scope:** These are conditional relationships, not an assertion that every soil responds identically. Soil type, land cover, management, climate, and local conditions affect the outcome.

Sources: USDA-NRCS Rangeland Soil Health: Infiltration; USDA-NRCS Soil Health.

### D. Atmospheric science — the atmosphere participates in water movement

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** NOAA and USGS describe exchanges between the atmosphere and Earth's surface through evaporation, evapotranspiration, condensation, and precipitation. Water is transported in the atmosphere and returned to the surface in different forms, including rain and snow.

NASA's Earth-system research program treats atmospheric processes alongside the geosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, and cryosphere. It identifies the atmosphere as a domain involving dynamics, thermodynamics, composition, radiative balance, air quality, and weather.

**Scope:** The atmosphere is not merely a passive container for water vapor. Atmospheric circulation, energy, temperature, and composition affect the timing and distribution of water. This record does not attempt a quantitative atmospheric model.

Sources: NOAA The Water Cycle; USGS Water Cycle; NASA Earth System Science Research.

### E. Ecology — water, soil, sediment, and habitat are connected to living systems

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The sources identify several direct interfaces between physical processes and living systems:

- Soil water is available to plants and can be returned to the atmosphere through transpiration.
- Groundwater contributes to the flow of many streams and rivers and influences river and wetland habitats.
- Sediment quantity and particle size can alter channel form and aquatic habitat.
- Runoff and erosion can transport nutrients, organic matter, and contaminants into streams, lakes, and rivers, affecting water quality and habitats.
- Vegetation influences how much water enters soil or runs off, while soil condition affects plant water availability.

NASA's biosphere research describes terrestrial and aquatic ecosystem interactions and changes in biogeochemistry and biodiversity.

**Scope:** The sources support physical and biological dependencies, not the claim that ecology can be reduced to hydrology or geology. Organisms and ecosystems have additional biological relationships and processes requiring their own evidence.

Sources: USGS Groundwater Flow and the Water Cycle; USGS Sediment Transport and Deposition; USDA-NRCS Infiltration; NASA Earth System Science Research.

## 4. Relationships established across the sources

The following are **CORRESPONDENCES** supported by the combined disciplinary sources. They are relationships between processes and domains, not claims that the disciplines have identical subject matter or methods.

| Interface | Source-supported relationship | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Atmosphere ↔ Hydrosphere | Evaporation, evapotranspiration, condensation, and precipitation transfer water between surface/biological stores and atmospheric stores. | That the water cycle is a simple closed loop with one route or fixed timing. |
| Hydrosphere ↔ Geosphere | Infiltration, groundwater flow, runoff, and discharge connect water with soil, rock, aquifers, streams, and oceans. | That all geological materials have the same permeability or groundwater behavior. |
| Hydrosphere ↔ Geomorphology | Flowing water can erode, transport, and deposit sediment, changing channels and sediment distributions. | That water is the only agent shaping landforms. |
| Geosphere/Soil ↔ Biosphere | Soil structure and stored water influence plant water availability; roots and vegetation interact with soil conditions. | That soil alone determines plant growth or ecosystem composition. |
| Biosphere ↔ Hydrosphere/Atmosphere | Plant uptake and transpiration connect biological activity to water movement and atmospheric moisture. | That all evapotranspiration is caused by plants; evaporation also occurs from nonliving surfaces. |
| Sediment ↔ Aquatic Ecology | Sediment amount and particle size influence channel form, water clarity, and habitat conditions. | That any one sediment condition is universally beneficial or harmful across all ecosystems. |
| Vegetation ↔ Runoff/Soil | Vegetation and soil condition influence infiltration, runoff, and erosion; water movement in turn affects vegetation and soil. | A single universal response independent of place, species, soil, and climate. |

### Initial synthesis

The strongest supported result is not that four sciences have become one discipline. It is that the same Earth processes are examined from distinct but interacting disciplinary perspectives.

Water is a particularly clear connector because it:
- changes physical state;
- moves through atmospheric, surface, subsurface, and biological stores;
- interacts with geological materials and soil;
- transports dissolved and particulate material;
- affects and is affected by living systems.

The evidence therefore supports investigating Earth as an interacting system while preserving the distinctions among hydrology, geology, atmospheric science, soil science, and ecology.

The relevant Scriptural passages include:

- **Genesis 1:6–8:** the firmament and the division of waters.
- **Genesis 1:9–10:** waters gathered and dry land appearing.
- **Genesis 1:11–13:** the earth bringing forth vegetation, including seed-bearing plants and fruit trees.

- **Genesis 2:5–6:** the text's statement that rain had not yet been sent upon the earth and that a mist went up from the earth and watered the whole face of the ground.

**TEXT STATES:** Genesis presents waters, dry land, vegetation, living creatures, and the watering of the ground in its own narrated order and context.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** Genesis 2:5–6 specifically distinguishes the absence of rain at that point in the account from the mist described as watering the ground.

**LIMIT:** These verses do not provide the modern technical account of evaporation, atmospheric circulation, groundwater recharge, sediment transport, or ecosystem hydrology. The scientific sources must establish those mechanisms within their own methods and evidence.

**No claim is made here** that the modern water cycle is fully described in Genesis 1–2, or that Genesis 2:5–6 is a technical explanation of the present-day hydrologic cycle. The Scripture is the foundation; the scientific findings are examined as evidence about created processes.

## 9. What remains unresolved

2. **Scale:** Which relationships remain consistent from soil pore and plant-root scales to watershed, regional, and global scales?
