## 1. First discriminating question

**In one documented watershed, how do precipitation, infiltration, groundwater, runoff, sediment transport, soil condition, vegetation, and aquatic habitat interact—and which relationships are directly measured rather than inferred from a general system diagram?**

The selected case is the original Shale Hills headwater catchment within the Susquehanna Shale Hills Critical Zone Observatory (SSHCZO), in central Pennsylvania.

The original catchment is approximately 0.08 km² (about 7.9–8 hectares), forested, and underlain primarily by Silurian-age Rose Hill shale. It drains through a first-order stream to Shaver's Creek, then the Juniata River, and ultimately the Susquehanna River.

The SSHCZO later expanded its research footprint to include 120 km² of the 165-km² Shaver's Creek watershed. This record keeps the original Shale Hills subcatchment distinct from the larger research footprint.

**Scope decision:** The small original catchment is the principal case because it has unusually dense, spatially distributed measurements. Findings from it must not automatically be generalized to the entire Susquehanna basin or to all watersheds.

## 3. The watershed itself: observed physical setting

**DIRECT OBSERVATION / SITE DESCRIPTION:**

- The original catchment is about 0.08 km².
- It is a first-order headwater basin with a small, intermittent or ephemeral stream.
- The stream flows generally westward toward Shaver's Creek.
- The landscape has ridges, hillslopes, swales, a valley floor, and a channel.

- Soils vary in depth and properties according to landscape position.

## 4. What is measured, and how?

### A. Precipitation and atmospheric conditions

The observatory documents precipitation measurements beginning in 2006 at the north ridge, with a weighing-bucket gauge recording at 10-minute intervals. Additional gauges were used at several locations during parts of the soil-monitoring program.

The site also has an instrumented 30-m tower and measurements of radiative and turbulent fluxes, including energy and carbon dioxide exchange. The observatory describes these as ongoing or long-running data streams, although each variable has its own deployment history and data continuity.

**Measured:** precipitation at specified gauges and times; atmospheric and surface-exchange variables at specified instruments and periods.

**Not established by the mere existence of a sensor:** a complete atmospheric model or a complete measurement of every location in the catchment.

Sources: Penn State SSHCZO About; Brantley et al. (2018), long-term observations and instrumentation.

### B. Soil moisture and soil-water movement

The site has used manual multi-depth time-domain reflectometry, tensiometers, and automated soil-moisture and temperature sensors. A network of 35 soil-moisture sites was established through successive monitoring efforts. More recent GroundHOG locations include ridgetop, midslope, and valley-floor positions on different hillslopes.

At the GroundHOG sites, sensors at 10, 20, and 40 cm record soil moisture and temperature at 10-minute intervals. Soil-water samples are also collected using suction lysimeters at specified locations and depths.

A 2020 peer-reviewed analysis used hourly soil-moisture records from five sites and five soil horizons during the 2009 water year. The paper reports the use of capacitance-type probes, with stated accuracy of approximately ±0.03 m³/m³ and precision of ±0.001 m³/m³.

**Measured:** sensor response at specified depths, places, and times; collected soil pore water at specified sampling locations and depths.

**Inferred from time series:** how precipitation signals propagate through soil profiles, and how soil-moisture fluctuations relate to catchment discharge.

Sources: Brantley et al. (2018), soil-moisture and lysimeter sections; Liu et al. (2020), Materials and Methods.

### C. Stream discharge

The stream has been monitored by the observatory team since 2006, with documented gaps.

- From 2006 to 2012, a double V-notch weir measured water depth; a rating curve was used to convert depth to discharge.
- No direct discharge measurements were obtained from November 2012 through October 2015 because the weir deteriorated.
- For that gap, model reanalysis information is used to estimate discharge.
- In October 2015, an H-flume was installed. Sensors measure water depth, temperature, and electrical conductivity; discharge is calculated from depth.

This distinction is essential: measured discharge and model-estimated discharge are not the same evidentiary category.

**Measured:** water depth and associated sensor variables during instrumented periods.

**Calculated from measurements:** discharge using the stated hydraulic rating or flume relation.

**Estimated:** discharge for the documented gap using model reanalysis.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), stream-discharge monitoring section.

### D. Groundwater

The catchment contains 32 wells with depths ranging from approximately 1 to 35 m. Groundwater level and temperature are recorded in 13 of those wells. Some wells have been monitored sporadically; more intensive measurements began in 2013. Where deployed, automatic sensors record at 15-minute intervals and are checked against manual measurements.

**Measured:** water level and temperature at instrumented wells and recorded times.

**Not established:** that every subsurface flow path is directly observed. The well network samples specified locations, not every fracture, pore, or pathway.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), groundwater and stream-monitoring sections.

### E. Vegetation and forest condition

The observatory's tree inventory covers trees larger than 20 cm stem diameter. More than 2,000 trees representing 23 canopy species were surveyed in inventory periods including 2008, 2012, and 2016. Researchers recorded location, species, diameter, vitality, injuries, and canopy class. Dendrometers on a smaller subset provide more frequent measurements of trunk growth. Leaf litter is collected at multiple points during autumn.

The published synthesis reports that oak species dominate the forest's basal wood area and annual wood growth, while productivity varies with topographic position and aspect.

**Measured:** tree identity and condition at survey dates, diameter, litter collection, and selected growth records.

**Derived or estimated:** watershed-scale biomass, carbon stocks, and productivity values may involve extrapolation from sampled trees and plots.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), vegetation and carbon-budget sections.

### F. Geology, soils, and landscape form

Researchers have mapped landform units, surveyed soil depth, sampled soil properties, examined bedrock fractures and mineral composition, and used high-resolution topography. A detailed soil survey analyzed 289 samples. Soil depth was also mapped using 318 auger points and spatial interpolation.

The site contains several soil series associated with differences in depth, drainage, and landscape position. The upper fractured rock and soil mantle are important to the hydrological interpretation.

**Measured:** sampled soil properties, auger depths, mapped terrain, and observed geological structures.

**Derived:** spatially continuous soil-depth maps and catchment-wide rates or distributions produced through interpolation, models, or calculations.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), geology, soil, and geomorphology sections.

## 5. Relationships established in the case study

### A. Precipitation → soil moisture: a measured input and response

The 2020 study compared throughfall and soil-moisture time series at different sites and depths. It found that the relationship varied by site and soil horizon. Upper horizons generally showed stronger correspondence with throughfall than deeper horizons, and the response differed across soil series and landscape positions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The precipitation signal does not appear identically at every soil depth or location. Soil profiles transform and delay the signal.

**Evidence type:** measured time series plus statistical wavelet/coherence analysis.

**Limit:** Correlation and time-lag analysis do not by themselves provide a direct image of every water pathway.

Source: Liu et al. (2020), results on throughfall and soil moisture.

### B. Soil moisture → catchment discharge: subsurface connectivity

The 2020 analysis compared soil-moisture fluctuations with catchment discharge. It found spatially variable coherence between the signals. The authors report that subsurface preferential flow likely contributes to discharge response at timescales of approximately 3–12 days, mainly through upper or middle soil horizons.

They also report that even hilltop locations may be hydrologically connected to the valley floor through subsurface preferential pathways.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Water entering the soil at one landscape position can be connected to stream discharge through subsurface pathways, rather than all water reaching the stream only by visible surface runoff.

**Evidence type:** soil-moisture and discharge records analyzed through time-series methods; the pathway contribution is inferred from signal relationships.

**Limit:** The paper explicitly discusses limitations and uncertainties. A statistical relationship is not equivalent to directly tracing every parcel of water from a particular sensor to the outlet.

Source: Liu et al. (2020), abstract and results.

### C. Soil and fractured bedrock → lateral water transport

The 2018 synthesis reports that more than 90% of water entering the catchment infiltrates the soil and fractured upper saprock and moves toward the valley along preferential flow lines through the upper soil and fractured layers. The remainder is described as infiltrating into a deeper oxidized zone and leaving as deep groundwater flow.

This is a published catchment-scale synthesis drawing on earlier studies, not a new measurement performed for this record. The same paper describes the upper soil and fractured rock as allowing lateral subsurface flow, while relatively little water recharges deep groundwater.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The local geological structure and soil mantle influence the pathways by which water moves through the catchment.

**Evidence type:** synthesis of field studies, water balance, geological characterization, and hydrological investigations.

**Limit:** The percentage is specific to the studied catchment and the cited work. It must not be generalized to other watersheds without evidence.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), soil/rock and hydrology discussion.

### D. Water ↔ weathering: chemical interaction between water and rock

The site synthesis describes mineral transformations and dissolution at different depths in the weathered shale profile. It reports that minerals including ankerite and pyrite dissolve at depth near or beneath the water table, while other mineral-weathering processes occur higher in the fractured zone and soil.

This supports a direct geochemical relationship: water moving through soil and rock participates in mineral reactions and transports dissolved constituents.

**Evidence type:** mineralogical and geochemical measurements interpreted within a geological and hydrological model.

**Limit:** The detailed chemical rates, reaction pathways, and spatial distributions require the underlying specialist studies. This record does not independently calculate them.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), rock and soil characterization.

### E. Vegetation ↔ water and energy exchange

The observatory measures vegetation, soil moisture, precipitation, and energy/carbon exchange. The published synthesis reports that forest composition and productivity vary with topographic position and aspect. It also notes that changes in tree mortality and loss of dominant species can affect carbon storage, evapotranspiration, and streamflow.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Vegetation is not merely located within the hydrological system; vegetation participates in water and energy exchange, and changes in forest condition can alter those exchanges.

**Evidence type:** tree inventories, litter measurements, tower flux observations, and ecological/hydrological synthesis.

**Limit:** The direction and magnitude of a particular mortality event's effect on streamflow require dedicated analysis. A stated potential effect is not a measured causal quantity for every event.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), vegetation and instrumentation sections.

## 7. Cross-disciplinary relationship map

The case supports the following relationships:

1. **Atmospheric science ↔ hydrology:** precipitation supplies water to the catchment; evapotranspiration and energy exchange connect land and vegetation to the atmosphere.
2. **Hydrology ↔ soil science:** soil moisture, storage, pore structure, and infiltration affect the timing and pathways of water movement.
3. **Hydrology ↔ geology:** fractures and weathered shale influence subsurface pathways and groundwater exchange.
4. **Geology ↔ geochemistry:** water-rock interaction participates in mineral dissolution and weathering.
5. **Hydrology ↔ geomorphology:** topography and hillslope form influence water convergence and the movement of material toward the valley.
6. **Ecology ↔ hydrology:** forest condition and vegetation are connected to evapotranspiration, water use, and streamflow.
7. **Ecology ↔ biogeochemistry:** vegetation, litter, soil organic carbon, and nutrient cycling connect living systems to soil and water chemistry.

**Synthesis:** The relationships are not merely adjacent topics. They are documented interfaces in which changes or processes in one domain can affect another. The disciplines remain distinct because they investigate different variables, mechanisms, scales, and questions.
