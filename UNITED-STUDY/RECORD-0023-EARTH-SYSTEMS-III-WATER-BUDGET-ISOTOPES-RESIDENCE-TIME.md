## 3. Water budget: accounting is not the same as direct measurement

A watershed water balance is commonly expressed as:

**Change in water storage = precipitation − evapotranspiration − stream discharge − net subsurface export/import**, with terms and sign conventions defined for the particular control volume and accounting period.

This is an accounting relationship. It does not mean every term is directly measured at every location. In a real watershed, precipitation and streamflow may be instrumented, evapotranspiration may be estimated from flux measurements or models, storage may be sampled through soil sensors and wells, and deep groundwater exchange may be poorly constrained.

### A. Published Shale Hills model example

Brantley et al. (2018) discuss a modeled water balance for 1 April through 31 December 2009. For that interval, the article reports 0.9 m of total precipitation. The model output apportioned annual precipitation as approximately:

- 39.7% to stream discharge;
- 58.8% to total evapotranspiration;
- 1.5% to water storage.

These are **model-derived watershed averages for the stated time period and model configuration**, not three independently measured quantities with equal evidentiary status. The article explains that watershed averages were calculated from gridded daily model output. It also reports that the groundwater influx term was unmeasured and tuned to reproduce stream chemistry.

The reported percentages therefore must not be presented as a closed, fully measured water budget. They are a modeled accounting result constrained by available observations and calibration choices.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), temporal-spatial patterns of water and discussion of the 2009 model.

### B. Seasonal storage and evapotranspiration

The same synthesis describes seasonal behavior: evapotranspiration rises from spring into summer and falls in winter; water storage decreases during summer as evapotranspiration increases. Monitoring-well water levels provide an observational line of evidence for seasonal changes in subsurface storage.

This is a relationship between atmospheric energy, vegetation, water loss, and subsurface storage. The model helps integrate those relationships, but individual observations and model estimates remain distinguishable.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018).

## 4. Stable isotopes: tracing water sources and mixing

Hydrogen and oxygen in water occur in different stable-isotope proportions. The cited Shale Hills studies use the ratios commonly written as δ²H and δ¹⁸O. These isotope signatures vary in precipitation and can be followed through soil water, groundwater, and stream water.

The value of the method is not that an isotope labels each water molecule with a unique route. Rather, changes in isotope composition across sampled water stores can reveal mixing, seasonal input signatures, and the damping of signals as water moves through and is stored in the catchment.

### A. Sampling network

The isotope research used:
- precipitation samples collected during precipitation events;
- soil water sampled at multiple depths and along several transects;
- groundwater sampled from wells;
- stream water sampled at the catchment outlet and other specified locations.

The observatory overview reports more than 4,700 water samples analyzed from June 2008 through December 2011. The peer-reviewed isotope study describes more than three years of monitoring from 2008–2012, with different sampling frequencies for precipitation, soil water, groundwater, and stream water.

This is substantial multi-compartment sampling, but it remains a network of defined points and intervals rather than continuous observation of all water throughout the catchment.

Sources: Shale Hills isotope hydrology overview; peer-reviewed stable-isotope study.

### B. Seasonal signal changes with depth

The peer-reviewed isotope study reports that seasonal isotope variation is strongest in precipitation and shallow soil water, becomes less variable at intermediate depths, and is strongly damped in deeper soil water and groundwater.

The groundwater isotope composition remained comparatively stable through the year and close to the amount-weighted annual precipitation signature. The authors interpret this as evidence that groundwater receives recharge from precipitation across seasons, while recharge mechanisms differ between growing and non-growing seasons.

The study also reports evidence for preferential flow in both vertical and lateral directions, with patterns varying by season and depth.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The water sampled at different depths and in different stores carries different degrees of the seasonal precipitation signal. This supports mixing and storage along subsurface pathways.

**Limit:** A sampled isotope signature does not reveal every path or uniquely identify every parcel's age. The interpretation depends on sampling design, hydrological assumptions, and the mixing behavior of the stores.

Source: Stable-isotope study (2013).

## 5. Residence time: there is not one age for all the water

A crucial distinction emerges from the literature: **residence time depends on which water store or flow domain is being discussed and on the method used to estimate it.**

### A. Shallow subsurface water

Jin et al. (2011) used seasonal damping of hydrogen and oxygen isotope signals between precipitation and subsurface water to estimate residence times under a well-mixed-pool assumption. Their reported estimates include approximately 0.5 years for soil water in a high-flow zone and 1.6 years for stream water using δD; estimates using δ¹⁸O include approximately 0.5 years for soil water and 0.8 years for groundwater.

The authors explicitly note substantial uncertainty in these estimates. They interpret the results as indicating that average water residence in the studied system is on the order of years, while also describing faster preferential pathways and slower, less-mobile stores.

### B. Shallow interflow and deep groundwater

A separate synthesis of Shale Hills and related lithological research distinguishes shallow interflow from deep groundwater. It reports shallow interflow residence times on the order of approximately 1–5 years, while deep groundwater residence times are estimated at approximately 20–30 years using water isotopes and SF₆ tracers.

These values refer to different flow domains and a different synthesis of evidence. They are not directly interchangeable with the 0.5-, 0.8-, or 1.6-year estimates above.

### C. Preliminary catchment-age estimate

The observatory isotope-hydrology overview reports preliminary estimates of water age in the catchment ranging from 4–9 months, with a mean of approximately 6 months. This is explicitly described as preliminary work using an age model that incorporates transient hydrodynamics and tracer information.

It is not a universal residence time for all Shale Hills water. It is a model-based preliminary catchment-age result from a particular research stage and method.

### D. Reconciliation

The different estimates should not be forced into one number:

| Reported estimate | Water domain / method | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 years | Soil water in a high-flow zone; isotope seasonal-amplitude method | Published estimate with uncertainty |
| 0.8 years | Groundwater estimate using δ¹⁸O in the cited study | Published estimate with uncertainty |
| 1.6 years | Stream water using δD in the cited study | Published estimate with uncertainty |
| 1–5 years | Shallow subsurface interflow in a later synthesis | Published range for a specified flow domain |
| 20–30 years | Deep groundwater, estimated using water isotopes and SF₆ tracers | Published range for a distinct deeper domain |
| 4–9 months; mean about 6 months | Preliminary catchment-age model | Preliminary model-based estimate |

These values are not necessarily contradictory. They concern different water stores, flow domains, averaging assumptions, sampling periods, and estimation methods.

**Unresolved:** A full reconciliation would require retrieving the underlying studies, defining each age/residence-time quantity precisely, and comparing assumptions, time periods, tracer models, and uncertainty intervals. This record does not claim to have completed that technical comparison.

Sources: Jin et al. (2011); stable-isotope study (2013); observatory isotope-hydrology overview; lithology/agriculture study (2018).

## 6. Water chemistry: hydrology and geology meet

Jin et al. (2011) examined magnesium concentrations and hydrogen/oxygen isotope signatures in precipitation, soil water, groundwater, and stream water.

The study reports that magnesium released through mineral dissolution in soil can move from lower-flow soil domains toward higher-flow pathways. Fresh water entering through macropores can have lower magnesium concentrations than water that has interacted longer with minerals.

The study also reports seasonal changes in the relative contribution of comparatively older groundwater and younger soil water to stream chemistry.

This gives a concrete interface:

**Water movement affects contact time and contact location with minerals; mineral reactions affect the dissolved chemical composition of water; the water carries that chemical signal toward the stream.**

This is not merely a similarity of terminology between hydrology and geology. It is a measured geochemical relationship interpreted with hydrological evidence.

**Limit:** The detailed reaction kinetics and chemical mass balance require the specialist geochemical analysis. This record does not calculate mineral dissolution rates.

Source: Jin et al. (2011).

## 7. Cross-disciplinary relationships established

1. **Atmosphere ↔ hydrology:** precipitation isotope composition provides an input signature; seasonal atmospheric conditions influence the water entering the catchment.
2. **Hydrology ↔ soil science:** isotope damping with depth and soil-moisture records reveal storage, mixing, and preferential pathways.
3. **Hydrology ↔ geology:** shallow fractured rock and deeper geological structure influence water movement and storage.
4. **Hydrology ↔ geochemistry:** water residence and flow paths influence mineral contact, dissolved constituents, and stream chemistry.
5. **Ecology ↔ hydrology:** evapotranspiration and tree water use connect forest processes to water loss and seasonal storage.
6. **Measurement science ↔ all domains:** gauges, wells, soil sensors, isotope analyzers, and models register different aspects of the same system, each with distinct uncertainty and coverage.

**Synthesis:** Water is not simply transported through a passive container. It is stored, mixed, delayed, redirected, and chemically changed as it interacts with soil, rock, and living systems. The particular pathways and residence times are conditional on the catchment's structure and state.
