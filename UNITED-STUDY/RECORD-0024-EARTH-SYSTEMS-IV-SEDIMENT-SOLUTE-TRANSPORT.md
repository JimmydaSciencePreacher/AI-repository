## 3. First distinction: dissolved solute versus suspended particle

Water samples can contain material in different physical and chemical forms.

- **Dissolved solutes:** chemical species present in solution, including ions released by mineral weathering or biological and atmospheric processes.
- **Suspended particles:** solid particles carried within moving water, including clay minerals and other fine material.
- **Deposited sediment:** material that has settled or accumulated in a channel, floodplain, valley, or other depositional setting.
- **Colloids:** very small particles dispersed in water; depending on analytical method and operational definition, they may be distinguished from truly dissolved species.

A measured dissolved concentration is not a measurement of suspended sediment concentration. A particle sample does not by itself establish the total dissolved load. Both may be transported by the same water, but they require different sampling and analytical methods.

## 4. Mineral weathering and elemental transport along the hillslope

Jin et al. (2010) examined soil cores from the ridge and a subsurface water-flow line on a planar hillslope, together with a bedrock core extending about 25 m. They used elemental depletion profiles relative to parent bedrock to investigate mineral transformations and weathering fluxes.

The study identifies several reaction fronts:
- deep carbonate depletion in bedrock;
- feldspar dissolution extending from several metres below the bedrock-soil interface upward;
- illite and chlorite reaction fronts within soils.

The study reports transformations involving chlorite and illite and the formation of secondary minerals including vermiculite, hydroxy-interlayered vermiculite, and minor kaolinite.

A key result is that elemental losses do not all leave the hillslope in the same form:
- Mg and K losses occur largely as dissolved solute fluxes;
- Al and Fe losses occur mostly through downslope transport of fine particles;
- physical erosion of bulk soil also occurs.

The authors' steady-state model estimates that physical erosion accounts for about half of total denudation at ridgetop and midslope positions. This is a model-based result, not a direct weighing of every particle removed from each hillslope position.

The study also reports that chemical weathering losses of Mg, Na, and K are higher upslope, while weathering slows toward the valley floor and Al and Si show net accumulation there.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Chemical weathering and physical erosion are distinct but interacting routes of material redistribution. Elements can be transported in solution or within particles, and their relative importance varies with landscape position.

**Limit:** The quantitative estimates depend on mass-balance assumptions, parent-material composition, erosion-rate constraints, and the steady-state interpretation. They are not universal rates for all shale landscapes.

Source: Jin et al. (2010), abstract and site-specific results.

## 5. A surprising result: particles also move underground

The 2018 study summarized by the Shale Hills CZO examined solute and particle fluxes during six storms and compared them with the longer-term chemical record preserved in the regolith.

The researchers found:
- stream suspended particles were primarily micrometre-scale, platy illite;
- particles were commonly coated with fine Al-, Fe-, and Si-rich oxides;
- stream-particle chemistry differed from surface soils except during intense dry-season rainstorms;
- at high discharge, stream particles were chemically similar to laboratory-extracted soil colloids;
- at low discharge, stream particles were more similar to groundwater particles.

The authors interpreted the change in particle composition as evidence that changing flow paths influence subsurface particle transport.

The study further estimated from regolith chemistry that cumulative particle loss at depths of 5–8 m in fractured bedrock accounted for approximately 58% of K loss and 24% of Mg loss in the studied profiles.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Particle transport is not restricted to visible surface erosion. Fine particles can be mobilized and transported through subsurface soil and fractured rock, contributing to long-term changes in rock and soil chemistry.

**Evidence status:** The storm sampling and particle analyses are empirical; the long-term elemental contributions are reconstructed from geochemical profiles and the study's interpretation.

**Limit:** The six storms are a finite sample of event conditions. The reported percentages refer to the studied regolith chemistry and depth range, not a universal watershed proportion.

Source: Particle fluxes in groundwater change subsurface shale rock chemistry over geologic time (2018), DOI 10.1016/j.epsl.2018.07.031; Shale Hills CZO annual findings summary.

## 6. Solute transport and concentration-discharge relationships

A stream's solute concentration can change as discharge changes. The relationship is often examined by plotting concentration against discharge (C–Q).

Two terms are useful:

- **Chemostatic behavior:** concentration remains comparatively stable despite substantial changes in discharge.
- **Chemodynamic behavior:** concentration changes systematically as discharge changes, including dilution or enrichment.

The Shale Hills synthesis reports that geogenic species associated with soil weathering, including Na, K, Mg, and Si, exhibit chemostatic behavior. Several bio-relevant species, including Ca, Mn, Fe, and dissolved organic carbon, exhibit chemodynamic behavior, with concentrations decreasing as discharge increases.

The synthesis explains that the underlying sources and their spatial distributions differ. Soil minerals are widespread, while soil organic carbon and biologically cycled elements are more spatially heterogeneous.

A reactive-transport model study by Li et al. (2017) examined chloride and magnesium. The model represents multiple flow contributions, including overland flow, lateral subsurface flow through soil, and deeper groundwater. Its simulations indicate that lateral soil flow contributes more than 70% of stream discharge in the modeled case. During wet periods, a larger connected portion of the watershed can contribute water and mobilize solutes that were less connected to the stream during dry conditions.

For magnesium, the model links clay dissolution to soil water content and lateral flow. This provides a mechanism by which increased water volume can be accompanied by increased magnesium supply, partly counteracting simple dilution.

**Evidence distinction:** The observed C–Q patterns are based on stream chemistry and discharge records. The detailed partitioning of flow contributions and the proposed process explanations are model results and interpretations, not direct measurements of every source contribution.

Sources: Brantley et al. (2018), C–Q section; Li et al. (2017).

## 7. Biology changes the route and timing of element export

Manganese provides a particularly useful example of the connection between geology, soil chemistry, vegetation, and stream export.

The Shale Hills synthesis reports that:
- forest vegetation takes up manganese from soils;
- annual Mn uptake by vegetation exceeds the rate of leaching from ridge soils by more than twenty times in the cited study;
- manganese is returned to soil through leaf litter;
- during litter decomposition, manganese can be oxidized and immobilized as Mn oxides;
- organic-rich swales contain higher dissolved Mn concentrations and can deliver Mn to the stream.

The synthesis concludes that biological cycling increases Mn retention in the ecosystem and slows its release from soils into streams.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** An element's movement is not determined only by rock dissolution and water flow. Biological uptake, storage in biomass, litter return, and chemical transformation can affect its retention and export.

**Limit:** The reported rates and patterns are site- and element-specific. They do not establish that vegetation retains every element or that all watersheds behave similarly.

Sources: Brantley et al. (2018), manganese cycling section; Herndon et al. (2015).

## 8. What the sediment dataset does—and does not—establish

A HydroShare resource documents turbidity observations collected upstream of the Shale Hills weir during 2013–2016.

The dataset description includes an important warning: the turbidity probe placement was not ideal because it had to be installed in the weir pond to remain submerged. The description notes that the stilling well installed with the new flume in 2015 improved the situation.

Turbidity is an optical measurement related to suspended material in the water. It is not automatically a direct measurement of sediment mass concentration. A site-specific calibration against collected suspended-sediment samples is generally needed to convert turbidity into suspended sediment concentration, and discharge is needed to calculate a time-integrated sediment load.

**Therefore this metadata record alone does not establish a validated sediment yield for Shale Hills.** It establishes that a turbidity dataset exists and that its own creators documented a placement limitation.

Source: HydroShare, SSHCZO Streamflow / Discharge, Stream Suspended Sediment — Shale Hills (2013–2016).

## 10. Cross-disciplinary relationship map

1. **Hydrology ↔ sedimentology:** water flow mobilizes, transports, and deposits particles; discharge and flow pathways influence which particles reach the stream.
2. **Hydrology ↔ geochemistry:** water contact, residence, and flow paths influence dissolution, solute concentrations, and chemical export.
3. **Geology ↔ soil science:** parent shale mineralogy and weathering fronts influence soil composition and the availability of particles and dissolved elements.
4. **Geomorphology ↔ hydrology:** slope, topography, and convergent flow paths influence erosion, lateral transport, and deposition.
5. **Ecology ↔ biogeochemistry:** vegetation uptake, litter return, and microbial or chemical transformations affect element retention and export.
6. **Measurement science ↔ environmental modeling:** sensor readings, water samples, particle analyses, discharge measurements, and models contribute different kinds of evidence. Their outputs must retain their respective limitations.

**Synthesis:** The same water movement can transport dissolved substances and particles, but the sources, mechanisms, and measurement methods differ. Material can be chemically transformed, biologically retained, moved through subsurface pathways, or deposited before it reaches the watershed outlet.
