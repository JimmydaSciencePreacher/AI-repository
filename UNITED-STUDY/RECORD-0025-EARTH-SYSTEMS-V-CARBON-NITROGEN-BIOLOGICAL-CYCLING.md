## 3. Carbon: movement between vegetation, soil, water, and atmosphere

### A. Vegetation fixes carbon and redistributes it

The Shale Hills watershed is a mature, predominantly oak forest with a diverse canopy. The 2018 synthesis reports aboveground net primary productivity of approximately 443 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ for the watershed. It reports that approximately 36% of annual aboveground carbon productivity enters leaf litter and 64% enters wood growth.

These are watershed-level estimates assembled from vegetation measurements and scaling. They describe aboveground productivity, not the entire carbon budget and not the amount of carbon permanently stored.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Photosynthetic uptake transfers atmospheric carbon into plant biomass. Carbon is then allocated to wood, leaves, roots, and other plant tissues; litter and root turnover transfer organic carbon into soils.

**Limit:** Net primary productivity is not identical to long-term sequestration. Carbon can return to the atmosphere through plant and microbial respiration, or move into water and other stores.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), vegetation and carbon-budget sections.

### B. Soil respiration returns carbon to the atmosphere

Soil respiration is the release of CO₂ from plant roots and soil heterotrophic organisms. Shale Hills research has monitored soil CO₂ concentrations and fluxes across topographic positions, including concave and planar slopes from ridge to toe slope. These measurements have been coupled with soil temperature, moisture, oxygen, and nitrous oxide measurements.

The Penn State research overview identifies geology and topography as controls on soil CO₂ accumulation and soil-to-atmosphere flux. It also identifies hydrologic export of dissolved inorganic carbon and iron redox reactions as important controls on soil CO₂ behavior.

This is a multi-domain relationship:
- vegetation and roots contribute respiratory carbon;
- microorganisms decompose organic matter and respire carbon;
- soil water and pore structure influence gas transport and storage;
- oxygen availability and redox chemistry influence transformations;
- topography and geology affect where water, gases, and reactants accumulate.

**Limit:** A soil CO₂ concentration is not the same as a surface CO₂ flux. A point flux measurement is not automatically a watershed-integrated annual flux. Spatial and temporal scaling must be justified.

Sources: Penn State Carbon Cycling in the Critical Zone overview; Hodges et al. (2019).

### C. Carbon can leave by water as well as gas

The carbon research overview identifies hydrologic export of dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) as a significant control in Shale Hills soil CO₂ dynamics.

This means carbon in the soil system does not have only one exit route. Some carbon returns to the atmosphere as CO₂; some can be transported in water as dissolved inorganic carbon; dissolved organic carbon is another distinct aquatic pathway.

These forms must not be merged:
- **CO₂ gas flux:** carbon crossing the soil-atmosphere interface as a gas.
- **DIC:** dissolved inorganic carbon species carried in water.
- **DOC:** dissolved organic carbon compounds carried in water.
- **Particulate organic carbon:** carbon transported with suspended solid material.

A concentration alone does not give a carbon export flux. A flux calculation requires concentration and water discharge over matched time intervals, with compatible units and sampling coverage.

**Unresolved:** The exact quantitative DIC and DOC export values, uncertainty ranges, and their partition by season and flow path are not reproduced here. The cited overview identifies the research but does not supply enough detail for a defensible watershed carbon-export calculation.

Sources: Penn State Carbon Cycling in the Critical Zone overview; Shale Hills biogeochemistry overview.

## 4. Nitrogen: inputs, uptake, retention, and losses

Nitrogen has a different set of pathways from carbon. Shale Hills is especially instructive because atmospheric deposition, nitrogen-bearing shale, plant uptake, microbial transformations, gaseous losses, and stream export all contribute to the watershed nitrogen balance.

### A. Nitrogen enters from more than one source

The 2018 Shale Hills synthesis reports recent atmospheric nitrogen deposition of approximately:
- 3–5 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ in precipitation;
- 1–2 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ in particulate matter.

It also reports that approximately 12% of nitrogen inputs at Shale Hills derive from weathering of shale. The 2018 nitrogen study gives an estimate of approximately 11% from rock weathering.

This is an important geological-biological connection: nitrogen is not exclusively an atmospheric input in this shale-based watershed. Parent material contributes nitrogen to the ecosystem as it weathers.

**Limit:** These are study-specific estimates and budget categories. Their periods and methods must be checked before combining them into a new total.

Sources: Brantley et al. (2018); Weitzman and Kaye (2018).

### B. Plants and soils retain nitrogen

The 2017 nitrogen-budget abstract reports a preliminary watershed nitrogen balance in which inputs exceeded outputs by approximately 4.3 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, equivalent to retention of about 54% of estimated inputs.

The abstract reports a high plant nitrogen uptake estimate of 18.3 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹. The 2018 synthesis describes tree uptake as perhaps 9–18 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ and notes that the forest canopy trees were approximately 120 years old.

The synthesis discusses possible explanations for the apparent retention: nitrogen may be accumulating in vegetation or soils, or some inputs and outputs may be unmeasured. Because soil nitrogen and bulk density vary spatially, changes in total soil nitrogen were difficult to establish directly.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Nitrogen entering the watershed is not necessarily exported immediately in stream water. It can be taken up into plant biomass, retained in soil organic matter, or transformed by biological and chemical processes.

**Limit:** Plant uptake is an internal transfer within the watershed, not automatically a permanent ecosystem loss or a net increase in stored nitrogen. Repeated uptake and litter return are part of internal cycling.

Sources: Weitzman and Kaye (2017) conference abstract; Brantley et al. (2018).

### C. Nitrogen returns to the atmosphere through gaseous pathways

Weitzman and Kaye (2018) measured surface-to-atmosphere N₂O fluxes along a topographic gradient and examined soil O₂, soil CO₂, pore-water nitrate, and other nitrogen-cycle indicators.

The paper's abstract reports that N₂O flux represented approximately 20% of nitrogen export from Shale Hills. The observatory's annual findings summary reports N₂O fluxes at approximately 19% of total ecosystem nitrogen losses and nearly twice the stream nitrogen export.

The study found that convergent flow-path locations had significantly higher surface N₂O fluxes than nonconvergent locations during summer, but not in other seasons. The researchers associated flux variation with shallow soil conditions, including oxygen and nitrate patterns.

The paper's nitrogen budget estimated total inputs of approximately 8 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ and outputs of approximately 3.7 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹. These estimates imply a difference of about 4.3 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ between estimated inputs and outputs. The budget includes measured and estimated terms; it is not a complete direct measurement of every nitrogen pathway.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Nitrogen can leave a watershed through gaseous pathways, not only through stream-water export. Soil microbial transformations and oxygen/water conditions can influence the production and release of N₂O.

**Limit:** N₂O is only one gaseous nitrogen pathway. A complete nitrogen budget also requires attention to N₂ and other relevant forms, deposition, biological fixation if present, storage changes, and possible unmeasured losses.

Sources: Weitzman and Kaye (2018); Shale Hills annual research findings.

## 5. A crucial comparison: carbon and nitrogen do not share one cycle

Carbon and nitrogen interact in soil organic matter, vegetation, microbial metabolism, and water transport. Yet their transformations and budgets are not interchangeable.

| Dimension | Carbon | Nitrogen |
|---|---|---|
| Major biological entry | Photosynthetic carbon fixation | Biological assimilation of available nitrogen |
| Important stores | Wood, leaves, roots, soil organic matter, dissolved pools | Biomass, soil organic matter, mineral and dissolved pools |
| Atmospheric exchange | CO₂ uptake and release; other carbon gases may matter in broader budgets | N₂O and N₂ production/loss; atmospheric deposition |
| Aquatic transport | DIC, DOC, particulate organic carbon | Nitrate, ammonium, dissolved organic nitrogen, particulate nitrogen |
| Geological contribution at Shale Hills | Mineral and rock interactions affect carbon chemistry and storage | Shale weathering is a documented nitrogen input |
| Main caution | Soil CO₂ concentration is not the same as ecosystem carbon balance | Plant uptake is not the same as permanent nitrogen storage |

**SYNTHESIS:** Carbon and nitrogen are coupled through organisms and organic matter, but each element has distinct chemical forms, sources, sinks, and measurement requirements. A shared ecosystem does not make their budgets identical.

## 6. Cross-disciplinary relationships established

1. **Atmosphere ↔ vegetation:** photosynthesis transfers carbon into biomass; atmospheric deposition supplies nitrogen to the forest.
2. **Geology ↔ biogeochemistry:** weathering of shale contributes nitrogen and influences the chemical setting in which carbon and nutrient transformations occur.
3. **Vegetation ↔ soil:** roots, litter, and biomass turnover transfer carbon and nitrogen between plant and soil pools.
4. **Microbiology ↔ soil chemistry:** microbial respiration and nitrogen transformations affect gas production, nutrient availability, and chemical conditions.
5. **Hydrology ↔ carbon and nitrogen:** water can transport DIC, DOC, nitrate, and other dissolved constituents; water content and flow paths also influence oxygen and redox conditions.
6. **Topography ↔ biogeochemistry:** convergent flow paths create spatially distinct water, oxygen, and reactant conditions that can affect soil gas fluxes.
7. **Measurement science ↔ ecosystem budgets:** chamber fluxes, soil gas profiles, vegetation inventories, stream chemistry, deposition estimates, and models measure different parts of the system and require careful scaling.

These are evidence-supported interfaces, not proof that the underlying processes are identical or governed by one interchangeable mechanism.
