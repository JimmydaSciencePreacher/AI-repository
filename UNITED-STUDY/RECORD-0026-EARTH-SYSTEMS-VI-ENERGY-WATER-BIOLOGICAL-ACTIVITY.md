## 3. Energy at the land surface: radiation is an input and output, not the whole energy budget

The Shale Hills tower includes a four-component radiometer that measures incoming and outgoing shortwave (solar) and longwave (terrestrial infrared) radiation. The site synthesis also describes an eddy-covariance system measuring turbulent exchanges of sensible heat, water vapor (used to estimate evapotranspiration and latent heat), momentum, and carbon dioxide.

The site synthesis reports that fluxes are estimated at 30-minute resolution. The radiometer measures hemispherically integrated radiation; the eddy-covariance footprint represents a changing upwind area, approximately 1 km² in the site synthesis, with its location and size varying with wind direction and atmospheric stability.

A conceptual surface energy balance is often written:

**Net radiation = sensible heat flux + latent heat flux + ground heat storage/flux + other storage and residual terms**, with sign conventions and included terms explicitly defined.

This is an accounting framework, not a claim that every term is measured perfectly at Shale Hills. Canopy and biomass heat storage, soil heat flux, advection, terrain effects, and measurement closure can matter. The exact form depends on the control volume and measurement setup.

### Evidentiary distinction

- **Radiation measurements:** incoming and outgoing shortwave and longwave components.
- **Eddy covariance:** turbulent vertical exchanges, including sensible heat and water vapor, under appropriate turbulence and stationarity conditions.
- **Latent heat:** energy associated with water changing phase; derived from water-vapor flux.
- **Evapotranspiration:** water transfer to the atmosphere through evaporation and plant transpiration; tower water-vapor flux does not automatically separate the two.
- **Energy-balance residual:** the remaining difference after accounting for measured and estimated terms; it should not be silently assigned to a process.

The Shale Hills synthesis cautions that eddy-covariance estimates are most representative when turbulence is sufficiently vigorous and the atmospheric surface-layer exchange is approximately one-dimensional and stationary. Screening is needed under unsuitable conditions.

Source: Brantley et al. (2018), instrumentation and eddy-covariance description.

## 4. Evapotranspiration: a coupled water and energy pathway

Evapotranspiration (ET) combines evaporation from soil, intercepted water, and other wet surfaces with transpiration through plants. It transfers water from the land surface to the atmosphere and consumes energy as latent heat.

The Shale Hills tower measures land-atmosphere water-vapor exchange and estimates ET from eddy covariance. However, that tower measurement does not by itself partition total ET into soil evaporation, canopy-interception evaporation, and plant transpiration.

A separate line of research at Shale Hills uses tree sap-flow sensors and hydrogen/oxygen stable isotopes to investigate tree water use and the depth from which trees obtain water. This connects plant physiology with soil hydrology and isotope hydrology.

The archived Shale Hills hydrology thesis describes a strong seasonal pattern: during the growing season, transpiration uses a large portion of soil water and shallow groundwater; the summer period accounts for about 30% of annual precipitation, while producing little significant groundwater recharge in the cited interpretation. As evapotranspiration declines in late September and October, rainfall can replenish soil water and increase groundwater recharge.

This is a seasonal interpretation of the catchment's hydrological behavior, not a universal calendar rule. It depends on temperature, vegetation activity, antecedent moisture, rainfall, soil conditions, and the specific years studied.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Plant water use is one pathway linking biological activity, atmospheric energy demand, soil-water storage, and groundwater recharge.

**Limit:** A catchment-wide ET estimate and an individual tree's sap flow are different spatial measurements. Neither alone determines the exact contribution of every plant, soil surface, and canopy store.

Sources: Brantley et al. (2018); Penn State Shale Hills tree-water-use project; Shale Hills hydrology thesis.

## 5. Soil moisture and temperature are spatially differentiated

Takagi et al. (2011) analyzed four years of volumetric soil-moisture measurements from 78 locations across the 7.9-hectare Shale Hills catchment, with measurements from the surface to 1.1 m depth.

The study reports that spatial variability in soil moisture increased exponentially as catchment-wide wetness increased, across measurement depths and soil-landform units. The increase coincided with the emergence of a shallow water table in the valley.

A separate GroundHOG dataset description documents soil moisture, electrical conductivity, and soil temperature measurements at three depths across eight sites in the wider Shale Hills/Garner Run field program. This is metadata describing the measurement network; it is not by itself evidence for a particular temperature or moisture trend.

The measurements show why a single soil-moisture value cannot stand in for the entire watershed. Soil moisture varies with location, depth, landform, and the overall wetness state of the catchment.

### Model-based topographic interpretation

Shi et al. (2018) used a coupled spatially distributed hydrological-biogeochemical model to examine spatial patterns in soil moisture, soil temperature, incoming solar radiation, and carbon processes.

The model predicted that valley floors and swales were wetter than planar slopes. It also predicted that north-facing planar slopes were slightly wetter than south-facing planar slopes, especially in the root zone, primarily because of lower modeled evapotranspiration on the north-facing slope.

These are model results, not direct measurements of every point on each slope. They offer a process-based interpretation to be tested against observations.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Topography, soil hydraulic properties, radiation exposure, and evapotranspiration can combine to produce different soil-water conditions across a relatively small watershed.

Sources: Takagi et al. (2011); HydroShare GroundHOG metadata; Shi et al. (2018).

## 6. Biological activity and soil respiration: temperature and moisture are conditions, not interchangeable causes

Soil organisms and plant roots respire carbon, and microbial decomposition transforms organic matter. Soil temperature, water content, oxygen availability, substrate supply, and soil structure influence these processes.

A Shale Hills field study summarized in a 2017 U.S. Department of Energy meeting abstract monitored soil temperature, moisture, and respiration across the catchment during the 2015 growing season. It used eight automated chambers and repeated measurements at 50 plots. The abstract reports substantial spatial variation in cumulative growing-season soil respiration, from 484 to 1,449 g C m⁻² across the landscape, with a mean of 855 ± 27 g C m⁻².

These figures are reported from a conference abstract and must retain that source status. They are growing-season measurements and must not be represented as annual ecosystem respiration or a complete carbon budget.

The result demonstrates spatial heterogeneity in soil respiration at the site. Temperature and moisture are relevant conditions, but the measured spatial range alone does not isolate the causal contribution of either variable. A causal explanation requires the study's statistical analysis and consideration of correlated factors such as topography, substrate, roots, and oxygen.

**Limit:** Soil respiration is not synonymous with microbial respiration alone; roots also contribute. Nor does soil respiration alone establish net ecosystem carbon exchange, which also depends on photosynthesis and other fluxes.

Source: Chen et al., DOE ESS PI Meeting 2017 abstract, “Influence of Topography on Soil Respiration in the Susquehanna-Shale Hills Critical Zone Observatory.”

## 7. Cross-disciplinary relationships established

1. **Radiation ↔ surface energy:** incoming and outgoing shortwave and longwave radiation contribute to the land-surface energy balance.
2. **Energy ↔ hydrology:** latent heat is linked to water-vapor exchange and evapotranspiration; energy availability helps condition evaporation and transpiration.
3. **Vegetation ↔ hydrology:** plant water uptake transfers water from soil and shallow groundwater to the atmosphere.
4. **Topography ↔ radiation and water:** slope orientation and terrain affect radiation exposure and water redistribution; model results predict related moisture differences at Shale Hills.
5. **Soil physics ↔ hydrology:** soil texture, hydraulic properties, depth, and water-table position influence soil-moisture patterns and storage.
6. **Soil moisture and temperature ↔ biology:** these conditions influence root and microbial activity, but their effects must be separated from other environmental variables.
7. **Measurement science ↔ modeling:** tower footprints, point sensors, plot chambers, catchment networks, and model grids represent different spatial and temporal scales.

**Synthesis:** Energy and water are coupled through surface-atmosphere exchange. Vegetation participates in that coupling through transpiration, while soil properties and topography distribute water and thermal conditions unevenly. Biological activity responds to these conditions and also changes the exchange of water and carbon.
