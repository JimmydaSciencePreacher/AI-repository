## 1. First discriminating question

**Why can similar rainfall events produce different streamflow and runoff responses in the same watershed?**

The investigation focuses on the interaction between precipitation and the watershed's pre-event condition: soil moisture, groundwater storage, season, vegetation water use, soil and bedrock properties, and flow-path connectivity.

A particularly valuable source is the Shale Hills artificial-irrigation experiment described by Qu and Duffy (2007). It reports a sequence of equal-rate, equal-duration artificial rainfall events applied as antecedent moisture increased. A later study by Scaife et al. (2020) examines nonlinear quickflow response using hourly precipitation, soil-moisture, groundwater, and streamflow observations.

This record distinguishes:
- rainfall input from throughfall and water reaching the soil;
- rainfall intensity from antecedent watershed wetness;
- infiltration-excess overland flow from subsurface and saturation-related responses;
- quickflow from total streamflow;
- direct field observations from model interpretation;
- the original Shale Hills catchment from nearby subcatchments.

## 3. The discriminating field experiment: same imposed rainfall, changing antecedent moisture

Qu and Duffy (2007) describe the Shale Hills watershed experiment conducted from July to September 1974. A spray-irrigation network applied artificial rainfall beneath the tree canopy and above the forest litter, across the watershed. This placement avoided canopy interception storage for the artificial irrigation treatments.

The selected sequence consisted of six equal artificial rainfall events, each imposed at **0.64 cm per hour for six hours**. The events were timed so that antecedent moisture increased from very dry conditions before the first event toward near-saturation before the last.

The researchers measured forcing and runoff at 15-minute intervals. They reported:
- approximately 12% of rainfall became runoff for the first event, under very dry antecedent conditions;
- approximately 63% became runoff for the sixth event, under very wet antecedent conditions;
- peak flow and total runoff increased as antecedent moisture increased.

The paper reports that significant infiltration-excess overland flow was not observed during the experiment. The authors considered the soil's infiltration capacity apparently sufficient for the imposed rainfall rate, while cautioning that the deep forest litter made that observation difficult to interpret conclusively.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** In this particular controlled sequence, equal-rate and equal-duration rainfall inputs produced substantially different runoff yields as the pre-event soil and groundwater conditions changed.

**Important experimental limit:** The events were sequential, not independent randomized trials. The watershed's state changed between events by design. The experiment demonstrates the importance of antecedent condition in this setting; it does not isolate every possible mechanism or establish a universal runoff ratio.

Source: Qu and Duffy (2007), Shale Hills field experiment, sections 6.1–6.3.

## 4. Why antecedent condition matters

Antecedent moisture means the water condition of the soil and subsurface before a new rainfall event. It is not just a surface description. It can include:
- water already stored in unsaturated soil;
- saturated soil thickness and shallow groundwater level;
- the distribution of wet and dry zones across the hillslope;
- the connection of saturated or preferential-flow areas to the channel.

Qu and Duffy (2007) report that the experiment's runoff response increased with antecedent moisture and discuss the relationship between unsaturated soil-water storage and saturated groundwater storage. Their coupled model was used to examine how initial moisture conditions could amplify peak flows and how microtopography influences surface saturation and connectivity.

The distinction is essential: a wet soil profile and a rising shallow water table are related but are not identical measurements. A model linking them is a process interpretation, not a substitute for the separate observations.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** A new rainfall input encounters an existing hydrologic state. The resulting response depends in part on how much storage is available and how water is connected to pathways that can deliver it to the stream.

## 5. Modern evidence: quickflow response is nonlinear

Scaife et al. (2020) analyzed multi-year hourly soil-moisture, groundwater, streamflow, and precipitation records from three forested headwater catchments, including Shale Hills.

Their findings include:
- maximum hourly rainfall did not significantly contribute to quickflow production at the study sites, which the authors related to highly conductive forest soils;
- variable subsurface states contributed to nonlinear runoff behavior;
- at Shale Hills, quickflow was almost entirely related to groundwater fluctuations;
- the relative runoff-generation pathways differed among the catchments in the study.

This result is important because it cautions against assuming that the most intense hour of rainfall is always the dominant predictor of quickflow. At the studied Shale Hills site, groundwater dynamics were especially informative.

**Limit:** This finding is specific to the analyzed catchments, data, and period. It does not mean rainfall intensity is irrelevant in every forest, soil, storm, or watershed. Nor does “quickflow related to groundwater fluctuations” mean that all streamflow is groundwater or that rainfall is unnecessary.

Source: Scaife et al. (2020), abstract and study results.

## 6. The seasonal state of Shale Hills changes the response

The Shale Hills site synthesis and Holmes (2011) thesis describe seasonal changes in water storage and flow paths.

### Growing season

From late June into mid-September, transpiration uses a substantial portion of soil water and shallow groundwater. Holmes reports that this interval accounts for about 30% of annual precipitation, while producing little significant recharge in the interpretation of the cited site studies.

### Autumn transition

As evapotranspiration declines in late September and October, rainfall can replenish soil water and increase groundwater recharge.

### Winter

Low temperatures can freeze the ground and reduce infiltration. Holmes reports that, in some years, the water table can fall below the channel during periods of frozen ground. Recharge resumes when temperatures remain above freezing long enough for thawing and snowmelt.

### Spring

Recharge can continue until increasing vegetation activity and evapotranspiration reduce vertical soil-water movement.

These are site-specific seasonal interpretations, not fixed calendar rules. They vary with temperature, vegetation, precipitation, antecedent storage, and year-to-year conditions.

Sources: Holmes (2011); Brantley et al. (2018).

## 7. Water does not travel through only one route

The sources identify several relevant pathways:
- infiltration through the soil surface;
- preferential movement through macropores associated with roots and animal burrows;
- lateral subsurface flow near soil horizons and the soil-bedrock interface;
- shallow groundwater movement toward the channel;
- brief surface-runoff pulses during some rainfall events;
- streamflow from a mixture of contributing water sources that changes with hydrologic condition.

Holmes (2011) summarizes evidence for preferential flow paths and lateral movement along soil interfaces. The thesis describes valley return flow when soils become saturated during large precipitation events, while also noting that overland flow is generally limited at Shale Hills.

Brantley et al. (2018) summarize model output for April–December 2009: during rainfall events, modeled surface runoff appears as short-lived early pulses, followed by longer-duration lateral subsurface flow. During summer low-flow conditions, modeled stream discharge is dominated by groundwater contribution. The synthesis reports lateral subsurface flow as contributing more than 70% of discharge during most of the year in that modeled period.

**Evidence distinction:** The 2009 flow partition is model output informed by site observations and parameterization. It is not a direct measurement of every water molecule's pathway.

## 8. Canopy interception and throughfall: a boundary that must be preserved

Natural rainfall first encounters the canopy. Some water may be temporarily stored on leaves and branches, evaporate, or reach the forest floor as throughfall or stemflow. The amount reaching the soil therefore need not equal the rainfall measured above the canopy.

The 1974 artificial-irrigation experiment deliberately applied water below the tree canopy, above the forest litter. It therefore did not reproduce the canopy-interception part of a natural rainfall event.

This is a critical experimental distinction:
- **Above-canopy precipitation** is the atmospheric input.
- **Throughfall** is water passing through canopy openings or dripping from foliage and branches.
- **Stemflow** is water routed down plant stems and trunks.
- **Interception loss** is water retained by vegetation and subsequently evaporated.

The sources consulted for this record do not establish a defensible quantitative annual canopy-interception budget for the original Shale Hills catchment. No numerical percentage is assigned here.

## 9. Cross-disciplinary relationships established

1. **Atmospheric science ↔ hydrology:** precipitation supplies water, but the watershed's response depends on the pre-event condition and flow pathways.
2. **Vegetation ↔ hydrology:** transpiration changes soil-water and shallow-groundwater storage, especially during the growing season.
3. **Soil physics ↔ groundwater:** unsaturated soil storage and saturated groundwater storage are coupled but distinct states.
4. **Topography ↔ flow connectivity:** microtopography, swales, valley positions, and soil interfaces influence where water accumulates and how it reaches the channel.
5. **Geology ↔ hydrology:** soil depth and low-permeability shale bedrock influence subsurface storage and lateral movement.
6. **Isotope hydrology ↔ flow-path interpretation:** δ²H and δ¹⁸O observations help distinguish water sources, seasonal recharge, and residence-time behavior.
7. **Experimental science ↔ modeling:** controlled irrigation tests a defined response, while coupled models examine how multiple interacting processes may produce the observed behavior.
8. **Measurement science ↔ inference:** rainfall, soil moisture, groundwater level, and stream discharge are separate measurements; no one of them alone represents the complete watershed state.

**SYNTHESIS:** Rainfall is an input, not a complete prediction of the resulting stream response. The watershed's antecedent water storage, vegetation activity, soil and bedrock properties, and connected flow paths help condition the outcome.
