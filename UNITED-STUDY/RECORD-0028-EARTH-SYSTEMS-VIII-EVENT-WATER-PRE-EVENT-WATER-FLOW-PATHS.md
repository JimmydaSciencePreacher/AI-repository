## 3. What the isotopes register

Water molecules contain hydrogen and oxygen. Natural water includes small proportions of the stable isotopes deuterium (²H) and oxygen-18 (¹⁸O), in addition to the more abundant ¹H and ¹⁶O.

The notation δ²H and δ¹⁸O expresses the isotope ratio of a sample relative to an international reference standard, conventionally reported in parts per thousand (‰).

These isotope ratios are useful environmental tracers because precipitation isotope composition varies with meteorological and seasonal conditions. Water entering soil can carry that signal. As water moves, mixes, and is stored, the signal can be altered or damped.

**Important distinction:** Stable isotopes do not function as a radioactive clock. A δ value is an isotope-composition measurement, not a direct age measurement. Age, travel time, and residence-time conclusions require hydrological interpretation, a tracer history, and often a model.

## 4. The Shale Hills monitoring network

Thomas et al. (2013) report a coordinated sampling design:

- Precipitation was collected automatically on an event basis.
- Soil water was collected approximately every two weeks using suction-cup lysimeters at multiple depths along four catchment transects.
- Groundwater and stream water were collected daily in the valley using automatic samplers.
- The monitoring period covered more than three years, from 2008 to 2012.

The different sampling intervals matter. Event-based precipitation samples capture changing storm and seasonal inputs. Biweekly soil samples resolve slower soil-profile changes but may not capture every short-lived storm pulse. Daily groundwater and stream samples reveal temporal variation in those pools at a different resolution.

The HydroShare dataset description identifies daily stream-water isotope records for 2008–2011 and reports that samples were analyzed using a liquid-water isotope analyzer following IAEA procedures.

**DIRECT OBSERVATION:** The research used multiple sampling locations, depths, and time intervals to compare isotope compositions among precipitation, soil water, groundwater, and stream water.

**Limit:** The network does not continuously measure every water molecule or every preferential pathway. The spatial and temporal resolution of each pool must be considered when interpreting a storm response.

Sources: Thomas et al. (2013); HydroShare dataset metadata.

## 5. Seasonal isotope signatures are progressively damped with depth

Thomas et al. (2013) found strong seasonal variation in precipitation isotope composition. Those seasonal patterns appeared in soil water, but the size of the variation decreased with depth.

The reported pattern was:
- **Shallow soil water, 30 cm or less:** largest variability and strongest reflection of precipitation inputs.
- **Intermediate soil water, approximately 40–100 cm:** moderate variability.
- **Deep soil water, 120 cm or more:** least variability, with average isotope composition near the groundwater average.
- **Groundwater:** relatively stable isotope composition through the year, close to the annual amount-weighted precipitation average.

The authors interpreted this progressive damping as evidence of hydrodynamic mixing and storage as water moves through the subsurface.

This is a major finding. The soil profile does not preserve every precipitation isotope fluctuation with equal strength at every depth. The signal becomes less variable as water moves into deeper and more mixed stores.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** The deep subsurface and groundwater integrate water contributions over time more strongly than shallow soil water does.

**Limit:** Damping is evidence of mixing and storage behavior; it is not, by itself, a unique measurement of the age of every water molecule.

Source: Thomas et al. (2013).

## 6. Preferential flow: water does not move uniformly through the soil

Thomas et al. (2013) report isotope-profile evidence for preferential flow occurring both laterally and vertically, at different depths and in different seasons.

Jin et al. (2011) provide a complementary hydrological and geochemical interpretation:
- Water moves vertically through the unsaturated soil.
- During periods of saturation, lateral flow can occur along interfaces where soil-horizon permeability changes.
- Macropores provide high-flow pathways for relatively fresh water.
- Soil-matrix zones can retain water longer and permit more mineral-water interaction.
- Shallow groundwater in fractured shale forms another water pool.

The authors distinguish low-flow soil water from high-flow soil water and shallow groundwater. The high-flow zones can receive relatively fresh water through macropores, while longer contact in low-flow zones permits greater clay-mineral dissolution and higher magnesium concentrations.

This shows that preferential flow is not simply “water moving faster.” It is a difference in pathway and contact:
- Some water moves through relatively conductive pores and interfaces.
- Some remains longer in soil-matrix storage.
- Some moves laterally along permeability contrasts.
- Some enters shallow groundwater and fractured bedrock pathways.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Shale Hills contains hydraulically differentiated pathways and stores. The isotope and chemistry evidence is inconsistent with treating the entire soil profile as one uniformly mixed reservoir.

Sources: Thomas et al. (2013); Jin et al. (2011); CZO isotope hydrology overview.

## 7. Stream water is a mixture of sources whose contributions change

Jin et al. (2011) found that stream water chemistry and isotope composition reflect contributions from at least two important subsurface sources:
- relatively old groundwater, with higher magnesium concentration and a more limited isotope range;
- relatively young soil water, with lower magnesium concentration and a wider range of isotope values.

The relative contributions of these sources change seasonally.

This complements the isotope monitoring results: groundwater is relatively stable in isotope composition, while shallow soil water carries a stronger and more variable seasonal signal. The stream's composition therefore depends on the relative contribution of these pools.

**Important distinction:** “Old” and “young” in this study are hydrological interpretations based on isotope-signal behavior and associated chemistry. They are not exact calendar ages assigned to individual stream-water molecules.

The term **pre-event water** generally refers to water already present in the catchment before a particular rainfall event. **Event water** refers to water supplied by that event. These labels are event-relative, not fixed ages: water that is pre-event for one storm may itself have entered the catchment during a previous storm.

An isotope-based hydrograph separation may estimate the relative contributions of event and pre-event water when the end-member isotope signatures are sufficiently distinct and the assumptions of the mixing model are satisfied. But seasonal isotope differences, spatially variable rainfall, soil-water mixing, and changing source signatures can complicate that separation.

No event-water percentage is asserted in this record because the specific storm end members, time series, and mixing calculation have not been retrieved and checked here.

Sources: Jin et al. (2011); Thomas et al. (2013).

## 8. Residence time, travel time, and signal damping are not synonyms

These terms must remain distinct:

- **Travel time:** the time taken by water to move between specified locations or boundaries.
- **Residence time:** the time water spends within a defined storage or system, with the exact definition depending on the reservoir and method.
- **Water age:** the time since a water parcel entered the hydrological system as precipitation, under the chosen age definition.
- **Isotope-signal damping:** reduction in the amplitude of isotope variation as water mixes and moves through stores.

Jin et al. (2011) inferred an average shallow-subsurface water residence time of less than two years from the isotope and hydrological evidence. This is a study-specific inference, not a direct stopwatch measurement and not a universal value for every soil or groundwater pathway.

The isotope data also show that the groundwater composition is relatively stable and close to the annual amount-weighted precipitation average. That observation is consistent with mixing of recharge from different seasons, but it does not establish a single residence time for all groundwater.

**Limit:** A damped seasonal isotope signal can reflect mixing, storage, and transport dispersion. It should not be converted into one exact water age without a defined model and supporting data.

Sources: Jin et al. (2011); Thomas et al. (2013); Duffy et al. (2013) conference abstract on catchment isotope age theory.

## 9. Cross-disciplinary relationships established

1. **Atmospheric science ↔ isotope hydrology:** precipitation carries seasonal and event-scale isotope signatures.
2. **Soil physics ↔ isotope hydrology:** soil depth, pore structure, and hydraulic contrasts affect how precipitation signatures move and mix.
3. **Hydrology ↔ geology:** shallow groundwater and fractured shale provide subsurface storage and flow pathways.
4. **Hydrology ↔ geochemistry:** water residence and mineral contact influence solute concentrations, including magnesium.
5. **Topography ↔ flow connectivity:** hillslope interfaces and valley groundwater conditions affect lateral flow and stream contributions.
6. **Vegetation ↔ hydrology:** growing-season water uptake changes soil-water storage and recharge conditions, affecting how isotope signals move through the profile.
7. **Measurement science ↔ inference:** event precipitation, biweekly soil water, daily groundwater, and daily stream samples have different temporal resolutions and cannot be treated as equivalent observations.
8. **Modeling ↔ field evidence:** age and mixing interpretations require models or assumptions that must be checked against the measured isotope histories.

**SYNTHESIS:** Water entering a watershed does not necessarily pass through a single uniform reservoir or emerge at the outlet as an unchanged parcel. It may move rapidly through preferential pathways, mix with stored soil water or groundwater, and emerge with an isotope composition that reflects multiple sources and histories.
