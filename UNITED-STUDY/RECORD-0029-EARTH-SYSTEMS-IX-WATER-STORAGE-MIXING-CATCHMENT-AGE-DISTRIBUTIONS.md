## 1. First discriminating question

**How can water entering a catchment at different times produce an outflow containing a distribution of water ages, and why can the age distribution of stored water differ from that of water leaving the catchment?**

The investigation follows the isotope and flow-path evidence from Records 0027–0028. It moves from individual stores and pathways to distributions that describe ensembles of water.

The record distinguishes:
- water age from transit time;
- storage age distribution from outflow transit-time distribution;
- mean age in storage from mean transit time of outflow;
- mobile and immobile domains as hydrological concepts, not absolute categories;
- mixing from preferential selection of water for discharge;
- tracer breakthrough from environmental-tracer inference;
- a conceptual model from a direct measurement;

## 3. What does water age mean?

In catchment hydrology, water age is commonly defined as elapsed time since water entered the system at the chosen input boundary, usually as precipitation or snowmelt.

If a water parcel enters at time (t_{in}) and is considered at time (t), its age is:

[
T = t - t_{in}
]

When that parcel leaves the defined catchment through streamflow at time (t_{out}), its transit time is:

[
	au = t_{out} - t_{in}
]

These definitions depend on the boundaries chosen. For example, the age of water since it fell as precipitation is not necessarily the same as its age since it entered the saturated groundwater zone.

**Important distinction:** Water age is a property used to describe the history of a water parcel. It is not a force that causes water to move. Hydrological transport is governed by hydraulic gradients, water availability, material properties, and other physical conditions. Age is carried along with the moving water.

Sources: Benettin et al. (2022); Sprenger et al. (2019).

## 4. A catchment contains an age distribution, not one universal age

At any particular time, the water stored in a catchment may include:
- water that entered recently;
- water that entered during earlier storms;
- water retained in soil pores;
- water moving through preferential pathways;
- water in shallow groundwater;
- water stored in deeper or less mobile domains.

A sample collected at the stream outlet may likewise contain water with a range of ages.

A distribution describes the range and relative representation of those ages. Its shape can contain information that a single average conceals.

For example, two hypothetical catchments could have the same mean outflow age while having different distributions:
- one could release mostly intermediate-age water;
- another could release a mixture of very young and much older water.

The same mean does not imply the same pathways, storage behavior, or response to a new storm.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Transit-time distributions provide more information about catchment behavior than a mean value alone, particularly where flow pathways and storage are heterogeneous.

Sources: McDonnell et al. (2010); Benettin et al. (2022).

## 5. Storage age distribution versus outflow transit-time distribution

This is the central distinction of the record.

### Storage age distribution

Describes the ages of water currently inside a defined system or storage volume.

### Outflow age distribution

Describes the ages of water leaving the system through a specified outlet, such as stream discharge.

These distributions are not generally identical.

Water may be present in storage without being equally likely to leave at a given moment. Some water may be connected to a flowing pathway, while other water remains in less mobile pores or storage zones.

Only under particular conditions—such as a well-mixed storage that is sampled uniformly by its outflow—do the storage and outflow age distributions coincide.

The exact conditions matter. A catchment with changing rainfall, evapotranspiration, groundwater levels, and flow-path connectivity is not automatically a steady, uniformly mixed reservoir.

Sources: Benettin et al. (2022); McDonnell et al. (2010).

## 6. Mobile and immobile water: a useful distinction with limits

Soil and geological materials contain pores and pathways with different hydraulic behavior.

A simplified conceptual distinction is:

**Mobile domain**
- Water in pathways that contribute relatively readily to flow.
- May include connected macropores and more conductive pore networks.
- Can respond relatively quickly to changing hydraulic conditions.

**Immobile or less-mobile domain**
- Water retained in pores or regions that exchange more slowly with mobile water.
- May contribute to storage and mixing over longer intervals.
- Can exchange water with mobile domains through diffusion or other transfer processes.

These terms are relative to the timescale and model being used. “Immobile” does not necessarily mean permanently motionless. A domain treated as immobile in one model may exchange water with another domain over a longer period.

The Shale Hills isotope-hydrology overview describes mobile and immobile deep-soil water and preliminary interpretations involving macropore-matrix flow partitioning. It also describes seasonal changes in water uptake and recharge.

This is consistent with the broader critical-zone literature, which recognizes that water storage and movement occur across pore sizes and domains with different exchange rates.

**Limit:** The Shale Hills overview's conceptual description does not, by itself, quantify every mobile and immobile storage volume or establish a complete catchment-wide distribution.

Sources: Shale Hills isotope hydrology overview; Benettin et al. (2022); Sprenger et al. (2019).

## 7. Mixing does not mean uniform mixing

When water from different histories comes into contact, its tracer composition may become mixed. But the degree and timing of mixing depend on the pathways and exchange processes.

A simple two-domain conceptual model distinguishes:
1. Water moving through a mobile domain.
2. Water retained in a less-mobile domain.
3. Exchange between the domains.
4. Water leaving through an outlet.

This can produce several behaviors:
- a rapid response from mobile pathways;
- delayed release from storage;
- damping of seasonal or event-scale tracer signals;
- a long tail in a tracer response;
- changes in which ages contribute to outflow as hydrological conditions change.

The important point is that a mixture can be non-uniform and time-dependent.

A model that assumes instantaneous complete mixing may fail to represent preferential pathways or delayed exchange where those processes are important.

Sources: Benettin et al. (2022); McGuire and McDonnell (2006).

## 8. StorAge-Selection: how a catchment selects water from storage

The StorAge-Selection (SAS) framework asks a particularly useful question:

**Which ages of stored water are selected to contribute to an outflow at a particular time?**

Rather than assuming that the outlet receives a representative sample of all stored water, SAS describes how water of different ages is preferentially supplied to streamflow or evapotranspiration.

For example:
- A catchment may preferentially discharge younger water under one set of conditions.
- Another condition may connect deeper or older stored water to the channel.
- Evapotranspiration may select water from storage differently from streamflow.

The selection can change with time and hydrological state.

This is a mathematical description of how storage contributes to outflow. It is not a claim that water consciously selects a route. “Selection” is the technical name of a distributional relationship between stored water ages and an output flux.

**Why it matters:** It provides a framework for connecting storage, flow pathways, and the age composition of water leaving a catchment without assuming that all water in storage is equally likely to exit.

Sources: Harman (2015); Rinaldo et al. (2015); Benettin et al. (2022).

## 9. Mean transit time is not mean storage age

A mean is a summary of a distribution. But the distribution being averaged must be specified.

- **Mean storage age:** average age of water currently in the defined storage.
- **Mean outflow transit time:** average transit time of water leaving through a defined outlet over the relevant period.
- **Mean age of a sample:** average age represented in that sample, according to the chosen sampling and age-distribution framework.

These means need not be equal.

McDonnell et al. (2010) emphasize that the age distribution in stream water and the age distribution of water remaining in the catchment are different objects. Their means coincide only under special conditions, such as a completely mixed reservoir under steady-state flow.

A human-population analogy can help explain the mathematics: the average age of people currently living in a population is not necessarily the same as the average age at death. The analogy illustrates two different sampled populations; it does not imply that hydrological systems and human populations operate by the same mechanism.

Its meaning depends on the model, age definition, observation period, and assumptions used.

Source: Shale Hills isotope hydrology overview.

## 11. Tracer breakthrough and environmental isotope histories

A controlled tracer experiment may introduce a known tracer at a defined input and monitor its arrival at an outlet. The resulting breakthrough curve records tracer concentration or mass flux over time.

The curve can reveal:
- early arrival through fast pathways;
- spreading due to different velocities and pathways;
- delayed release from storage;
- long tails associated with slow transport or exchange.

Environmental tracers such as naturally varying stable isotopes are different. Their input histories may be seasonal, event-based, or otherwise variable, and the output reflects the combined history of inputs, storage, transport, and mixing.

A breakthrough curve is not a photograph of every flow path. Its interpretation depends on the tracer input, measurement location, sampling frequency, and transport model.

**Limit:** A tracer response may constrain a range of possible transport behaviors without uniquely identifying the geometry or every microscopic pathway.

Sources: McGuire and McDonnell (2006); Benettin et al. (2022).

## 12. Cross-disciplinary relationships established

1. **Soil physics ↔ hydrology:** pore structure and hydraulic conductivity influence mobile and less-mobile domains.
2. **Hydrology ↔ mathematics:** probability distributions describe the range of water ages and transit times.
3. **Isotope chemistry ↔ hydrology:** tracer histories help constrain storage, mixing, and transport.
4. **Geology ↔ hydrology:** soil-bedrock interfaces, fractures, and geological structure influence storage and pathways.
5. **Vegetation ↔ hydrology:** transpiration removes water from storage and may select water from particular soil domains.
6. **Hydrology ↔ systems modeling:** age-balance and SAS approaches connect changing storage to changing outflow composition.
7. **Measurement science ↔ inference:** tracer histories constrain models but do not uniquely reveal every pathway.
8. **Environmental science ↔ time-series analysis:** a mean value can conceal variation, multiple populations, and long tails.

**SYNTHESIS:** A catchment's water output reflects not simply the amount of water stored, but the ages and pathways of the water that are connected to each outflow at a given time. Storage and discharge are related, but their age distributions need not be identical.
