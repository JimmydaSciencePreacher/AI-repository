## 1. First discriminating question

**When water moves through soil, rock, and groundwater, how do dissolved substances move with it, spread, exchange with surrounding materials, react, or emerge at a watershed outlet?**

Record 0029 examined the age distributions of water. This record asks whether dissolved substances necessarily share the same movement history as the water carrying them.

They do not always.

A dissolved substance can be:
- transported with flowing water;
- spread by dispersion and molecular diffusion;
- temporarily or persistently retained by sorption;
- released from minerals through dissolution;
- removed from solution by precipitation;
- transformed by biological or abiotic reactions;
- exchanged between mobile water and less-mobile pore domains.

The investigation distinguishes:
- water flux from solute mass flux;
- concentration from total transported mass;
- conservative from reactive solutes;
- advection from dispersion and diffusion;
- sorption from chemical destruction;
- dissolution from desorption;
- precipitation of a mineral from precipitation as rain;
- water age from solute residence or reaction time;
- measured chemical patterns from inferred reaction pathways.

## 3. The first distinction: water movement and solute movement

Water is the moving fluid. A dissolved solute is transported within that fluid, but its behavior may also be influenced by its own chemical properties and interactions with soil, rock, organisms, and other phases.

A simple example illustrates the distinction:
- A relatively conservative dissolved constituent can travel with water and spread through dispersion.
- A constituent that sorbs to mineral or organic surfaces can be delayed relative to the water.
- A constituent produced by mineral dissolution can enter the water during transport.
- A reactive constituent can be consumed, transformed, or precipitated before reaching the outlet.

Therefore, observing water movement does not by itself establish the complete history of every dissolved substance carried by that water.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Solute transport is coupled to water flow, but the solute's movement and persistence can also depend on transport spreading, exchange, and reactions.

Sources: Reilly et al. (1987); Goode (1999); National Academies.

## 4. Advection: transport with bulk water movement

**Advection** is the transport of dissolved material by the bulk movement of the fluid.

In a simplified case, a dissolved conservative tracer moves with the flowing groundwater. Its average advective movement is associated with the groundwater pore velocity.

This idealized description assumes the solute is not significantly retarded or transformed by reactions with the surrounding medium.

Advection answers: **Where does the moving water carry the dissolved substance?**

It does not, by itself, describe:
- spreading caused by different pore velocities;
- molecular diffusion;
- sorption and release;
- mineral reactions;
- biological transformation.

Source: Reilly et al. (1987); National Academies.

## 5. Dispersion and diffusion: spreading is not the same as bulk transport

Two processes can spread a dissolved substance beyond the path described by average bulk flow.

### Hydrodynamic dispersion

Water moves at different velocities through different pores and pathways. Differences in pore geometry, path length, and local velocity cause a dissolved plume or tracer pulse to spread.

Dispersion includes longitudinal spreading along the general direction of flow and transverse spreading across it.

### Molecular diffusion

Dissolved species move in response to concentration or chemical-potential gradients, including where bulk water movement is very small or absent.

Diffusion can therefore exchange solute between flowing pore water and adjacent, slower or less-mobile water domains.

These processes have distinct meanings:
- Advection carries solute with bulk water flow.
- Dispersion spreads solute because flow velocities and pathways vary.
- Diffusion moves solute down a molecular concentration or chemical-potential gradient.

A broadened tracer signal does not necessarily mean that the water itself followed one broadened physical channel. It can reflect a distribution of pathways and exchange processes.

Sources: Reilly et al. (1987); Goode (1999); National Academies.

## 6. Conservative and reactive solutes

A **conservative solute** is treated as one that does not undergo significant chemical or biological reaction under the conditions and timescale of the investigation. Chloride is often used as an approximate conservative tracer in groundwater studies, although its suitability must be checked for the particular setting.

A **reactive solute** can interact with the surrounding medium or be transformed.

Examples of processes affecting reactive solutes include:
- sorption and desorption;
- ion exchange;
- mineral dissolution and precipitation;
- oxidation and reduction;
- hydrolysis;
- biological uptake and transformation;
- biodegradation.

The terms are operational and context-dependent. A constituent can be sufficiently conservative for one study's timescale and conditions but reactive in another environment.

**Limit:** “Conservative” does not mean that a substance is physically incapable of interaction under every possible condition. It means those reactions are negligible for the specific analysis or are sufficiently accounted for.

Sources: Groundwater Project; National Academies; Goode (1999).

## 7. Sorption and desorption: retention can delay without destroying mass

**Sorption** describes transfer of a dissolved substance from water onto or into a solid phase, including mineral surfaces or organic matter. The broader term includes different mechanisms, such as adsorption at surfaces and absorption into a material.

**Desorption** is release back into the aqueous phase.

When sorption is reversible, the solute can be temporarily retained and later released. Its apparent movement can therefore be slower than the water's movement.

This is commonly described as **retardation**.

Retardation is not necessarily destruction or permanent removal. A reduction in dissolved concentration at one location may reflect transfer to a solid phase rather than disappearance of total mass from the system.

The extent of sorption can depend on:
- the chemical species;
- pH and redox conditions;
- mineral and organic surface properties;
- competing ions;
- concentration;
- contact time;
- whether the process is reversible or kinetically limited.

Sources: Goode (1999); Groundwater Project.

## 8. Dissolution and precipitation: minerals can add or remove dissolved constituents

Water interacts chemically with minerals.

### Mineral dissolution

A mineral dissolves and releases constituents into solution. The resulting dissolved concentration depends on factors including mineral composition, reactive surface area, water chemistry, reaction kinetics, and contact time.

### Mineral precipitation

Dissolved constituents form or enter a solid mineral phase. This removes those constituents from the aqueous solution, subject to the relevant chemical conditions and kinetics.

Dissolution and precipitation are not the same as rainfall and snowfall. Here, “precipitation” means formation of a solid phase from solution.

A water sample's chemical composition therefore reflects both the water's source and its interactions along the pathway.

**Important:** A high dissolved concentration does not independently identify one unique mineral reaction. Source contributions, concentration by evapotranspiration, ion exchange, mixing, and other processes may also matter.

Sources: Jin et al. (2011); National Academies.

## 9. Shale Hills: a field example of hydrology controlling weathering and chemistry

Jin et al. (2011) investigated magnesium concentration, hydrogen and oxygen isotopes, and water chemistry in precipitation, soil water, groundwater, and stream water at the Susquehanna Shale Hills Critical Zone Observatory.

The study identified three hydrologically and chemically distinct water types:
1. Low-flow soil water in the A and B soil horizons.
2. High-flow water in preferential pathways, including macropores and horizon interfaces.
3. Shallow groundwater.

The researchers reported that clay-mineral dissolution occurred predominantly in low-flow soil zones, where longer water-mineral contact was associated with higher magnesium concentrations. Magnesium released from these zones could diffuse or move into higher-flow pathways.

The high-flow zones carried relatively fresh water and had lower magnesium concentrations. During saturation, lateral flow could occur along permeability contrasts between soil horizons.

The study also attributed elevated groundwater magnesium to dissolution of magnesium-bearing carbonate minerals in the shallow subsurface, including ankerite, while noting variability among groundwater samples.

**DIRECT OBSERVATION:** The research measured differences in Mg concentrations and isotope compositions among water types, locations, and times.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** The combined isotope, chemistry, and hydrological evidence supports a model in which water pathways and contact time influence mineral weathering and the chemical composition of water reaching the stream.

**Limit:** The proposed pathway and reaction model is an interpretation of multiple observations. It is not direct observation of every individual dissolution event or every solute molecule.

Source: Jin et al. (2011).

## 10. A particularly important Shale Hills relationship: low-flow reaction zones and high-flow export pathways

The Shale Hills study proposes a coupled relationship:

- Low-flow soil domains allow longer contact between water and clay minerals.
- Mineral dissolution releases magnesium into soil water.
- Magnesium can move from low-flow domains toward high-flow pathways by diffusion and/or water movement.
- High-flow pathways can transport water and dissolved constituents relatively rapidly toward the stream.
- Groundwater supplies another chemically distinct contribution.

This is not a claim that all weathering occurs only in low-flow zones or that every solute follows magnesium's behavior. It is the study's site-specific conceptual model for the measured magnesium and isotope patterns.

The result is a useful example of coupled processes: hydraulic structure affects chemical reaction conditions, while weathering and soil development influence the material properties that shape later water movement.

Jin et al. describe this as a long-term coupling between soil and rock weathering and the hydrological features of the watershed.

## 11. Concentration is not mass flux

A concentration is the amount of a substance per volume of water. It does not, by itself, tell us how much total substance is transported past a location.

For a dissolved constituent, an instantaneous mass-flux calculation commonly requires both water discharge and concentration:

[
	ext{Mass flux} = Q 	imes C
]

where:
- (Q) is water discharge (volume per time);
- (C) is dissolved concentration (mass per volume).

The units must be compatible.

A stream can have a lower concentration during high flow and still transport a greater total mass per unit time because the water discharge is much larger.

Conversely, a high concentration during low flow does not automatically mean the greatest mass export.

To estimate total export over a period, mass flux must be integrated over time using sufficiently resolved discharge and concentration data.

**Limit:** This simple expression applies to the dissolved constituent represented by the concentration measurement. Total material export may also require suspended sediment, particulate-bound material, additional phases, and appropriate sampling.

This distinction is essential when comparing stream chemistry with watershed-scale weathering or nutrient export.

Sources: Jin et al. (2011); USGS solute-transport framework.

## 12. Water age is not solute reaction time

Record 0029 distinguished water age and transit time. This record adds a related but different question.

A water parcel may have an age defined by the time since it entered the catchment. A dissolved constituent carried by that water may:
- have entered the water only recently through mineral dissolution;
- have been sorbed and later desorbed;
- have been transformed by biological activity;
- have moved between mobile and less-mobile domains;
- have been present in the water before the parcel entered the current system boundary.

Therefore, water age does not automatically equal the age of a dissolved constituent or the duration of its chemical reaction.

The reaction history depends on the constituent's source, phase transfers, chemical kinetics, and pathway.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Water transit time and solute reaction or residence time are related but distinct quantities.

Sources: Goode (1999); National Academies; Jin et al. (2011).

## 13. Cross-disciplinary relationships established

1. **Hydrology ↔ geochemistry:** water movement determines where and for how long water contacts reactive materials.
2. **Soil physics ↔ chemistry:** pore structure and flow domains influence contact time, diffusion, and solute exchange.
3. **Mineralogy ↔ aqueous chemistry:** mineral composition and dissolution kinetics affect dissolved-ion concentrations.
4. **Surface chemistry ↔ transport:** sorption and desorption can delay and later release solutes.
5. **Microbiology ↔ chemistry:** organisms can take up or transform dissolved substances, changing their chemical form and persistence.
6. **Physics ↔ transport modeling:** advection, dispersion, and diffusion describe distinct transport mechanisms.
7. **Hydrology ↔ watershed accounting:** discharge and concentration together determine dissolved mass flux.
8. **Measurement science ↔ inference:** concentration measurements, discharge records, isotope data, and models answer different questions and must be combined carefully.
9. **Geology ↔ long-term hydrology:** weathering can change soil and rock properties, which in turn influence later water pathways.

**SYNTHESIS:** Water transports dissolved substances, but transport is only part of their history. Their measured concentrations and eventual export reflect flow, spreading, exchange, retention, chemical reactions, biological transformations, and mixing.
