## 3. Carbon pools and carbon fluxes are different quantities

A **carbon pool** is an amount of carbon held in a defined reservoir at a particular time. Examples include:
- atmospheric carbon dioxide;
- living plant biomass;
- roots;
- litter and dead wood;
- soil organic matter;
- dissolved carbon in water;
- ocean carbon;
- carbonate rocks and sediments.

A **carbon flux** is the transfer of carbon between pools per unit time. Examples include:
- photosynthetic uptake from the atmosphere;
- plant and microbial respiration;
- litterfall;
- decomposition;
- dissolved carbon export in streamflow;
- erosion and particulate transport;
- burial and geological storage.

A pool is a stock; a flux is a rate of transfer. A large pool does not necessarily imply a large current flux, and a large flux does not necessarily imply long-term accumulation.

A simplified accounting relationship is:

[
Delta C_{	ext{pool}} = 	ext{inputs} - 	ext{outputs}
]

For a complete system budget, all relevant inputs, outputs, and transfers across the chosen boundary must be included. Internal transfers change the distribution among pools but do not create or destroy carbon within the defined closed accounting boundary.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Carbon-cycle research must distinguish the amount stored from the rate at which carbon moves.

Sources: NOAA Carbon Cycle; NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory; NASA Earth Observatory.

## 4. Photosynthesis: atmospheric carbon enters biological material

In oxygenic photosynthesis, plants, algae, and cyanobacteria use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into organic compounds, releasing oxygen.

A simplified net equation is:

[
6CO_2 + 6H_2O + 	ext{light energy}
ightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2
]

This equation is a simplified summary, not a complete representation of the many biochemical reactions involved.

Photosynthesis transfers carbon from an inorganic atmospheric or aqueous pool into organic biological material.

That carbon can subsequently:
- remain in leaves, stems, and roots;
- be transferred through food webs;
- enter litter and soil;
- be respired;
- be transported in dissolved or particulate form;
- become stabilized or buried over longer periods.

Photosynthetic uptake is not identical to long-term carbon sequestration. Carbon incorporated into a leaf may return to the atmosphere through respiration or decomposition. Long-term storage depends on the balance of inputs, outputs, and stabilization over the relevant timescale.

Sources: NOAA Carbon Cycle; NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory.

## 5. Gross primary production, plant respiration, and net primary production

Three quantities must not be collapsed.

### Gross primary production (GPP)

The total rate at which primary producers fix carbon through photosynthesis over a defined area and time.

### Autotrophic respiration (Ra)

Carbon released through respiration by plants and other primary producers. It includes metabolic costs associated with maintaining and building living tissues.

### Net primary production (NPP)

The carbon remaining after autotrophic respiration is subtracted from gross primary production:

[
NPP = GPP - R_a
]

NPP represents the rate of production of new plant biomass and other plant-derived organic matter after plant respiratory costs. It is not automatically equal to the amount of carbon stored in soil or the whole ecosystem.

Plant material can be consumed, shed, decomposed, transported, or stored.

**Important:** NPP is not the same as net ecosystem production. The latter also accounts for heterotrophic respiration and other ecosystem-level terms, depending on the stated accounting convention.

Sources: NOAA carbon-cycle resources; Shale Hills carbon-cycling research overview.

## 6. Respiration and decomposition return carbon to other pools

Living organisms use organic compounds in cellular metabolism. Respiration releases carbon dioxide, while decomposition breaks down dead organic material through the activity of microorganisms and other decomposers.

In a forest soil, the measured soil CO2 efflux commonly combines:
- autotrophic respiration from living roots and associated organisms;
- heterotrophic respiration from microorganisms decomposing organic matter;
- potentially other contributions, depending on the measurement method and site.

Therefore, **soil respiration is not synonymous with decomposition alone**.

The rate of soil CO2 release can vary with:
- soil temperature;
- water content and oxygen availability;
- root activity;
- substrate availability;
- soil texture and pore structure;
- microbial community and activity;
- topographic position;
- geological and mineral conditions.

At Shale Hills, researchers have measured soil CO2 concentrations and fluxes along topographic transects and compared locations with different geological and hydrological settings. Penn State's research overview reports that geology, topography, hydrologic export of dissolved inorganic carbon, and iron redox reactions are important controls on soil CO2 patterns.

Sources: Penn State Carbon Cycling in the Critical Zone; Shale Hills Biogeochemistry.

## 7. Dissolved and particulate organic carbon are distinct transport forms

Carbon can leave soil and move through a watershed in more than one physical form.

### Dissolved organic carbon (DOC)

Organic carbon present in the operationally defined dissolved fraction of a water sample, commonly measured after filtration using a specified filter cutoff.

DOC can be transported through soil pore water, groundwater, and streams. Its concentration and export can vary with rainfall, flow paths, soil conditions, and the availability of soluble organic compounds.

### Particulate organic carbon (POC)

Organic carbon associated with suspended or transported particles, such as eroded soil, organic debris, and sediment.

POC transport is influenced by erosion, sediment mobilization, channel processes, and deposition.

These fractions are operationally defined by sampling and filtration methods. They should not be treated as interchangeable.

At Shale Hills, hydropedology research has mapped soil organic carbon and investigated DOC in soil pore waters, stream export, flushing, and the role of redox conditions and metals in stabilizing DOC in soils.

**Limit:** A measured DOC concentration at one sampling point does not establish total watershed DOC export. Export requires concentration and water discharge over time, with appropriate spatial and temporal sampling.

Sources: Shale Hills Hydropedology; Penn State Carbon Cycling in the Critical Zone.

## 8. Carbon can be retained or stabilized in soil

Organic carbon entering soil does not all decompose at the same rate or follow the same pathway.

Some organic matter is readily available to microorganisms. Other material may persist longer because of:
- physical protection within aggregates or small pores;
- association with mineral surfaces;
- chemical conditions that limit decomposition;
- limited oxygen or other environmental constraints;
- slower accessibility to decomposers.

Mineral association and redox processes can affect whether organic carbon remains in soil or is mobilized.

The Shale Hills research overview identifies redox and metals as areas of investigation concerning DOC stabilization and soil carbon storage.

**Limit:** “Stabilized” does not mean permanently immutable. Carbon persistence is timescale-dependent, and a change in environmental conditions can alter its mobility or decomposition.

Sources: Shale Hills Hydropedology; Penn State Carbon Cycling in the Critical Zone.

## 9. The nitrogen cycle: transformations among distinct chemical forms

Nitrogen is required for proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll, and other biological compounds. Atmospheric nitrogen gas (N2) is abundant, but most organisms cannot use it directly.

Nitrogen cycling involves several distinct transformations.

### Nitrogen fixation

Certain microorganisms convert atmospheric N2 into biologically usable reduced nitrogen, commonly ammonia/ammonium. Biological fixation introduces reactive nitrogen into ecosystems.

Other inputs include atmospheric deposition and, in some settings, fertilizer or other human additions.

### Mineralization (ammonification)

Microorganisms decompose organic nitrogen in dead material and waste, releasing inorganic nitrogen, commonly ammonium (NH4+).

### Nitrification

Under suitable oxygenated conditions, microorganisms oxidize ammonium through nitrite (NO2-) to nitrate (NO3-).

### Assimilation

Plants and microorganisms take up inorganic nitrogen and incorporate it into organic compounds.

### Denitrification

Under oxygen-limited conditions, certain microorganisms can reduce nitrate through intermediate nitrogen oxides, ultimately producing gaseous nitrogen products such as N2O and N2.

Other pathways, including dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium and anammox, can also be important in particular environments. This record does not assume they have been quantified at Shale Hills.

These processes are not synonyms. They change nitrogen's chemical form, availability, mobility, or pathway out of the ecosystem.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Nitrogen cycling is a network of biological and chemical transformations, not simply the movement of one unchanged substance.

Source context: Weitzman and Kaye (2018); DOE carbon-nitrogen cycle background; Shale Hills watershed synthesis.

## 10. Nitrogen retention and nitrogen export

Nitrogen entering a watershed may be:
- taken up by plants;
- incorporated into microbial biomass;
- retained in soil organic matter;
- transformed among inorganic and organic forms;
- released as gases;
- transported in stream water;
- leached below the rooting zone.

A nitrogen budget compares inputs and outputs across a defined boundary and time period. The difference is a net accumulation or net loss only if the budget includes the relevant pathways and uncertainties.

At Shale Hills, the 2018 watershed synthesis reports that the catchment remained a net nitrogen sink despite a history of elevated atmospheric deposition. Its compiled budget estimated that more than half of total inputs were retained. The synthesis also states that estimated gaseous losses were about twenty times stream exports.

A necessary source comparison qualifies that ratio. The primary Weitzman and Kaye (2018) paper reports that measured N2O fluxes were about 19% of total ecosystem nitrogen losses and nearly twice stream nitrogen export. It separately estimates other gaseous losses, including N2, using literature-derived ratios. The broader 2018 watershed synthesis reports a different aggregate gaseous-to-stream comparison. These figures are not interchangeable: they refer to different budget constructions and combinations of measured and estimated terms.

Weitzman and Kaye (2018) investigated soil N2O fluxes along topographic gradients and measured associated variables including soil CO2, soil O2, nitrate in pore water, and nitrogen cycling indicators. Their work examined how topography and hydrology influence conditions associated with N2O production and release.

**Limit:** The watershed budget combines measurements, estimates, and literature-based relationships. The primary paper and later synthesis report different comparisons for gaseous losses versus stream export. Preserve each figure with its own source and accounting definition; do not compress them into one settled ratio or generalize them to other watersheds.

Sources: Shale Hills watershed synthesis (2018); Weitzman and Kaye (2018).

## 11. Carbon and nitrogen cycles are coupled, but not identical

Carbon and nitrogen often move through the same organisms and environments.

Examples:
- Plants require carbon to build organic compounds and nitrogen to construct proteins and nucleic acids.
- Microorganisms decompose organic matter containing both carbon and nitrogen.
- The availability of nitrogen can influence plant growth and carbon uptake.
- Carbon-rich organic matter supplies energy and substrates for many microbial transformations of nitrogen.
- Hydrological flow can transport dissolved organic carbon and inorganic or organic nitrogen.
- Oxygen availability and redox conditions influence both carbon decomposition and nitrogen transformations.

However, the cycles have different chemical forms, reservoirs, transformations, and outputs.

A carbon budget cannot be substituted for a nitrogen budget. Their coupling must be examined through the specific processes and measurements that connect them.

Source context: DOE carbon-nitrogen cycle background; Shale Hills watershed synthesis; Weitzman and Kaye (2018).

## 12. Shale Hills: a field example of coupled biological, hydrological, and geochemical processes

The Shale Hills research program provides a useful case because investigators have studied:
- soil CO2 concentrations and surface fluxes;
- topographic and geological variation;
- soil water and hydrological pathways;
- dissolved inorganic carbon export;
- dissolved organic carbon in soil pore water and streamflow;
- redox and iron-related controls;
- soil microbial activity;
- phosphorus availability;
- nitrogen deposition, retention, gaseous losses, and stream export.

The Penn State research overview reports that hydrologic export of dissolved inorganic carbon and iron redox reactions are significant controls on soil CO2 behavior. It also describes connections among weathering, root activity, phosphorus availability, and microbial communities.

The 2018 Shale Hills watershed synthesis reports that its carbon budget contains both directly measured and modeled or extrapolated components. It warns, in effect, that the spatial and temporal coverage differs among the budget terms.

The nitrogen synthesis identifies strong retention within forest vegetation and soil processes, with gaseous pathways contributing substantially to nitrogen output.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** A forested watershed cannot be understood by examining plants, soil, water, minerals, or microorganisms in isolation. Its carbon and nutrient behavior emerges from interactions among these differentiated domains.

**Limit:** This is a synthesis of site research, not a claim that every process has been measured continuously or that the same relative contributions occur in every forest.

Sources: Penn State Carbon Cycling in the Critical Zone; Shale Hills watershed synthesis; Weitzman and Kaye (2018); Shale Hills Hydropedology.

## 13. Pools, fluxes, and system boundaries: the accounting problem

A watershed budget must specify:
- the spatial boundary;
- the time interval;
- the pools included;
- the input pathways;
- the output pathways;
- which internal transfers are counted;
- which quantities are directly measured;
- which values are estimated, extrapolated, or modeled;
- uncertainty and missing data.

For example, a forest carbon budget may include atmospheric exchange, plant growth, litterfall, soil carbon, respiration, dissolved export, and particulate export.

A nitrogen budget may include wet and dry deposition, biological fixation, plant uptake, soil storage, gaseous losses, stream export, and leaching.

The budget's apparent residual is not automatically a newly discovered process. It may reflect measurement uncertainty, omitted pathways, inconsistent time periods, or imperfect spatial scaling.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** A system budget is only as complete and interpretable as its boundary definitions, measurements, assumptions, and uncertainty accounting.

Sources: Shale Hills watershed synthesis (2018); NOAA carbon-cycle resources.
