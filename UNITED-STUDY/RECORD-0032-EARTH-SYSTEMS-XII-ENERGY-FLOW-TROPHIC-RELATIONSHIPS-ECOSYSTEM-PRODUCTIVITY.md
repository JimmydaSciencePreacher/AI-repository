## 3. Energy and matter: related, but not interchangeable

Energy and matter are both necessary for organisms and ecosystems, but they have different accounting behavior.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Matter consists of material constituents that can be transferred, stored, chemically transformed, and reused. Energy is transferred and transformed; within an ecosystem it is not recycled in the same way as carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, or water.

Energy is conserved. It does not vanish when an organism respires or when energy passes between trophic levels. Rather, energy is transformed, and a substantial fraction becomes dispersed as heat. That dispersed energy is less available to support further biological work within the ecosystem.

Most ecosystems receive continuing energy from sunlight. Photosynthetic organisms convert a portion of incoming light into chemical energy stored in organic compounds. Consumers obtain chemical energy by eating organisms or organic material. Respiration and other metabolic processes transform chemical energy to support cellular work, with heat released.

Some ecosystems are not based primarily on local photosynthesis. Chemosynthetic autotrophs use energy from inorganic chemical reactions, including at deep-sea hydrothermal systems where sunlight does not reach.

Therefore:

- most ecosystems are solar-energy supported;
- some ecosystems receive a substantial base of energy through chemosynthesis;
- organic matter carries both material constituents and chemical energy;
- the movement of carbon does not, by itself, describe the full energy budget;
- the cycling of nutrients does not mean that energy is recycled.

Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e, section 46.2; OpenStax Concepts of Biology, section 20.1; University of Michigan, Flow of Energy.

## 4. Primary producers: entry of energy into most food webs

Primary producers are organisms that make organic compounds from inorganic carbon sources. They include:

- plants;
- algae and phytoplankton;
- photosynthetic bacteria;
- chemosynthetic bacteria and archaea in environments where chemical energy supports primary production.

Photoautotrophs use light energy to drive photosynthesis. In broad terms, photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in organic molecules while fixing carbon from carbon dioxide.

Chemoautotrophs use energy released by chemical reactions rather than light. Their existence prevents the overstatement that every ecosystem's energy base must be direct sunlight.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Primary production supplies the organic matter and chemically stored energy that support consumers and other heterotrophs in the ecosystem.

**LIMIT:** Primary producers do not capture all incoming solar radiation. Only a fraction of incident radiation is available to photosynthetic reactions, and only part of the captured energy remains stored in new organic matter after the producer's own metabolic costs.

Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e, section 46.2; University of Michigan, Flow of Energy.

## 5. Gross primary production and net primary production

Two related but distinct quantities are essential.

### Gross primary production (GPP)

GPP is the total rate at which primary producers capture and incorporate energy through primary production. In photosynthetic terrestrial and aquatic ecosystem research, GPP is commonly estimated through carbon uptake during photosynthesis.

### Autotrophic respiration (Ra)

Primary producers use some of the captured chemical energy for cellular maintenance, metabolism, transport, growth processes, and other biological functions. Carbon released through this respiration returns to the atmosphere or surrounding water as carbon dioxide.

### Net primary production (NPP)

NPP is the remaining rate of new organic production after autotrophic respiration is accounted for.

In carbon-accounting notation:

NPP = GPP − Ra

The expression is commonly reported as a carbon flux per unit area per unit time, such as grams of carbon per square meter per year.

In energy-accounting work, production may instead be expressed as energy per unit area per unit time. A carbon-based estimate can serve as a proxy for chemical energy only when the relevant conversion assumptions and organic-matter composition are specified. Carbon mass and energy are not identical units.

**IMPORTANT DISTINCTION:** NPP is a rate of new biomass production, not the total standing biomass already present. A fast-growing population can have high productivity while maintaining relatively low standing biomass if its material is consumed or decomposed rapidly.

NASA's validation resource notes that GPP and NPP products are commonly expressed in carbon mass per area per time. It also describes field biomass harvesting, allometric relationships, and ecosystem CO2 exchange methods used to evaluate estimates.

Sources: NASA CEOS Land Product Validation Subgroup; OpenStax Biology 2e; University of Michigan, Flow of Energy.

## 6. Productivity is a rate; biomass is a stock

Record 0031 established the importance of distinguishing a pool from a flux. The same discipline is necessary here.

- **Standing biomass:** the amount of living or recently living biological material present within a defined area or trophic group at a particular time.
- **Productivity:** the rate at which new biomass is produced over a defined time interval.
- **Turnover:** the rate at which existing biomass is replaced, consumed, shed, or otherwise transferred.

A snapshot of biomass does not reveal the full rate of production.

OpenStax describes the English Channel example in which phytoplankton standing biomass is lower than zooplankton biomass. This does not mean the phytoplankton are incapable of supporting the consumers. Rapid phytoplankton reproduction and consumption can maintain high production while keeping the standing stock low at the moment it is measured.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** A small standing stock can support substantial consumer production when the producer population has a sufficiently high turnover rate.

**LIMIT:** Biomass, productivity, and turnover must be measured or estimated over compatible spatial boundaries and time intervals before quantitative comparisons are made.

Source: OpenStax Biology 2e, section 46.2.

## 7. Consumers: ingestion, assimilation, and production

Consumers obtain energy by ingesting living organisms, parts of organisms, or organic material. Energy does not pass automatically from one trophic level to the next merely because organisms are connected in a food web.

Three processes must be distinguished.

### Ingestion

The consumer takes in food or organic material. Not all available resource production is necessarily eaten.

### Assimilation

The consumer absorbs and incorporates a portion of the ingested material. Some ingested material is not assimilated and is eliminated as waste.

### Production

The consumer uses assimilated resources for maintenance and metabolism; the remainder available for growth and reproduction contributes to new consumer biomass.

Trophic transfer efficiency is influenced by the combined efficiencies of ingestion, assimilation, and production.

A useful conceptual expression is:

Trophic transfer efficiency = ingestion efficiency × assimilation efficiency × production efficiency

This is a framework for understanding why transfer varies. It is not a single universal constant.

Factors affecting the component efficiencies include:

- resource abundance and accessibility;
- prey defenses, mobility, and edibility;
- consumer feeding strategy;
- digestibility and biochemical composition;
- body size and physiology;
- ectothermic or endothermic metabolism;
- temperature and environmental conditions;
- activity, maintenance, growth, and reproduction.

Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e, section 46.2; Wu et al. (2026).

## 8. The 10% rule: useful approximation, not universal law

The familiar ecological rule of thumb states that approximately 10% of production at one trophic level is transferred into production at the next level.

It is useful for introductory models, but it must not be treated as a fixed law applying to every organism, food web, or ecosystem.

OpenStax's Silver Springs, Florida, example reports approximately 14.48% transfer efficiency between the primary-producer and primary-consumer levels. It also identifies substantial variation in how consumers use, assimilate, and convert food into new biomass.

A 2026 global synthesis by Wu and colleagues compiled 2,052 estimates from 122 studies:

- mean energy trophic-transfer efficiency: 5.92%;
- 95% confidence interval for the mean: 4.72%–7.39%;
- marine ecosystem mean: 8.13%;
- freshwater ecosystem mean: 5.53%;
- terrestrial ecosystem mean: 1.52%;
- mean nutrient trophic-transfer efficiency: 11.13%.

The study found that energy transfer efficiency varied among ecosystem types and with consumer and environmental characteristics. Its overall energy estimate was lower than the familiar 10% benchmark.

The energy and nutrient figures are especially important to keep separate. The synthesis found different average transfer efficiencies for energy and nutrients. A nutrient's movement through a food web is not the same measurement as the transfer of usable energy.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Trophic transfer efficiency is variable. The 10% figure is a teaching approximation, not a universal ecological constant.

**LIMIT:** The 2026 synthesis combines published estimates made under different study designs and definitions. Its results are a broad empirical synthesis, not a single direct measurement of every ecosystem. Its dataset also has unequal representation among marine, freshwater, and terrestrial systems.

Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e, section 46.2; Wu et al. (2026), *Science Advances*.

## 9. Grazing and detrital food webs

Food chains are simplified linear descriptions. Most ecosystems are better represented by food webs because organisms can feed at multiple trophic positions and can be consumed by several different organisms.

Two broad pathways are important.

### Grazing food web

A simplified pathway is:

Primary producer → herbivore → secondary consumer → higher-level consumer

Energy stored in producer biomass becomes available to consumers when they eat the producer or another consumer.

### Detrital food web

A second pathway begins with dead organic matter and biological waste:

Dead organisms and organic waste → detritivores and decomposers → consumers of those organisms

Detritivores consume organic detritus. Decomposers, including fungi and bacteria, break down dead and decaying organic material.

These pathways are connected. Material produced in a grazing food web can later enter detrital pathways through litter, dead organisms, shed tissues, and waste.

The detrital pathway is not an energy-recycling loop. Organisms in this pathway obtain chemical energy from organic material, use some of it in metabolism, and release heat. The material's chemical constituents can be transformed and returned to environmental pools where they may become available for new biological production.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Decomposers are not outside the ecosystem's energy budget. They are participants in food-web energy transfer and in the transformation of organic matter.

Sources: OpenStax Concepts of Biology, section 20.1; OpenStax Biology 2e, section 46.2.

## 10. Why energy pyramids differ from biomass pyramids

Ecologists use three common pyramid models:

- numbers of organisms;
- standing biomass;
- energy production or transfer over time.

Pyramids of numbers can be upright or inverted. A single tree may support many insects, for example.

Pyramids of standing biomass can also be inverted. In aquatic systems, rapidly reproducing phytoplankton can support a larger standing biomass of consumers even though the phytoplankton are the producers.

Energy pyramids are different. When defined over comparable trophic levels, areas, and time intervals, energy production available at successive trophic levels decreases because energy is used in metabolism, dissipated as heat, not assimilated, or transferred into other pathways such as detritus.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Energy pyramids are consistently upright, while number and standing-biomass pyramids can take different shapes.

**LIMIT:** A pyramid is a model. It depends on correct identification of trophic levels and compatible measurement boundaries. It is not a literal picture of every feeding relationship.

Source: OpenStax Biology 2e, section 46.2.

## 11. The connection to Record 0031: carbon is a carrier and accounting measure, not energy itself

Record 0031 distinguished carbon pools from carbon fluxes and identified photosynthesis as a process that transfers carbon from inorganic pools into organic biological material.

Record 0032 adds the energy perspective:

1. Photosynthesis fixes carbon into organic compounds while converting light energy into chemical energy.
2. The organic compounds contribute to producer biomass.
3. Some of the captured energy is used by the producer through respiration.
4. NPP represents new organic production after autotrophic respiration.
5. Consumers obtain chemical energy by ingesting organic material.
6. Decomposers obtain chemical energy from dead organic material and waste.
7. Respiration and other metabolic activity transform chemical energy, with heat released.
8. Carbon atoms can continue through biological, chemical, hydrological, and geological pathways even as usable energy becomes increasingly dispersed.

The carbon and energy budgets therefore overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

A carbon budget tracks the movement and storage of carbon across defined pools and boundaries. An energy budget tracks energy inputs, transformations, transfers, storage, and dissipation.

NASA's GPP/NPP resource reports those productivity products in carbon mass per area per time. This is a carbon-accounting convention for biological production, not evidence that grams of carbon are themselves a measure of energy without a defined conversion.

The USGS-hosted 2002 synthesis also cautions that net ecosystem production is a carbon-accumulation concept. It must account for relevant carbon transfers and losses, including dissolved and particulate export and disturbance. A simplified balance of GPP and respiration alone can omit important carbon pathways.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** The same biological process can be examined through more than one valid disciplinary accounting framework. The frameworks describe connected aspects of the process but must not be collapsed into one quantity.

Sources: NASA CEOS Land Product Validation Subgroup; Randerson et al. (2002), USGS Publications Warehouse; OpenStax Biology 2e.
