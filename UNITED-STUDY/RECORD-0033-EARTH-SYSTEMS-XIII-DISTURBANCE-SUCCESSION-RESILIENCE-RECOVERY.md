## 1. First discriminating question

How do disturbances alter the relationships among primary production, standing biomass, trophic pathways, nutrient cycling, and ecosystem recovery—and what can be measured to distinguish resistance, resilience, and a lasting change in ecosystem state?

Record 0032 distinguished energy flow from matter cycling, productivity from standing biomass, and trophic transfer from nutrient transfer. This record examines how those relationships respond when an ecosystem is disturbed.

The central caution is:

**Recovery does not necessarily mean return to the previous ecosystem state.**

A system may:
- experience little measurable change;
- change and subsequently return toward a reference condition;
- recover some functions but not its former species composition;
- reorganize into a different but functioning community;
- cross a threshold and persist in an alternative regime;
- continue changing because the disturbance regime or environmental conditions have also changed.

These outcomes must not be collapsed into one word.

## 3. Disturbance: what is being changed?

A disturbance is an event or process that alters ecological conditions, organisms, resource availability, or ecosystem structure and function.

Examples include:
- wildfire;
- drought;
- flooding;
- storms and windthrow;
- insect outbreaks;
- disease;
- landslides;
- volcanic activity;
- grazing;
- logging and land clearing;
- pollution;
- introduced species;
- altered fire, flood, or drought regimes.

A disturbance is not defined solely by whether humans caused it. Natural and human-caused disturbances can interact, and human activity can change the frequency, intensity, duration, or spatial pattern of disturbances.

Disturbance must be described using its actual attributes:
- agent or cause;
- magnitude or intensity;
- severity of ecological effects;
- spatial extent;
- duration;
- frequency and recurrence interval;
- season and timing;
- spatial pattern;
- pre-disturbance ecosystem condition;
- environmental conditions before, during, and after the event.

Intensity and severity should not be treated as synonyms. Intensity describes a property of the disturbance event; severity describes the effects on the ecosystem. For example, fire behavior and the resulting mortality, soil effects, or organic-matter consumption are related but distinct observations.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The same disturbance agent can produce different ecological outcomes depending on its characteristics and the ecosystem's condition.

**LIMIT:** Calling an event a disturbance does not by itself tell us whether the result is harmful, beneficial, neutral, temporary, or persistent. Those are empirical questions about the affected system and the reference condition.

Sources: Disturbance theory primer (2024); Turner and Seidl (2023).

## 4. Resistance, resilience, and recovery are not interchangeable

Ecological literature uses resilience in more than one established sense. The definitions must be declared before a measurement or comparison is made.

### Resistance

Resistance describes how little a system changes when exposed to a disturbance.

A system with high resistance shows relatively small changes in the measured variables or functions during the disturbance.

Examples of variables that might be measured:
- mortality;
- canopy cover;
- primary production;
- soil respiration;
- stream nutrient concentration;
- species abundance;
- ecosystem carbon exchange.

Resistance is not the same as recovery. If a system barely changes, there may be little change to recover from.

### Resilience as return rate

Some ecological stability frameworks use resilience to describe the rate at which a system returns toward a defined reference condition after it has changed.

This use asks: how quickly does a specified variable or function approach its reference trajectory?

The reference condition must be explicit. It may be a stable value, a seasonal cycle, or another dynamic trajectory.

### Ecological resilience in the Holling sense

Holling's ecological-resilience concept concerns how much disturbance a system can absorb before its underlying structure, processes, and feedbacks shift into a different regime.

This is not identical to the return-rate definition.

### Recovery

Recovery concerns the extent to which the system returns to its former reference condition. A system may recover one variable or function while remaining changed in other respects.

For example, vegetation cover may return while species composition, soil properties, nutrient cycling, or habitat structure remain different.

**IMPORTANT:** The scientific literature does not use the word resilience in one perfectly uniform way. A record must state which definition it is using instead of silently switching between them.

Sources: “Unifying the concepts of stability and resilience in ecology” (2021); USGS, “Quantifying resilience” (2016); Curtin and Parker (2019).

## 5. Succession: community change through time

Ecological succession describes changes in community composition and structure through time.

Following disturbance, succession may involve:
- surviving organisms resuming growth;
- seed banks germinating;
- seeds or spores arriving from surrounding areas;
- vegetative resprouting;
- colonization by early-arriving species;
- changes in competition and resource availability;
- establishment of later-arriving species;
- changes in vegetation structure and habitat;
- changes in soil organisms and nutrient processes.

Primary succession is generally used for development on newly exposed surfaces with little or no developed soil. Secondary succession occurs where a community has been disturbed but soil or biological legacies remain.

The distinction is not simply “life versus no life.” A severely disturbed site can retain seeds, roots, microbes, organic matter, soil structure, nutrients, and surviving organisms. These legacies influence what follows.

Succession is not necessarily a fixed sequence that always ends in one predictable climax community. The trajectory can be influenced by:
- disturbance recurrence;
- climate;
- soil and hydrology;
- topography;
- species availability;
- biological interactions;
- invasive species;
- herbivory;
- human land use;
- changing environmental conditions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Successional pathways depend on both the disturbance and the biological and physical legacies that remain afterward.

**LIMIT:** A generalized succession diagram is not a prediction for every location. A specific trajectory requires local evidence and an explicit time horizon.

Sources: Disturbance theory primer (2024); USGS, “Mechanisms of forest resilience” (2022); Turner and Seidl (2023).

## 6. Persistence, recovery, and reorganization in forests

The USGS forest-resilience framework distinguishes several mechanisms and outcomes.

### Persistence

Individuals survive disturbance or stress and continue to grow or reproduce.

Evidence may include:
- survival of mature trees;
- surviving roots or underground structures;
- resprouting;
- retained seed sources;
- continued function of surviving populations.

### Recovery through reproduction and establishment

When established individuals do not survive, populations may re-establish through seeds or other propagules.

Evidence may include:
- seed dispersal;
- germination;
- seedling establishment;
- vegetative regeneration;
- survival of young plants;
- recruitment into later size classes.

Recovery can fail if seed sources are absent, establishment conditions are unsuitable, repeated disturbances occur, or climate and other conditions no longer support the former community.

### Reorganization

A forest may retain forest cover but change in composition, structure, or both.

The 2022 PNAS synthesis identifies four broad forest pathways:

1. **Resilience:** structure and composition remain substantially unchanged.
2. **Restructuring:** structure changes while composition remains substantially similar.
3. **Reassembly:** composition changes while overall structure remains substantially similar.
4. **Replacement:** both structure and composition change.

A more profound change may lead from forest to a nonforest state, such as shrubland or grassland dominance.

These are conceptual pathways for classifying outcomes, not a mandatory sequence through which every forest passes.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Similar-looking vegetation cover does not prove that the original community has returned. Conversely, changed species composition does not automatically mean all ecosystem functions have failed.

Sources: USGS, “Mechanisms of forest resilience” (2022); PNAS, “Post-disturbance reorganization of forest ecosystems in a changing world” (2022).

## 7. Thresholds, feedbacks, and alternative states

Some ecosystems can persist in more than one configuration under a range of environmental conditions.

A threshold is a boundary beyond which a relatively small additional change can produce a disproportionately large shift in system behavior.

Feedbacks can reinforce a state or make a transition more likely.

Examples studied in ecology include:
- clear-water and turbid-water lake states;
- forest and grass-dominated states;
- coral-dominated and algae-dominated reef states;
- vegetated and degraded dryland states.

A regime shift is more than a temporary fluctuation. It involves a substantial reorganization of the system's structure, function, or controlling feedbacks.

The USGS discussion of resilience emphasizes that systems can reorganize into alternative states after a critical threshold is exceeded. Folke and colleagues describe how loss of functional groups, biodiversity, and response diversity, together with pollution, climate change, and altered disturbance regimes, can increase vulnerability to regime shifts.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** A disturbance's effect cannot always be predicted from its immediate magnitude alone. Prior conditions, accumulated pressures, feedbacks, and the system's proximity to a threshold can influence the outcome.

**LIMIT:** The existence of thresholds and alternative states must be demonstrated for the system under examination. Not every abrupt change is evidence of a tipping point, and not every ecosystem has two or more stable states.

Sources: USGS, “Quantifying resilience” (2016); Folke et al. (2004); Disturbance theory primer (2024).

## 8. Disturbance and the Record 0031–0032 budgets

The preceding records examined carbon and nutrient movement, then energy transfer and productivity. Disturbance connects these domains, but it does not affect them all in the same way.

### Carbon

A disturbance can change:
- living biomass;
- dead organic matter;
- litter inputs;
- soil carbon;
- photosynthetic uptake;
- plant and microbial respiration;
- combustion emissions;
- dissolved and particulate export;
- carbon accumulation over time.

A decrease in standing biomass does not by itself establish the full carbon balance. The fate of carbon must be followed across the relevant pools and boundaries.

### Nutrients

Disturbance can alter:
- nutrient uptake by vegetation;
- mineralization and decomposition;
- erosion and sediment movement;
- leaching and stream export;
- atmospheric deposition;
- gaseous nitrogen losses;
- microbial transformations;
- nutrient retention and availability.

A nutrient pulse after disturbance may be temporary, and increased availability does not automatically mean increased long-term retention or improved ecosystem condition.

### Energy and productivity

Disturbance can alter:
- photosynthetic leaf area;
- primary production;
- consumer resources;
- food-web structure;
- decomposition;
- respiration;
- energy transfer to consumers.

A reduction in canopy cover can change energy capture, but the size and duration of that effect depend on vegetation recovery, species composition, climate, and other conditions.

### Hydrology and soils

Disturbance may change:
- infiltration;
- surface runoff;
- evapotranspiration;
- soil temperature;
- soil moisture;
- erosion;
- sediment delivery;
- streamflow and water chemistry.

These changes can feed back into vegetation establishment and nutrient transport.

**SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** Disturbance is a cross-domain event whose consequences may be expressed simultaneously in biological, chemical, hydrological, geological, and atmospheric measurements.

**LIMIT:** A disturbance does not produce one universal direction of change. It may increase one measured flux while decreasing another, and responses may differ over days, seasons, years, and decades.

Sources: Disturbance theory primer (2024); Turner and Seidl (2023); USGS, “Mechanisms of forest resilience” (2022).

## 9. Novel disturbance regimes

A disturbance regime is the characteristic pattern of disturbances in a place over time.

Its attributes include:
- type of disturbance;
- frequency;
- severity;
- intensity;
- duration;
- spatial extent;
- seasonality;
- interactions with other disturbances.

A novel disturbance regime can arise when:
- an established disturbance becomes more frequent;
- events become more severe or last longer;
- disturbance timing changes;
- several disturbance agents interact;
- a new disturbance agent appears;
- climatic or biological conditions change the ecosystem's response.

Turner and Seidl (2023) emphasize that ecosystems may encounter disturbance regimes outside the historical ranges to which their communities were exposed. The resulting responses can be nonlinear and difficult to predict.

For example, repeated disturbances may prevent seedling establishment even when a single disturbance would have been followed by recovery. Drought can interact with insects or fire. Invasive species can alter fuel structure, competition, and regeneration.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The ecological outcome depends not only on the individual event but also on the changing pattern of events and the ecosystem's response capacity.

**LIMIT:** Historical ranges are not always completely known, and a short observational record may not capture the full range of natural variability.

Source: Turner and Seidl (2023).

## 10. What must be measured before declaring recovery?

A single indicator cannot establish complete ecosystem recovery.

A defensible assessment specifies:

1. **Reference condition:** What state or trajectory is the comparison target?
2. **Boundary:** What area, ecosystem, or watershed is being assessed?
3. **Time horizon:** How long after disturbance is the assessment made?
4. **Variables:** Which structural, compositional, and functional properties are measured?
5. **Measurement method:** Are results field measurements, remote sensing, models, or a combination?
6. **Uncertainty:** What is the sampling coverage and measurement error?
7. **Disturbance history:** Have subsequent disturbances occurred?
8. **External conditions:** Have climate, hydrology, land use, or other drivers changed?

Potential indicators include:

| Dimension | Possible measurements |
|---|---|
| Vegetation structure | Canopy cover, height, basal area, biomass |
| Species composition | Species presence, abundance, diversity, functional groups |
| Regeneration | Seedling density, recruitment, survival, resprouting |
| Productivity | GPP, NPP, growth rates |
| Carbon | Living biomass, deadwood, litter, soil carbon, CO2 exchange |
| Nutrients | Soil nutrient pools, mineralization, leaching, stream export |
| Hydrology | Infiltration, runoff, streamflow, soil moisture |
| Soil | Erosion, organic matter, temperature, microbial activity |
| Food webs | Consumer abundance, trophic relationships, decomposer activity |
| Ecosystem function | Pollination, decomposition, nutrient retention, habitat provision |

Remote sensing can help monitor canopy cover, vegetation greenness, burn severity, and changes over large areas. It does not automatically measure species composition, belowground processes, or all ecosystem functions.

A return to the same canopy-cover value does not prove that soil carbon, microbial communities, species composition, or trophic relationships have returned to their former conditions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Recovery is multidimensional and must be evaluated against an explicit reference using multiple appropriate indicators.
