The investigation must distinguish:

- species richness from species abundance;
- species diversity from genetic diversity;
- taxonomic diversity from functional diversity;
- species identity from ecological role;
- functional similarity from functional interchangeability;
- functional redundancy from complementarity;
- food-web relationships from mutualistic networks;
- local diversity from regional diversity;
- biodiversity from ecosystem function;
- ecosystem function from ecosystem services;

The governing caution is:

**A species count alone does not describe all the ecological roles, interactions, dependencies, or consequences of biodiversity change.**

## 3. Biodiversity: several dimensions, not one count

Biodiversity is commonly considered at multiple levels.

### Genetic diversity

Genetic variation within and among populations. It can affect traits, adaptation, disease resistance, and the capacity of populations to respond to changing conditions.

### Species diversity

Species diversity includes at least two distinct features:
- richness: how many species are present;
- evenness: how evenly individuals are distributed among those species.

A community with ten species dominated almost entirely by one species differs from a community with ten species represented more evenly.

### Functional diversity

Functional diversity concerns the variety and distribution of organism traits that influence ecological processes.

Examples of relevant traits include:
- plant rooting depth;
- leaf chemistry and structure;
- nitrogen fixation;
- growth rate;
- body size;
- feeding strategy;
- dispersal ability;
- drought tolerance;
- decomposition capacity;
- pollination behavior.

Functional diversity asks what organisms do and how their traits affect ecological processes, rather than only how many named species are present.

### Phylogenetic diversity

Phylogenetic diversity considers the evolutionary relationships among organisms. It can provide information about how much evolutionary history is represented in a community, although evolutionary distance does not automatically translate into functional difference.

### Ecosystem diversity

Variation among habitats, communities, and ecosystem types across a landscape or region.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Biodiversity is multidimensional. Species richness is useful, but it cannot by itself describe genetic variation, functional traits, evolutionary relationships, or ecosystem diversity.

**LIMIT:** No single biodiversity index captures every dimension. The metric must match the question being investigated.

## 4. Species identity and ecological roles

An organism's ecological role is not defined only by its taxonomic name.

A species can participate in several processes during its life:
- acquiring energy;
- consuming other organisms;
- fixing or transforming nutrients;
- dispersing seeds;
- pollinating plants;
- modifying habitat;
- competing for resources;
- providing food or shelter;
- decomposing organic matter;
- forming mutualistic or parasitic relationships.

A species may have more than one role, and the strength of each role may change with life stage, season, location, and environmental conditions.

A functional group collects organisms according to selected traits or processes. Examples include:
- nitrogen-fixing plants and microbes;
- pollinators;
- seed dispersers;
- herbivores;
- predators;
- decomposers;
- mycorrhizal fungi;
- ecosystem engineers.

Functional groups are analytical categories. They are not proof that all members are identical or interchangeable.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Ecological roles must be investigated through organism traits, interactions, and measured processes rather than inferred from a broad taxonomic label alone.

## 5. Complementarity and selection effects

Biodiversity experiments have identified several mechanisms through which species mixtures can influence ecosystem productivity.

### Complementarity effects

Species can use resources in different ways or at different times and places. This may allow a mixture to use available resources more completely than a monoculture.

Examples include:
- different rooting depths;
- differences in seasonal growth;
- different light-capture strategies;
- nitrogen-fixing species contributing nitrogen to the system;
- facilitation through changes to local soil or microclimate.

Complementarity can also include positive interactions among species.

### Selection effects

A diverse mixture has a greater chance of including a species that performs especially well under the tested conditions. Its contribution can increase the measured productivity of the mixture.

Selection and complementarity are distinct analytical contributions. They can occur together.

### Global experimental synthesis

Chen and colleagues (2025) analyzed 452 plant-diversity experiments and reported an average productivity increase of 15.2% when comparing monocultures with mixtures averaging 2.6 species.

Their analysis attributed 65.6% of the net biodiversity effect to complementarity and 34.4% to selection effects. The strength of the net effect varied among grassland, forest, container, cropland, and aquatic experiments.

The reported mean is not a universal productivity multiplier. It is an average across the experiments and conditions included in the analysis.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Species mixtures can increase productivity through complementarity and selection, but the magnitude and relative contribution of those mechanisms vary with species, ecosystem, and experimental context.

**LIMIT:** A productivity effect is not automatically evidence that every ecosystem function or service increases by the same amount.

Source: Chen et al. (2025).

## 6. Functional similarity is not interchangeability

The term functional redundancy is often used for cases in which several species appear to perform similar ecological functions.

However, the term can imply more interchangeability than the evidence supports.

Two species may perform a similar broad function while differing in:
- timing;
- environmental tolerance;
- resource use;
- interaction partners;
- response to drought or temperature;
- vulnerability to predators or disease;
- contribution under rare or extreme conditions.

A species that appears replaceable under present conditions may become important under a different disturbance or environmental state.

The 2023 article “Reconsidering functional redundancy in biodiversity research” argues that functional redundancy can be overused and misleading. It recommends thinking in terms of functional similarity as a gradient rather than assuming that species performing broadly similar functions are interchangeable.

A 2020 review and meta-analysis found a positive mean relationship between functional redundancy and ecological stability/resilience. But the evidence base directly linking measured redundancy to these outcomes was limited: 32 studies from 15 articles were included in the meta-analysis.

These findings are not contradictory. Functional similarity among multiple species may buffer some functions under some conditions, while the assumption that species are fully interchangeable remains unjustified.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Similarity can provide some capacity for compensation, but it does not establish complete interchangeability.

**LIMIT:** The amount of compensation depends on which species remain, the function being measured, environmental conditions, and whether the remaining organisms can maintain the relevant process.

Sources: “Reconsidering functional redundancy” (2023); “Does functional redundancy affect ecological stability and resilience?” (2020).

## 7. Ecological networks: relationships among organisms

An ecological network represents organisms or populations as nodes and their interactions as links.

Different network types describe different relationships.

### Food webs

Food-web links represent feeding relationships:
- predator and prey;
- herbivore and plant;
- consumer and resource;
- detritivore and organic matter.

Food webs describe pathways through which energy and material move.

### Mutualistic networks

Mutualisms are interactions in which both participating organisms receive a net benefit under the specified conditions.

Examples include:
- pollinators and flowering plants;
- seed-dispersing animals and plants;
- mycorrhizal fungi and plant roots;
- nitrogen-fixing symbioses.

The benefits and interaction strengths may vary with environmental conditions and partner identity.

### Host–parasite and disease networks

These networks represent relationships in which one organism uses another as a host, often imposing a cost on the host.

### Competition networks

Competition networks represent interactions where organisms reduce one another's access to limiting resources.

### Habitat-modification relationships

Some organisms alter physical conditions or create habitat used by other organisms. These relationships can affect many additional species.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** There is no single ecological network that represents every relationship. Feeding, mutualism, parasitism, competition, and habitat modification must be distinguished.

## 8. Network structure and network rewiring

An ecological network is not necessarily static.

The 2026 review “The rewiring of ecological networks in a variable world” identifies changes in:
- network topology: which organisms are connected;
- interaction strength: how strongly connected organisms affect one another.

Environmental change can alter interactions through changes in:
- species behavior;
- physiology;
- morphology;
- abundance;
- species composition;
- seasonal timing;
- resource availability.

For example, a pollinator may visit different flowering species when the timing or availability of flowers changes. A predator may shift its diet when its preferred prey declines. These changes can alter the interaction network even if the same species remain present.

Rewiring can be a response to ordinary environmental variability. It can also become persistent or directional under human-driven change.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Species presence alone does not describe the full network. The interactions among species, their strengths, and their timing also matter.

**LIMIT:** A changed interaction is not automatically evidence of network collapse or improved resilience. Its consequences depend on the wider network and the function being measured.

Source: “The rewiring of ecological networks in a variable world” (2026).

## 9. Biodiversity and ecosystem functioning

Ecosystem functions are biological, chemical, and physical processes within ecosystems.

Examples include:
- primary production;
- decomposition;
- nutrient cycling;
- soil formation;
- pollination;
- seed dispersal;
- water filtration and retention;
- carbon storage;
- regulation of herbivores and pests;
- habitat creation.

Ecosystem services are benefits people obtain from ecosystems. They are related to ecosystem functions but are not identical to them.

A process may be ecologically important without being directly measured as a human service. Conversely, a service can depend on several ecological functions and on social, economic, and geographic conditions.

### Global synthesis of 423 studies

Woodward and colleagues (2026) synthesized 423 studies of biodiversity and ecosystem functioning and services across terrestrial, freshwater, marine, and estuarine systems.

The synthesis reports that biodiversity enhances most functions and services examined, but the form and strength of the relationships vary.

Examples of findings include:
- regulating services such as water quality and pollination often showed linear relationships with biodiversity measures;
- oceanic blue-carbon sequestration was especially sensitive to biodiversity;
- some hazard-regulation services appeared less sensitive to broad diversity measures and depended strongly on a smaller number of functionally unique foundational species;
- the persistence of those foundational species nevertheless depended on wider community relationships.

The study also emphasizes that the general form of biodiversity–function relationships remains context dependent. Some functions may show saturating responses; others may show more linear relationships or sensitivity to particular species.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Biodiversity can support ecosystem functions and services, but different functions depend on different species, traits, and interactions.

**LIMIT:** A global synthesis cannot establish that every increase in species richness improves every function at every location. The specific function, diversity metric, ecosystem, and scale must be identified.

Source: Woodward et al. (2026).

## 10. Diversity and stability across trophic levels

Biodiversity–stability research has often examined one trophic group at a time, especially plants.

Srednick and Swearer (2024) reviewed 420 diversity–synchrony–stability studies:
- 74% were monotrophic, examining one trophic level;
- 26% were multitrophic;
- 91% of the monotrophic studies described stabilizing effects of asynchrony.

Their analysis of algae–herbivore communities showed that diversity and synchrony relationships differed between tropical and temperate systems.

The result matters because ecosystems are not isolated lists of species. Producers, herbivores, predators, decomposers, and mutualists interact.

### Asynchrony

Species populations may fluctuate at different times or in different directions.

If one species declines while another increases, aggregate community function may fluctuate less than the individual populations.

This can contribute to temporal stability at the community level.

But synchrony is not universally harmful or beneficial. Its effects depend on the trophic group, interaction structure, environmental conditions, and the scale of measurement.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Diversity–stability relationships can change when multiple trophic levels are considered. Single-level results cannot automatically be generalized to whole food webs.

**LIMIT:** The 2024 review identifies a research imbalance; its reported proportions describe the reviewed literature, not the actual proportions of ecological processes in nature.

Source: Srednick and Swearer (2024).

## 11. Species composition and ecosystem function can recover differently

Record 0033 established that ecosystem recovery is multidimensional.

The 2020 global meta-analysis of intact and recovering ecosystems examined 478 comparisons from 21 studies.

It reported:
- intact sites within the same natural ecosystem could differ substantially in species composition while retaining more similar ecosystem functioning;
- in recovering ecosystems, the relationship between recovery of species composition and recovery of ecosystem function was weak and saturating;
- species composition directly explained only a small portion of the variation in function in the examined recovering sites;
- relationships differed among functions, with biomass and biotic structure showing different patterns from several soil functions.

This does not mean species composition is unimportant. It means that species composition and ecosystem function are related in complex ways and cannot be assumed to recover in lockstep.

A function can be maintained by a changed community, and a community can regain some species without restoring every function.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Species composition and ecosystem function are distinct measurements. Recovery of one does not prove recovery of the other.

**LIMIT:** The synthesis included 21 studies and 478 comparisons. Its conclusions should not be treated as a universal law for all ecosystems or all functions.

Source: “The species composition—ecosystem function relationship” (2020).

## 12. Forest biodiversity experiments: evidence and scale

Forest biodiversity experiments are important because forests contain interacting plant, soil, microbial, herbivore, predator, and mutualist communities.

Liu and colleagues (2026) review three decades of forest biodiversity experiments and report that increasing tree diversity can promote multiple ecosystem functions through:
- resource partitioning;
- abiotic facilitation;
- biotic facilitation;
- other species interactions.

The review notes that some effects strengthen over time in the longest-running experiments.

It also identifies trophic mediation: diversity at other trophic levels can influence the effects of tree diversity on forest productivity.

The authors emphasize that new experiments manipulating tree diversity and diversity at other trophic levels together are needed to test causal relationships more directly.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Forest productivity cannot always be explained by tree species richness alone. Interactions with soil organisms and other trophic groups can influence the outcome.

**LIMIT:** Forest biodiversity experiments cover a limited set of sites and conditions compared with all global forests. Experimental results must be interpreted within their tested species combinations, environments, and time spans.

Source: Liu et al. (2026).
