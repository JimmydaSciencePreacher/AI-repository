## 1. First discriminating question

How do organisms beneath the visible surface transform, transport, exchange, and make nutrients available—and how do roots, fungi, bacteria, archaea, soil animals, water, gases, and mineral surfaces participate in relationships that sustain terrestrial ecosystems?

The visible plant is not an isolated system. Its growth is related to physical soil conditions and biological processes occurring in and around the root zone.

**Central distinction:** Soil is not merely a container holding roots. It is a physical, chemical, and biological environment in which materials are transformed and transported through interacting processes.

This record does not assume that all soil organisms benefit plants, every plant requires mycorrhizae, all fungi connect all plants, resource exchange is always equal, or microbial presence proves microbial activity.

## 3. Soil: physical, chemical, and biological domains

Soil develops through interactions among mineral material, organic matter, organisms, water, air, and time.

Its properties include mineral particle size and composition; soil structure and aggregation; pore spaces and bulk density; water content and movement; oxygen availability and gas exchange; temperature; pH; organic matter; nutrient pools; and roots and soil organisms.

Soil is heterogeneous. Conditions differ over millimeters, centimeters, depth, season, and landscape position.

- **Mineral material:** Sand, silt, clay, and mineral surfaces differ in physical and chemical properties. Clay and iron- or aluminum-bearing minerals can interact with organic compounds.
- **Organic matter:** Includes plant residues, roots and root-derived inputs, microbial cells and residues, animal remains, dissolved organic compounds, and mineral-associated organic matter.
- **Pore space:** Holds air and water and provides pathways for roots, organisms, and dissolved substances.

**FINDING:** Soil processes cannot be reduced to biology alone or chemistry alone. Organisms operate within a physical and chemical environment that affects what can be accessed, transformed, and transported.

## 4. The rhizosphere: the root-influenced zone

The rhizosphere is the soil region influenced by living roots and their activities. Roots affect it through water and nutrient uptake, root growth and turnover, release of exudates and mucilage, changes in pH, and interactions with fungi, bacteria, and other organisms.

Root exudates include sugars, amino acids, organic acids, and other metabolites. Their composition varies with plant identity, developmental stage, nutrient status, soil, and stress.

Exudates can provide carbon substrates to microorganisms, alter microbial activity and community composition, mobilize some mineral nutrients, affect local pH, influence microbial competition, and participate in plant defense and signaling.

The rhizosphere is not simply a zone in which microbes are fed. It is a changing interface where plant activity alters microbial conditions and microbial activity can alter nutrient availability and plant performance.

A 2026 meta-analysis of 121 publications and 3,174 observations found that root-exudate additions increased microbial biomass on average while decreasing several measures of microbial alpha diversity. Thus “more microbial biomass” and “more microbial diversity” are not the same result.

**LIMIT:** Exudate-addition experiments differ from natural root release in timing, concentration, soil conditions, and design. A microbial response does not by itself establish a whole-ecosystem benefit.

Sources: 2026 root-exudate meta-analysis; 2026 rhizosphere review.

## 5. Decomposition, mineralization, and immobilization

These terms are related but not identical.

- **Decomposition:** Breakdown and transformation of dead organic material through physical, chemical, and biological processes. It can involve fragmentation by soil animals, enzymatic breakdown by fungi and bacteria, consumption and transformation, and leaching of soluble compounds.
- **Mineralization:** Conversion of organically bound elements into inorganic forms. Examples include organic nitrogen becoming ammonium and organic phosphorus becoming inorganic phosphate.
- **Immobilization:** Uptake of inorganic nutrients by microorganisms or plants and incorporation into organic biomass.

Mineralization and immobilization can occur simultaneously. Nutrients released by mineralization may be immobilized, adsorbed to minerals, transported in water, or lost from the ecosystem.

**FINDING:** Decomposition describes breakdown and transformation of organic material; mineralization converts organically bound elements into inorganic forms; immobilization incorporates available nutrients into biomass.

**LIMIT:** Faster decomposition does not necessarily mean greater long-term nutrient retention or soil carbon accumulation. The fate of products must be traced.

## 6. Bacteria, archaea, fungi, and soil animals

- **Bacteria** participate in decomposition, nutrient transformations, nitrogen fixation in appropriate organisms and conditions, nitrification, denitrification, phosphorus mobilization, root interactions, and disease suppression or disease.
- **Archaea** include organisms involved in important soil processes, including ammonia oxidation in many soils.
- **Fungi** participate in decomposition, nutrient acquisition and transport, plant symbioses, soil aggregation, microbial interactions, disease, and parasitism.
- **Soil animals** such as nematodes, mites, springtails, earthworms, enchytraeids, and insects influence residue fragmentation, microbial grazing, nutrient release, soil mixing, pore formation, and aggregation.

Different taxa have different capabilities. A taxonomic identification does not establish that a process is active at a particular time.

**FINDING:** Soil function emerges from many organism groups interacting with one another and with physical and chemical conditions.

**LIMIT:** DNA profiles, organism counts, or biomass measurements do not independently reveal the rate of every process performed by a community.

## 7. Nitrogen transformations

Important nitrogen processes include:

1. **Biological nitrogen fixation:** Certain bacteria and archaea convert atmospheric N₂ into biologically usable reduced nitrogen, commonly ammonia/ammonium, through nitrogenase-mediated processes. It is energetically demanding and occurs only in organisms with the relevant machinery under suitable conditions.
2. **Ammonification:** Organic nitrogen compounds are converted to ammonium during decomposition and mineralization.
3. **Nitrification:** Specialized microorganisms oxidize ammonium to nitrite and then nitrate.
4. **Denitrification:** Under suitable conditions, microorganisms reduce nitrate and related nitrogen oxides toward gaseous products, including N₂ and N₂O.
5. **Plant and microbial uptake:** Organisms take up ammonium and nitrate and incorporate nitrogen into organic compounds.

These processes depend on oxygen, water content, temperature, pH, carbon and nitrogen availability, soil structure, and microbial composition.

**FINDING:** Nitrogen availability is not a fixed property of soil. It is influenced by ongoing transformations, biological uptake, transport, and loss.

**LIMIT:** The presence of an organism associated with a nitrogen process does not prove that the process is occurring at a measurable rate. Rates require appropriate process measurements or validated proxies.

## 8. Phosphorus and mineral interactions

Phosphorus occurs in dissolved inorganic forms, organic compounds, mineral-associated pools, precipitated or strongly sorbed forms, and plant or microbial biomass.

Roots and microorganisms can influence phosphorus availability through uptake, organic-acid release, phosphatase enzymes, mineral weathering, local pH changes, and mycorrhizal exploration of soil.

Unlike nitrogen, phosphorus has no major atmospheric gas phase comparable to the N₂ reservoir. Its movement is strongly influenced by mineral weathering, biological cycling, erosion, and water transport.

A nutrient can be present in soil but unavailable for immediate plant uptake because it is chemically bound, physically inaccessible, or outside the active root zone.

**FINDING:** Total soil nutrient content and immediately available nutrient content are distinct measurements.

## 9. Mycorrhizae: root–fungus associations

Mycorrhizae are symbiotic associations between plant roots and certain fungi.

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizae (AM):** AM fungi enter the root cortex and form branched arbuscules at the plant–fungus exchange interface. They are widespread among land plants, though not universal across all plant lineages.
- **Ectomycorrhizae (ECM):** ECM fungi form a sheath around fine roots and a network between root cells called the Hartig net. They are important partners of many tree species.
- **Ericoid and other forms:** Specialized associations occur in particular plant groups and environments, with differing nutrient-acquisition strategies.

A simplified exchange:
- the plant supplies photosynthetically fixed carbon compounds to the fungus;
- the fungal partner explores soil beyond the immediate root surface and can transfer mineral nutrients to the plant;
- the exchange is influenced by plant and fungal identity, soil conditions, nutrient availability, and other organisms.

This is not a fixed equal barter. Costs and benefits vary. In some conditions the association can provide little benefit or impose a net cost on a plant.

A 2024 review of forest trees describes mycorrhizal nutrient acquisition in exchange for plant carbon and emphasizes variation among ectomycorrhizal fungi and environmental contexts.

A 2024 meta-analysis of 187 original studies reported average increases in plant biomass and nitrogen and phosphorus measures following AMF inoculation. Responses varied with host group, experimental conditions, fungal treatment, nutrient conditions, and water availability. These averages are not guaranteed field outcomes.

**FINDING:** Mycorrhizal fungi can extend plant access to soil resources and participate in nutrient exchange, but the form and benefit of the association vary.

**LIMIT:** Mycorrhizae are not a universal requirement for every plant. Inoculation results do not guarantee equivalent outcomes in mature natural ecosystems.

Sources: Pena and Tibbett (2024); Wu et al. (2024).

## 10. The plant–fungus–bacterium continuum

Duan et al. (2024), in *Nature Reviews Microbiology*, describe a plant–arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungus–bacterium continuum.

The framework recognizes that:
- plants supply carbon to fungal partners;
- fungal hyphae extend into surrounding soil;
- bacteria can associate with fungal hyphae;
- some bacteria live within fungal cells;
- bacteria and fungi can influence nutrient transformations;
- mineral nutrients, especially phosphorus and nitrogen, move through interacting biological interfaces.

The **hyphosphere** is the soil region influenced by fungal hyphae. The **mycorrhizosphere** is the broader soil region influenced by the plant–mycorrhizal association.

The review describes top-down carbon movement from plants and bottom-up mineral nutrient movement through fungal and bacterial associations. “Continuum” describes connected biological interactions; it does not mean every plant, fungus, and bacterium belongs to one universal network.

**FINDING:** Nutrient acquisition can involve multiple interacting organisms rather than a simple direct transfer from soil mineral to plant root.

**LIMIT:** Pathways and magnitudes depend on species, soil, nutrient, and environment. Mechanisms proposed for one association cannot automatically be generalized to all mycorrhizal systems.

Source: Duan et al. (2024).

## 11. Carbon allocation and the limits of barter language

Plants provide carbon to mycorrhizal fungi, and fungi can provide nutrients to plants. It is tempting to describe this as a perfectly regulated market or equal exchange.

A 2024 *New Phytologist* review and synthesis examined whether plant carbon transfer is directly regulated by nutrient delivery from fungi. Its authors concluded that current knowledge did not establish a simple direct regulation of carbon transfer by fungal nutrient delivery. They found plant growth responses more closely linked to nutrient uptake than to carbon transfer itself, while identifying research gaps.

Therefore:
- carbon allocation is real;
- fungal nutrient transfer is real;
- regulation is complex;
- exact causal coupling is not fully resolved;
- economic metaphors such as “trade,” “reward,” or “sanction” require caution.

**LIMIT:** The review does not prove nutrient delivery never affects carbon allocation. It concludes that current evidence does not establish a simple direct mechanism as a general rule.

Source: “What determines transfer of carbon from plants to mycorrhizal fungi?” (2024).

## 12. Fungal networks: what is established and what is not

Fungal hyphae can form extensive structures through soil. Mycorrhizal fungi can connect with plant roots and create pathways for resource movement.

**Supported:**
- hyphae extend beyond root surfaces;
- mycorrhizal fungi transfer nutrients to host plants;
- plants allocate carbon to fungal partners;
- some experiments demonstrate movement of labeled substances through fungal pathways;
- fungal and bacterial interactions can affect nutrient cycling.

**Requires context-specific evidence:**
- whether two plants are connected by the same fungal individual;
- whether a resource reaches another plant through a direct fungal pathway;
- whether transfer meaningfully affects recipient growth or survival;
- whether a network persists over a specified period;
- whether effects occur at field and ecosystem scales.

**Not established merely by saying “network”:**
- all nearby trees are connected;
- all connected plants exchange resources;
- transfers are always beneficial;
- fungi intentionally choose recipients in a human-like sense;
- a forest operates as one brain or conscious organism;
- a network has a centralized controller.

The 2024 review of AM networks emphasizes experimental complexity and ecological relevance. Results from simplified laboratory systems must be distinguished from evidence in complex natural ecosystems.

**FINDING:** Belowground fungal networks are real biological structures, but popular descriptions can exceed demonstrated evidence about connectivity, resource sharing, and ecological effects.

## 13. Decomposition, mycorrhizae, and soil carbon

Mycorrhizal fungi can influence decomposition, but the direction is not uniform.

- **Gadgil effect:** Ectomycorrhizal fungi may compete with free-living decomposers for nutrients or substrates and suppress some decomposition processes.
- **Priming effect:** Inputs or activities associated with roots and mycorrhizal fungi may stimulate microbial decomposition of existing soil organic matter.

A 2024 meta-analysis of 43 studies and 97 trials reported that mycorrhizal fungi promoted decomposition by 6.7% on average across included comparisons, with substantial variation.

This does not mean all mycorrhizal fungi always accelerate decomposition. Outcomes depend on fungal type, substrate, soil conditions, microbial partners, and experimental design.

Soil carbon storage is not determined by decomposition rate alone. Carbon enters soil through root turnover, exudates, litter, fungal and microbial biomass, animal residues, and dissolved organic compounds. Carbon leaves or is transformed through respiration to CO₂, dissolved export, erosion, combustion, and movement into other pools.

Some organic matter becomes associated with minerals or physically protected within aggregates. A 2024 soil-organic-matter review emphasizes plant–microbe–mineral interactions and controls on stabilization and loss.

**FINDING:** Soil carbon outcomes depend on carbon inputs, microbial transformation, mineral interactions, physical protection, and losses.

**LIMIT:** Greater fungal activity, microbial biomass, or decomposition does not automatically mean greater soil carbon storage. Net carbon balance must be measured or estimated across defined pools and boundaries.

Sources: Mycorrhizal decomposition meta-analysis (2024); soil organic matter frameworks review (2024).

## 14. Soil structure, aggregation, and water

Soil structure is the arrangement of mineral particles and organic materials into aggregates and the pore spaces between them.

Biological contributions include fungal hyphae binding or enmeshing particles, roots influencing soil structure, microbial extracellular polymers, organic matter inputs, and earthworm and other soil-animal activity.

Aggregates influence water infiltration and retention, aeration, root penetration, erosion resistance, and physical protection of organic matter.

The relationships are not one-directional. Soil texture, mineralogy, moisture, land use, disturbance, and organic matter all influence aggregation.

**LIMIT:** A single aggregate-stability measurement does not describe every soil function or establish long-term carbon sequestration.
