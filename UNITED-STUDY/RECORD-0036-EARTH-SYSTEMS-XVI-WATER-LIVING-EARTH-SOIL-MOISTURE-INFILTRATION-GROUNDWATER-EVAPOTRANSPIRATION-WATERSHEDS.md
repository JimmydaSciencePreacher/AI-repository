## 3. Water pools and water fluxes

A useful first distinction is between a **pool** and a **flux**.

- A **pool** is water stored in a place or form: ocean water, atmospheric vapor, snow and ice, lakes, rivers, wetlands, soil moisture, groundwater, or water held in living organisms.
- A **flux** is water moving between pools or across a defined boundary: precipitation, evaporation, transpiration, infiltration, percolation, runoff, streamflow, groundwater recharge, or groundwater discharge.

A pool is a quantity or storage. A flux is a movement over time. The two must not be confused.

Water also changes physical state:
- liquid water can evaporate into vapor;
- vapor can condense into liquid droplets;
- liquid water can freeze;
- ice can melt;
- snow or ice can sublime directly into vapor.

The hydrological cycle is not a single circular pipe with one fixed route. Water can follow different pathways, remain in storage for different lengths of time, and move between surface and subsurface systems.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Water is continually exchanged among atmospheric, surface, subsurface, frozen, and biological pools. Solar energy and gravity are major drivers of these movements.

**LIMIT:** A general water-cycle diagram identifies important pathways but does not establish the quantity, rate, residence time, or direction of every transfer at a particular site.

## 4. Precipitation, interception, and the land surface

Precipitation reaches the land as rain, snow, sleet, hail, or other forms. Before water reaches the soil, some may be intercepted by vegetation, buildings, or other surfaces.

Canopy interception may:
- temporarily store water on leaves, branches, and stems;
- return some water to the atmosphere through evaporation;
- direct some water down stems as stemflow;
- release some water as throughfall.

The amount and timing depend on precipitation characteristics, canopy structure, wind, temperature, and antecedent wetness.

When water reaches the ground, it may:
- infiltrate into soil;
- collect in depressions or surface-water bodies;
- move downslope as overland flow;
- enter a stream through surface or subsurface pathways;
- evaporate;
- be stored temporarily as snow or ice.

**IMPORTANT DISTINCTION:** Precipitation is an input to a land area. It is not identical to infiltration, runoff, recharge, or plant-available water.

## 5. Infiltration and percolation

**Infiltration** is the entry of water from the land surface into the soil.

**Percolation** is the subsequent movement of water through soil or rock, commonly downward under gravity, although subsurface flow can also have horizontal and upward components under appropriate hydraulic conditions.

Infiltration is affected by:
- rainfall or snowmelt intensity and duration;
- antecedent soil moisture and saturation;
- soil texture and structure;
- pore continuity and compaction;
- vegetation and surface cover;
- slope and surface roughness;
- frozen ground;
- land use and impervious surfaces.

A dry, well-structured soil may accept water at a different rate from a compacted or already saturated soil. Clay-rich and sandy soils differ in pore-size distribution and hydraulic behavior; it is not sufficient to rank every soil by texture alone because structure, organic matter, layering, and compaction also matter.

Water that infiltrates does not necessarily reach an aquifer. It may remain in the root zone, move laterally, be taken up by plants, evaporate from near-surface soil, enter deeper unsaturated layers, or eventually reach the saturated zone.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Infiltration and percolation connect surface hydrology with soil physics and groundwater hydrology.

**LIMIT:** The amount of precipitation infiltrating at a site cannot be inferred from rainfall total alone. Soil condition, rainfall intensity, slope, land cover, and antecedent moisture matter.

## 6. Soil-water storage and plant-available water

Soil pores contain varying proportions of air and water. Water is retained by capillary and surface forces, and its movement depends on gradients in water potential and the hydraulic properties of the soil.

Several soil-water conditions must be distinguished:

- **Saturation:** Most or all connected pore space is filled with water. Oxygen availability to roots can become limited when air-filled pore space is reduced.
- **Field capacity:** A practical soil-water condition after excess gravitational water has drained substantially. It is not a universal fixed water content for every soil.
- **Permanent wilting point:** A conventional reference condition at which a plant can no longer recover turgor under the defined test conditions.
- **Available water capacity:** Commonly estimated as the water content at field capacity minus the water content at permanent wilting point, for a specified soil depth or volume.

The amount of water a soil can hold and the amount a plant can extract are not identical. Soil texture and structure, rooting depth, root distribution, salinity, temperature, plant species, and atmospheric demand influence actual uptake.

A soil can contain water that roots cannot extract rapidly enough to meet transpiration demand. Conversely, water may be present at depth but inaccessible to a plant whose roots do not reach it.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Total soil moisture is not equivalent to plant-available water. Water retention and accessibility depend on soil and plant properties.

**LIMIT:** Field capacity and wilting point are operational reference concepts. They do not mean every plant responds identically or that a single numerical threshold describes all soils and species.

## 7. The unsaturated zone, saturated zone, and groundwater

Below the land surface, water may move through an **unsaturated zone**, where pores contain both air and water, and a **saturated zone**, where connected pores and fractures are filled with water.

The upper surface of the saturated zone is commonly called the water table. Its depth can change with recharge, discharge, pumping, season, and local geology.

**Groundwater** is water stored and moving within saturated pores and fractures in soil, sediment, and rock. An **aquifer** is a geological formation or body of material capable of storing and transmitting usable quantities of groundwater.

Groundwater behavior depends on:
- porosity and the connected pore or fracture network;
- hydraulic conductivity and permeability;
- hydraulic gradients;
- aquifer geometry and confining layers;
- recharge and discharge;
- pumping and other human withdrawals.

Groundwater can move slowly or, in some fractured or karst settings, move through preferential pathways. Residence times vary widely.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Groundwater is connected to surface water through recharge and discharge. Groundwater can sustain springs, wetlands, and stream base flow, and streams can also recharge groundwater under appropriate conditions.

**LIMIT:** Not every stream is groundwater-fed to the same degree, and not every infiltrating water molecule reaches a deep aquifer. The connection must be evaluated for the specific geological and hydrological setting.

## 8. Groundwater recharge and discharge

**Groundwater recharge** is water entering a groundwater system. A common natural pathway is precipitation that infiltrates the land surface and percolates through the unsaturated zone to the saturated zone. Recharge can also occur from rivers, lakes, irrigation, or other sources where hydraulic conditions permit.

Recharge is not necessarily immediate. Water may take days, years, or much longer to pass through the unsaturated zone, depending on geology, soil, depth, and pathways.

**Groundwater discharge** occurs when groundwater leaves an aquifer or saturated zone, for example:
- into a stream or river;
- at a spring;
- into a lake or wetland;
- into the ocean;
- through pumping or other withdrawals.

The distinction between recharge and discharge matters for water availability. An aquifer can receive recharge and still decline if discharge and withdrawals exceed replenishment over the relevant period.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Groundwater storage is dynamic. Its condition reflects the balance of inflows and outflows over time.

**LIMIT:** A wet season or a single storm does not establish that a deep aquifer has been replenished. Recharge rates and storage responses must be measured or modeled for the particular aquifer.

## 9. Root uptake and the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum

The **soil–plant–atmosphere continuum (SPAC)** describes linked water movement from soil, through roots and plant tissues, and into the atmosphere.

A simplified pathway is:

1. Water moves through soil toward the root surface.
2. Roots absorb water across root tissues.
3. Water enters the plant's vascular system, including xylem.
4. Water moves toward leaves through the plant's hydraulic pathway.
5. Water evaporates from moist internal leaf surfaces and diffuses through stomata to the atmosphere as transpiration.

Water movement is strongly influenced by gradients in water potential. Under many conditions, the water potential becomes progressively more negative from soil toward leaves and the atmosphere, driving movement along the pathway.

This is not simply a passive pipe. Soil hydraulic conductivity, root architecture, root membrane properties, xylem structure, leaf anatomy, stomatal regulation, and plant water status influence the rate.

As soil dries, hydraulic conductivity can decline and water becomes more difficult to deliver to roots. Plants may alter stomatal opening, root growth, leaf area, osmotic regulation, or other physiological responses. These responses vary among species and environments.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Plant water use connects soil hydrology, plant physiology, and atmospheric exchange. Vegetation is both influenced by water availability and an active mediator of terrestrial water fluxes.

**LIMIT:** The SPAC is a scientific framework for water transport, not a claim that every plant uses an identical hydraulic strategy. Crop studies and models must not be generalized without qualification to every forest, grassland, wetland, or plant species.

## 10. Evaporation, transpiration, and evapotranspiration

These terms describe related but distinguishable processes.

- **Evaporation:** Liquid water changes to vapor from soil, open water, wet vegetation, or other surfaces.
- **Transpiration:** Water absorbed by plants moves through plant tissues and is released as vapor, primarily through leaf stomata.
- **Evapotranspiration (ET):** The combined transfer of water vapor to the atmosphere through evaporation and transpiration. Depending on the measurement or model, it may include evaporation from intercepted water on vegetation.

ET is influenced by available energy, humidity, wind, temperature, soil moisture, vegetation type, leaf area, rooting depth, and stomatal behavior.

ET is not simply a property of the atmosphere or the plant alone. It emerges from interactions between energy, water availability, vegetation, and atmospheric conditions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Vegetation changes the partitioning and timing of water movement between soil and atmosphere. The same precipitation input can produce different ET and runoff patterns under different vegetation and land-cover conditions.

**LIMIT:** ET estimates may be direct, indirect, or model-derived. Measurement methods have different spatial and temporal scales and uncertainties. A single ET estimate should not be treated as a direct measurement of transpiration alone.

## 11. Runoff, streamflow, and watershed response

A **watershed** or **drainage basin** is the land area from which water drains toward a common outlet, subject to the chosen surface-water and groundwater boundaries.

Water can reach a stream through:
- overland flow;
- shallow subsurface flow;
- groundwater discharge;
- direct precipitation onto the stream;
- snowmelt and other stored-water releases.

Runoff response depends on rainfall intensity and duration, soil saturation, infiltration capacity, topography, geology, vegetation, drainage networks, wetlands, lakes, and human land use.

A stream may continue flowing after rainfall has stopped because groundwater or other stored water continues to discharge. This sustained contribution is commonly described as base flow, although its sources and proportions vary by watershed.

Urbanization and impervious surfaces can reduce infiltration and increase rapid runoff, while drainage, dams, reservoirs, channel modification, irrigation, and water withdrawals can alter timing and magnitude of streamflow.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Streamflow integrates multiple upstream processes. A river hydrograph is not a direct record of rainfall alone; it reflects storage, routing, groundwater exchange, evapotranspiration, and watershed characteristics.

**LIMIT:** A measured streamflow change does not identify its cause by itself. Attribution requires additional observations and analysis.

## 12. Water budgets: accounting for inputs, outputs, and storage

A water budget applies conservation of mass to a defined system boundary and time interval.

For a simplified watershed, one common form is:

`P + Q_{in} = ET + \Delta S + Q_{out}`

where:
- `P` = precipitation entering the watershed;
- `Q_{in}` = water entering across the watershed boundary, such as surface-water or groundwater inflow;
- `ET` = evapotranspiration leaving to the atmosphere;
- `ΔS` = change in water stored within the defined watershed;
- `Q_{out}` = water leaving as surface-water or groundwater outflow.

Additional terms may be required for imports, diversions, pumping, irrigation return flows, or other human transfers. The exact equation depends on the system boundary and which terms are included.

For a simplified closed watershed with no external inflows or diversions, a commonly used expression is:

`\Delta S = P - ET - Q`

where `Q` is the measured streamflow leaving the basin, provided groundwater exchange and other omitted terms are negligible or accounted for in the storage/boundary definition.

The equation is an accounting relationship. It does not, by itself, explain the physical mechanisms that determine infiltration, groundwater flow, ET, or runoff. Each term must be measured, estimated, or modeled, and uncertainty must be considered.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** A water budget provides a common quantitative framework for relating distinct hydrological processes without treating them as identical.

**LIMIT:** A water budget can appear balanced because of measurement uncertainty, omitted fluxes, or assumptions about groundwater boundaries. A mathematically closed account does not guarantee that every component has been independently measured accurately.

## 13. Water, soil, nutrients, and living systems

Water movement connects directly to the soil processes examined in Record 0035.

Water can:
- dissolve and transport ions and soluble organic compounds;
- carry nutrients toward roots;
- move nutrients downward through leaching;
- transport sediment and particulate matter;
- alter oxygen availability in soil pores;
- influence microbial activity and decomposition;
- affect root growth and root–soil contact;
- change the movement and availability of phosphorus and nitrogen;
- transport contaminants into groundwater or streams.

Water transport is not nutrient transport in every case. Dissolved substances may move with water, but adsorption, precipitation, biological uptake, chemical reactions, filtration, and physical retention can alter their movement.

The same water flux can therefore have different consequences depending on the chemical species, soil minerals, biological activity, and hydrological pathway.

**FINDING:** Hydrology, soil science, geochemistry, microbiology, plant physiology, and ecology meet at real material interfaces. Water is a transport medium and a biological requirement, but it does not erase the distinct behavior of the substances and organisms involved.

**LIMIT:** A water-flow measurement alone does not establish nutrient flux or contaminant transport. Those require concentration data and an appropriate flow measurement over the same interval and boundary.

## 14. Scale, timing, and residence

Hydrological processes operate across different spatial and temporal scales.

- Rainfall intensity can change over minutes.
- Soil moisture can vary across centimeters, depths, and days.
- Plant uptake and transpiration vary over hours, seasons, and growth stages.
- Streamflow responds over event, seasonal, and annual scales.
- Groundwater storage and movement can integrate years to centuries or longer in some systems.

A local soil-moisture sensor does not represent an entire watershed. A daily streamflow value does not describe the full annual water balance. A short experiment cannot automatically establish long-term groundwater behavior.

**FINDING:** The same system can show different behavior depending on the observation scale and time interval.

**LIMIT:** Scale mismatch is a common source of invalid inference. Comparisons must state the spatial boundary, depth, time interval, and measurement method.
