## 1. First discriminating question

How do flowing water, gravity, soil structure, vegetation, geology, and land use detach, transport, deposit, and transform sediment—and how are sediment movement, dissolved loads, channel form, floodplains, wetlands, and aquatic habitats connected within a watershed?

Record 0036 followed water through the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum and watershed. This record follows material carried by that water. Water and sediment interact, but they are not the same material and do not necessarily move together at the same rate or by the same mechanism.

**Central distinction:** Erosion, transport, and deposition are related stages or processes, not synonyms. Material can be detached without being transported far; transported material can be deposited temporarily and later remobilized; and material removed from one location does not necessarily reach a watershed outlet.

This record does not assume that all erosion is caused by water, all eroded soil reaches a stream, all sediment is harmful, or every sediment deposit is permanent.

## 3. Weathering, erosion, transport, and deposition

These processes must be distinguished.

- **Weathering:** Physical, chemical, or biological alteration and breakdown of rock or mineral material in place. Weathering can weaken or change material without transporting it away.
- **Erosion:** Detachment and removal of soil, sediment, or rock material by agents such as flowing water, waves, wind, ice, or gravity. In some disciplinary contexts, erosion includes both detachment and removal; the exact usage should be stated.
- **Transport:** Movement of detached material by water, wind, ice, or gravity.
- **Deposition:** Settling or accumulation of transported material when the transporting conditions no longer keep it moving, or when the material encounters a suitable storage environment.

A rock can weather in place. Its fragments may later be detached by runoff, transported downslope, carried into a channel, deposited on a floodplain, and remobilized during a later flood.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Weathering supplies or alters material; erosion removes it; transport moves it; deposition stores it at a new location. These processes can interact, but none should be used as a synonym for all the others.

**LIMIT:** A visible sediment deposit does not, by itself, identify where the particles originated, how they traveled, or how long they will remain in place.

## 4. Sediment sources and detachment

Sediment can originate from:
- sheet and rill erosion on hillslopes;
- gully and channel erosion;
- streambank collapse and channel-bed scour;
- weathered bedrock and unconsolidated deposits;
- landslides and other mass movements;
- shoreline and floodplain erosion;
- human-disturbed surfaces, including construction and poorly protected agricultural land.

The mechanisms of detachment differ. Raindrop impact can dislodge particles. Overland flow can detach and carry material. Flowing streams can erode banks and beds. Gravity can move saturated or unstable soil downslope. Wind and ice can also transport sediment.

Particle resistance depends on more than particle size. Cohesion, cementation, root reinforcement, soil moisture, aggregation, packing, vegetation, and the forces exerted by the transporting agent all matter.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Sediment supply depends on both the availability of detachable material and the forces capable of detaching it. A high-energy flow cannot transport material that is not available to it, and abundant loose material does not move unless an effective transport force acts.

**LIMIT:** A measurement of sediment in a stream does not automatically identify the source area or erosion process. Source attribution requires additional field evidence, tracers, sediment characteristics, or appropriate models.

## 5. Vegetation, soil structure, and erosion resistance

Vegetation can influence erosion and sediment delivery through several distinct mechanisms:
- canopy interception reduces the direct impact of some raindrops;
- leaves, stems, litter, and ground cover reduce exposed soil;
- roots can reinforce soil and affect aggregate stability;
- organic matter and biological activity can influence soil structure;
- surface roughness can slow overland flow;
- root channels and soil organisms can alter infiltration pathways;
- vegetation along streambanks can influence bank resistance and channel conditions.

These effects depend on plant type, root depth and density, soil, slope, rainfall, disturbance, and season. Vegetation does not prevent every form of erosion. Under some conditions, concentrated flow, bank undercutting, mass movement, or extreme events can overcome local resistance.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Vegetation is one of several controls on erosion and sediment movement. It affects the pathways and forces involved rather than acting as a universal barrier.

**LIMIT:** The presence of vegetation alone does not establish that erosion is absent or that sediment delivery downstream is negligible.

## 6. Hillslope pathways: sheet flow, rills, gullies, and mass movement

Hillslope erosion occurs through multiple pathways.

- **Sheet erosion:** Relatively broad removal of thin layers of soil from a surface.
- **Rill erosion:** Concentrated shallow channels formed by flowing water; rills can be removed or altered by tillage and other surface disturbance.
- **Gully erosion:** Larger, more persistent channels that cannot ordinarily be removed by normal tillage.
- **Mass movement:** Downslope movement of soil, rock, or debris under gravity, including landslides and debris flows. Water can increase instability by changing pore-water pressure and material strength.

These categories are useful descriptions, but real landscapes can contain transitions and combinations. Rills can develop into gullies; gullies can connect hillslopes to streams; landslides can deliver large sediment pulses to channels.

**FINDING:** Sediment delivery depends on connectivity. Material detached on a hillslope may be stored behind vegetation, in depressions, on footslopes, or on floodplains rather than reaching a stream immediately.

**LIMIT:** Soil loss measured at a plot is not equivalent to sediment yield measured at a watershed outlet. The difference includes intervening storage, remobilization, and additional sediment sources.

## 7. Sediment transport in streams: suspended load and bedload

In flowing water, sediment is commonly transported in two broad physical modes.

- **Suspended-sediment load:** Particles carried within the water column by turbulent flow and other suspension processes. Fine sand, silt, and clay commonly contribute, although the exact size distribution varies with flow and conditions.
- **Bedload:** Particles moving close to or in contact with the streambed through rolling, sliding, and intermittent hopping or saltation.

The distinction is operational rather than an absolute permanent classification. A particle can move as bedload at one moment and become suspended under a different flow condition. Grain size alone does not determine the mode; particle density and shape, turbulence, water depth, velocity, and bed conditions also matter.

USGS sources distinguish the total sediment load as the combined suspended-sediment load and bedload, while noting that the measurement of each component has different practical limitations.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Suspended transport and bedload transport are different modes of movement within a stream. Their relative contributions vary with flow, sediment supply, particle properties, and channel conditions.

**LIMIT:** A water sample collected away from the streambed does not directly measure the full bedload. Suspended-sediment sampling and bedload estimation require different methods.

## 8. Dissolved load is not particulate sediment

Streams also transport dissolved material: ions and dissolved compounds carried in solution. This is commonly called dissolved load.

It must be distinguished from:
- suspended particles;
- particles moving as bedload;
- dissolved nutrients;
- particulate organic matter;
- living organisms transported in the water.

Chemical weathering and groundwater inputs can contribute dissolved substances. Sediment particles can carry adsorbed nutrients or contaminants, but this does not make particulate transport identical to dissolved transport.

**FINDING:** A stream transports material in multiple physical and chemical forms. The dissolved load and particulate sediment load require different measurements and interpretations.

**LIMIT:** Turbidity is not a direct measurement of all sediment or dissolved material. Turbidity can be related to suspended sediment through site-specific calibration, but particle size, color, shape, organic matter, and instrument conditions affect the relationship.

## 9. Flow energy, entrainment, and transport capacity

Flowing water exerts forces on the bed and on particles. Whether a particle begins to move depends on the balance between the forces exerted by the flow and the forces resisting movement.

Relevant controls include:
- flow depth and velocity;
- boundary shear stress;
- turbulence and short-lived fluctuations;
- channel slope and geometry;
- particle size, shape, and density;
- cohesion, armoring, and bed packing;
- availability of sediment.

The threshold for initiating motion is not necessarily identical to the condition needed to keep particles moving after transport begins. Particle impacts and local bed structure can influence entrainment and cessation.

**TRANSPORT CAPACITY** is a model-dependent description of how much sediment a flow can transport under specified conditions. It must be distinguished from actual sediment load, which also depends on the sediment supplied to the flow.

A stream can have capacity to transport more sediment than is available. It can also receive more sediment than it can carry under the prevailing conditions, leading to deposition.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Sediment transport reflects an interaction between flow forces, particle resistance, and sediment supply. Flow magnitude alone is not a complete predictor.

**LIMIT:** A simplified velocity threshold or particle-size chart cannot represent every channel because cohesion, turbulence, depth, bed structure, and sediment supply vary.

## 10. Deposition, temporary storage, and remobilization

Deposition occurs when particles settle or accumulate in a location where the transporting force is insufficient to keep them moving, or where flow and channel conditions favor storage.

Common storage locations include:
- channel beds and bars;
- inside bends and other lower-velocity channel zones;
- floodplains;
- wetlands;
- lakes and reservoirs;
- estuaries and coastal environments;
- depressions and footslopes on hillsides.

Coarser particles generally settle more rapidly than finer particles under comparable conditions, but particle shape, density, water viscosity, turbulence, and flocculation affect settling.

Deposition is not necessarily permanent. A later flood, channel migration, changing flow regime, or human disturbance may remobilize stored material.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Deposition transfers sediment from active transport into a storage location. That storage can be temporary, persistent, or eventually buried and consolidated, depending on subsequent conditions.

**LIMIT:** A deposit observed today does not establish permanent sediment removal from the watershed or long-term geological storage.

## 11. Channel form, floodplains, and sediment balance

Channels change through erosion and deposition. A reach may experience:
- **aggradation:** net accumulation of sediment and an increase in bed elevation;
- **degradation:** net removal of bed material and a decrease in bed elevation;
- **lateral migration:** movement of channel position through bank erosion and deposition;
- **scour and fill:** localized removal and subsequent accumulation of bed material.

The response depends on the balance among sediment supply, transport capacity, flow regime, channel geometry, bank resistance, vegetation, and boundary conditions.

Floodplains provide areas where water can spread and sediment can be deposited during overbank flows. They can store sediment and influence the timing and distribution of floodwater. They are not simply passive containers: vegetation, topography, soil, and channel connections affect how water and sediment move through them.

Reservoirs and dams can trap sediment and alter downstream sediment supply. A reduction in sediment supply can contribute to channel adjustment downstream, depending on flow and channel conditions.

**FINDING:** Channel form is related to water and sediment movement over time. A channel is not a fixed conduit whose geometry is independent of the material it transports.

**LIMIT:** A local change in channel bed elevation does not automatically establish the sediment balance of an entire watershed. Reach-scale and basin-scale measurements answer different questions.

## 12. Sediment concentration, sediment load, and sediment yield

These quantities must not be confused.

- **Sediment concentration** is the mass of sediment per volume of water at a sampling location and time.
- **Sediment load or sediment discharge** is the mass of sediment passing a defined cross-section per unit time.
- **Sediment yield** is the amount of sediment exported from a defined drainage area over a stated time interval, commonly expressed relative to area.

A high concentration at low streamflow may represent a different total mass transport than a lower concentration during very high streamflow.

A simplified suspended-sediment load calculation combines water discharge and suspended-sediment concentration, with appropriate unit conversion. The USGS technical literature describes this relationship and emphasizes that load estimation requires representative samples and discharge measurements.

Annual or event-scale sediment yield requires time coverage. A few isolated samples may miss major storm events, seasonal changes, or periods of unusually high transport.

**FINDING:** Concentration is not load, and load at a sampling station is not automatically a complete measure of watershed sediment yield.

**LIMIT:** Sampling frequency, cross-sectional variability, unsampled near-bed transport, rating-curve assumptions, and storm timing can affect estimates.

## 13. Sediment, nutrients, contaminants, and aquatic habitats

Sediment is part of natural watershed and aquatic processes. Excessive or altered sediment delivery can nevertheless affect ecosystems and infrastructure.

Potential effects, depending on amount, grain size, timing, chemistry, and receiving environment, include:
- increased turbidity and reduced light penetration;
- burial or abrasion of benthic habitat;
- changes in spawning substrate and egg survival;
- clogging of spaces among gravel and cobbles;
- transport of phosphorus, metals, pesticides, or other substances attached to particles;
- filling of pools, channels, reservoirs, or drainage structures;
- changes in channel depth, width, and flood conveyance.

Not all sediment is pollution. Streams and rivers naturally transport and deposit sediment, and many aquatic and floodplain habitats depend on sediment processes. The relevant question is whether sediment amount, timing, grain size, chemistry, or delivery rate has been altered beyond the conditions compatible with the particular ecosystem.

**FINDING:** Sediment quantity and character can connect watershed land use and erosion to downstream water quality, channel form, habitat, and infrastructure.

**LIMIT:** A turbidity increase alone does not identify the pollutant, source, biological effect, or ecological severity. Those require additional measurements and site-specific assessment.

## 14. Human alteration of sediment pathways

Human activities can change both sediment supply and the routes by which sediment moves.

Examples include:
- construction and exposed soil;
- agricultural tillage and inadequate ground cover;
- road cuts, ditches, and culverts;
- mining and quarrying;
- deforestation and vegetation removal;
- streambank modification and channelization;
- dams and reservoirs;
- stormwater drainage and impervious surfaces;
- wetland drainage and floodplain disconnection.

These changes do not all act in the same direction. A reservoir may trap sediment locally while changing downstream supply. A road ditch may connect a disturbed slope directly to a stream. A restored floodplain may increase opportunities for sediment storage. Effects depend on location, design, operation, and watershed context.

**FINDING:** Human actions can alter sediment sources, transport connectivity, and storage. The same rainfall event can produce different sediment responses in watersheds with different land cover and infrastructure.

**LIMIT:** A land-use category alone does not establish a sediment load. Site conditions, practices, storm history, and connectivity must be examined.
