## 1. First discriminating question

How do minerals, rocks, tectonic processes, water, temperature, organisms, and time interact to form, alter, transport, and recycle Earth's solid materials—and how do geological structures and material properties establish conditions for landscapes, soils, groundwater, and living systems?

The previous records followed water and sediment through watersheds. This record steps back to examine the solid materials and geological arrangements upon which those pathways operate.

**Central distinction:** A mineral is not a rock; a rock is not a landform; geological structure is not the same as surface shape; weathering is not erosion; parent material is not necessarily the bedrock directly beneath a soil; and soil formation is not simply the accumulation of broken rock.

These distinctions are essential because the same broad material can be transformed by different processes, and the same process can produce different results depending on composition, structure, environment, and time.

This record does not assume that every soil forms directly over its underlying bedrock, that the rock cycle follows one fixed sequence, or that all geological change occurs at the same rate.

## 3. Minerals and rocks: composition versus aggregate

A **mineral** is a naturally occurring solid with an ordered internal structure and a characteristic chemical composition or defined compositional range. Mineral identification uses properties such as hardness, cleavage, luster, streak, density, and crystal form. No single visible property, especially color, is sufficient for reliable identification in every case.

A **rock** is a naturally occurring solid aggregate or mass of mineral material. Many rocks contain several minerals; some rocks are composed predominantly of one mineral or mineral group. Rocks may also contain glass, organic material, or other components, depending on how they formed.

Examples:
- Granite commonly contains quartz, feldspar, and mica or other minerals.
- Basalt commonly contains plagioclase and pyroxene, with mineral composition varying by type.
- Limestone commonly consists largely of calcite, although its composition and texture vary.
- Quartzite is composed predominantly of quartz and forms through metamorphism of quartz-rich sandstone.

The distinction matters because a rock's properties are not simply the name or property of one mineral. Mineral proportions, grain size, cement, pores, fractures, texture, and fabric influence the rock's behavior.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Mineral composition and rock texture jointly help determine the physical and chemical properties of geological material.

**LIMIT:** A rock name does not provide a complete chemical analysis or establish every property of a particular specimen. Field identification may be provisional and require laboratory methods.

## 4. The three major rock groups

Geologists commonly classify rocks by their principal formation processes.

### 4.1 Igneous rocks

Igneous rocks form when molten rock cools and solidifies.

- **Magma** is molten rock below the Earth's surface.
- **Lava** is molten rock at the surface.
- **Intrusive (plutonic) igneous rocks** cool underground, often slowly enough for larger crystals to develop.
- **Extrusive (volcanic) igneous rocks** cool at or near the surface, often more rapidly and commonly with finer crystals or glassy textures.

Cooling rate, chemical composition, pressure, water content, and crystallization history influence the resulting minerals and texture.

**LIMIT:** Not every igneous rock has the same mineral composition or grain size. The broad intrusive/extrusive distinction does not replace petrological classification.

### 4.2 Sedimentary rocks

Sedimentary rocks form through several pathways, including:
- accumulation and lithification of mineral or rock fragments;
- precipitation of minerals from solution;
- accumulation and transformation of biological material.

For clastic sedimentary rocks, a common sequence is weathering and erosion, transport, deposition, burial, compaction, and cementation. The details vary with sediment type and depositional environment.

**Lithification** is the conversion of loose sediment into sedimentary rock, commonly through compaction and cementation. It is not the same process as crystallization from magma.

Chemical and biochemical sedimentary rocks may form through mineral precipitation or biological processes rather than by simple compaction of fragments.

**LIMIT:** Not every sedimentary rock follows the same clastic pathway. Limestone, evaporites, coal, and other sedimentary materials have distinct formation histories.

### 4.3 Metamorphic rocks

Metamorphic rocks form when pre-existing rocks are transformed by changes in temperature, pressure, stress, and chemically active fluids, without the rock undergoing wholesale melting as the defining process.

Metamorphism may change:
- mineral assemblages;
- crystal size and texture;
- mineral alignment and foliation;
- grain boundaries and fabric;
- chemical distribution through fluid-assisted reactions.

Examples include limestone transformed to marble and quartz-rich sandstone transformed to quartzite.

**LIMIT:** Metamorphism occurs across a range of conditions. Not every metamorphic rock is foliated, and not every mineral change requires the same pressure-temperature path.

## 5. The rock cycle: connected pathways, not a compulsory circle

The **rock cycle** is a conceptual model for the formation and transformation of Earth's rock materials.

Possible pathways include:
- magma cooling and crystallizing into igneous rock;
- rock weathering and erosion producing sediment;
- sediment deposition, burial, compaction, and cementation producing sedimentary rock;
- burial, heating, pressure, deformation, and fluid interaction producing metamorphic rock;
- melting producing magma from pre-existing rock;
- uplift and exposure making rocks available to surface weathering;
- mineral dissolution and precipitation creating new mineral material.

A rock can follow different routes. An igneous rock may weather into sediment and become sedimentary rock; it may be buried and metamorphosed; it may melt and later crystallize again. A sedimentary rock may be uplifted and eroded, metamorphosed, or melted under suitable conditions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The rock cycle expresses connected transformations among geological materials over time. It is not a single sequence through which every rock must pass in the same order.

**LIMIT:** A simplified cycle diagram is not a complete geological history. It does not by itself establish the age, source, pressure-temperature history, or sequence of events for a particular rock body.

## 6. Geological structure: arrangement, deformation, and discontinuity

Geological structure concerns the arrangement, orientation, continuity, and deformation of rocks and geological materials.

Important structural features include:

- **Bedding:** Layering commonly formed during sediment deposition.
- **Folding:** Bending or curvature of originally planar or layered rock units under deformation.
- **Faulting:** Displacement of rock blocks along a fracture or fracture zone.
- **Joints and fractures:** Discontinuities in rock; joints generally refer to fractures without appreciable displacement along the fracture surface.
- **Foliation:** Planar fabric or mineral alignment developed in many metamorphic rocks.
- **Unconformities:** Surfaces representing gaps in the geological record, commonly involving erosion or non-deposition.
- **Intrusions:** Bodies of igneous material that entered pre-existing rock.

Structure influences how rocks respond to stress, how groundwater may move through fractures, how slopes may fail, and how weathering and erosion expose different units.

A fracture may provide a pathway for water, but it may also be mineral-filled or poorly connected. A fault may act as a conduit in one part of a system and a barrier in another, depending on its materials, geometry, and surrounding rock.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Geological structure affects the distribution, mechanical behavior, and fluid pathways of rock bodies.

**LIMIT:** A line on a geological map is a representation of an interpreted structure, not the structure itself. Subsurface geometry and hydraulic behavior may require field, geophysical, borehole, and other evidence.

## 7. Plate tectonics and the large-scale arrangement of rock

Plate tectonics describes the relative movement of large, rigid lithospheric plates over geological time. Their interactions produce characteristic geological settings.

- **Divergent boundaries:** Plates move apart; new crust is generated at spreading centers.
- **Convergent boundaries:** Plates move toward one another; subduction, collision, mountain building, volcanism, and deformation may occur depending on the boundary.
- **Transform boundaries:** Plates move laterally relative to one another along faults.
- **Plate-boundary zones:** Some broad regions distribute deformation across multiple structures rather than one narrow boundary.

Tectonic processes can create, deform, uplift, bury, fracture, and expose rock bodies. They influence mountain belts, basins, volcanic regions, earthquake-producing faults, and the long-term arrangement of geological materials.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Plate interactions are major controls on crustal formation, deformation, and the geological settings in which rocks are created and transformed.

**LIMIT:** Plate tectonics does not mean every local landform or fracture is directly attributable to a plate boundary. Local geology, erosion, sedimentation, climate, and other processes also shape landscapes.

## 8. Uplift, exposure, and the distinction between tectonic and surface processes

**Uplift** refers to an increase in the elevation of rock or land relative to a reference level. Tectonic processes can cause uplift, but changes in surface elevation and exhumation can also reflect erosion, isostatic adjustment, sediment loading or removal, and other processes.

**Exhumation** is the bringing of rocks toward the surface from deeper conditions, commonly through removal of overlying material and tectonic processes.

**Erosion** removes material from the surface. It can expose previously buried rock, but erosion is not itself identical to tectonic uplift.

The distinction matters because a mountain range's present elevation, the depth at which its rocks formed, and the time those rocks became exposed are different questions.

**FINDING:** Tectonic processes, uplift, exhumation, weathering, and erosion interact but must be independently described.

**LIMIT:** Present-day topography alone cannot reconstruct a complete tectonic or erosional history. Geological mapping, dating, structural analysis, and other evidence are needed.

## 9. Weathering: physical, chemical, and biological contributions

**Weathering** is the breakdown or alteration of rock and minerals at or near Earth's surface. It can occur in place and does not necessarily transport the resulting material away.

### 9.1 Physical weathering

Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without requiring a change in the chemical identity of the constituent minerals.

Mechanisms include:
- freeze-thaw action;
- heating and cooling;
- wetting and drying;
- salt crystal growth;
- pressure release and unloading;
- abrasion by moving particles;
- root growth and other biological physical effects.

Physical breakdown can increase exposed surface area, potentially increasing the opportunity for chemical reactions.

### 9.2 Chemical weathering

Chemical weathering changes minerals through reactions with water, dissolved gases, acids, and other chemical agents.

Important processes include:
- dissolution;
- hydrolysis;
- oxidation and reduction;
- hydration;
- carbonation and reactions involving carbonic acid.

For example, carbonate minerals such as calcite can dissolve in water containing dissolved carbon dioxide and acidity. Feldspar minerals can undergo hydrolysis and contribute to the formation of clay minerals and dissolved constituents.

Chemical weathering can release ions to water, form secondary minerals, and change the strength and porosity of rock.

### 9.3 Biological influence

Organisms can affect weathering through:
- root penetration and physical disruption;
- organic acids and other chemical compounds;
- microbial reactions that alter mineral chemistry;
- organic matter that influences water retention and chemical conditions.

Biological effects are not a separate universal mechanism operating independently of physical and chemical conditions. They interact with them.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Weathering is a set of interacting physical, chemical, and biological processes that alter geological material.

**LIMIT:** Weathering rates depend on mineral composition, surface area, temperature, water, acidity, organisms, fractures, and exposure. No single rate applies to all rocks or environments.

## 10. Parent material: soil need not form from the bedrock directly beneath it

**Parent material** is the mineral or organic material in which soil development begins. It may be:
- weathered material derived from local bedrock;
- sediment deposited by rivers;
- windblown dust or sand;
- glacial deposits;
- volcanic ash;
- slope deposits moved by gravity;
- organic material accumulated in wetlands or other settings;
- mixtures of materials from multiple sources.

A soil may therefore develop in transported material that differs from the bedrock beneath it.

The distinction between **bedrock** and **parent material** is essential. Bedrock may influence a soil, but it is not automatically the immediate source of every particle in the soil profile.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Soil parent material has multiple origins and may be transported before soil-forming processes develop a soil profile.

**LIMIT:** The origin of a soil material cannot always be identified by appearance alone. Field relationships, stratigraphy, mineralogy, geochemistry, and landscape context may be required.

## 11. The five soil-forming factors

Soil development reflects the interaction of five commonly recognized factors:

1. **Parent material:** The initial mineral or organic material and its physical and chemical properties.
2. **Climate:** Temperature, precipitation, moisture regime, and other climatic conditions that influence weathering, leaching, and biological activity.
3. **Organisms:** Plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, other microorganisms, and humans that affect mixing, organic matter, pores, chemistry, and structure.
4. **Topography or relief:** Slope, aspect, landscape position, drainage, erosion, deposition, and water-table conditions.
5. **Time:** The duration over which soil-forming processes act and interact.

These factors are not five isolated causes that can be added without interaction. They influence one another. A change in drainage can alter biological activity and chemical weathering; vegetation can change organic inputs and soil structure; slope can influence erosion and soil depth.

A useful conceptual expression in soil science is that soil is a function of parent material, climate, organisms, relief, and time. It is a framework for recognizing interacting controls, not a universal numerical equation that predicts every soil property without data.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Soil properties and horizons reflect the interaction of multiple soil-forming factors over time.

**LIMIT:** Time alone does not determine soil development. A young soil under intense weathering may develop differently from an older soil under cold, dry, or erosive conditions. Soil development is also affected by disturbance, burial, deposition, and human activity.

## 12. Soil horizons and profile development

A soil profile may display horizons that differ in color, texture, structure, mineral composition, organic matter, and other properties.

Common master-horizon designations include:
- **O:** Organic layers dominated by organic material.
- **A:** Mineral surface horizon commonly enriched with organic matter.
- **E:** Eluvial horizon in which removal of clay, iron, aluminum, or organic matter has occurred where such a horizon develops.
- **B:** Subsurface horizon showing accumulation or transformation of materials and other forms of soil development.
- **C:** Material relatively little affected by soil-forming processes.
- **R:** Consolidated bedrock.

Not every soil has every horizon, and the sequence, thickness, and properties vary. A soil profile is not required to display a complete textbook sequence.

Soil formation includes additions, losses, transformations, and translocations:
- organic matter and mineral material may be added;
- water and erosion may remove material;
- minerals may be transformed by weathering;
- clay, dissolved substances, and organic compounds may move within the profile.

**FINDING:** A soil profile records the combined effects of parent material, climate, organisms, relief, water movement, and time.

**LIMIT:** Horizon labels classify observed soil features; they do not independently identify the entire history or exact age of a soil.

## 13. The critical zone: connected rock, soil, water, air, and life

The **critical zone** is commonly described as the near-surface environment extending from the top of vegetation and soil down through weathered material toward unaltered rock and the interfaces with groundwater.

It brings together:
- geological material and mineral reactions;
- soil formation and structure;
- water infiltration and subsurface flow;
- atmospheric exchange;
- roots and biological communities;
- weathering, erosion, and sediment movement.

The critical zone is not a single material or a single layer of soil. It is an interacting region in which water, gases, minerals, organisms, and energy influence one another.

Weathering reactions depend on water flow paths, mineral surfaces, residence times, temperature, and biological activity. Physical processes can expose fresh mineral surfaces; chemical processes can alter minerals; biological processes can affect both.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The critical zone provides a cross-disciplinary framework for examining interactions among geology, soil, hydrology, geochemistry, and ecology.

**LIMIT:** The term is a scientific organizing concept. It does not mean all critical-zone processes have been measured or fully understood at a given site.

## 14. Geological composition and water chemistry

The mineral composition of rocks and soils influences the chemical constituents that can enter water.

Examples:
- dissolution of calcite can contribute calcium and bicarbonate to water;
- weathering of feldspars can release elements such as potassium, sodium, calcium, and dissolved silica, depending on mineral composition and reaction conditions;
- oxidation of sulfide minerals such as pyrite can generate acidity and mobilize metals under suitable conditions;
- carbonate minerals can neutralize acidity through dissolution reactions;
- secondary minerals such as iron oxides and clay minerals can retain or release elements through adsorption, precipitation, and other reactions.

The actual chemistry of groundwater and surface water depends on the mineral assemblage, water composition, flow paths, residence time, redox conditions, temperature, and biological activity.

**FINDING:** Geological material is one source of the inorganic constituents of water. Water chemistry can therefore carry evidence of water-rock interaction.

**LIMIT:** A dissolved element in water does not uniquely identify its source rock. Multiple sources and reactions may produce similar chemical signatures; source attribution requires a broader geochemical investigation.
