## 1. First discriminating question

How do geological materials and structures determine where groundwater is stored, how it moves, how quickly it can be transmitted, and how aquifers connect with soils, springs, rivers, wetlands, and human water systems?

Record 0038 examined mineral composition, geological structure, weathering, and soil parent material. This record investigates water beneath the land surface and the geological conditions that permit its storage and movement.

**Central distinction:** The presence of pore space does not by itself establish that water can move through a material at a useful rate. Storage, connectivity, transmission, hydraulic driving force, and actual flow are related but distinct.

This record also distinguishes the geological capacity to store or transmit water from the amount of water available for sustainable human withdrawal. An aquifer is not an unlimited reservoir.

## 3. What is groundwater?

Groundwater is water present beneath Earth's land surface within saturated soil, sediment, rock, and other geological materials.

It occupies openings such as:
- spaces between sediment grains;
- pores within some rocks;
- fractures and joints;
- dissolution openings in soluble rocks;
- interconnected openings in weathered material.

The groundwater system includes more than water itself. It includes the solid geological framework, openings in that framework, water occupying those openings, and the boundaries and processes that govern inflow, outflow, and storage.

The saturated zone is the region in which connected pore spaces are filled with water, subject to local conditions and capillary effects. The unsaturated zone above the water table can also contain water, but its pores are not completely saturated.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Groundwater occurs within geological materials and their openings. Its movement and storage depend on the properties and connectivity of those openings, the water's physical properties, and hydraulic conditions.

**LIMIT:** Not all subsurface water is readily recoverable, and not all saturated material is a productive aquifer.

## 4. Porosity: how much open space exists?

Porosity is the fraction of a material's total volume occupied by void space.

A conceptual expression is:

`n = V_v / V_t`

Where:
- `n` is porosity;
- `V_v` is the volume of void space;
- `V_t` is total material volume.

Porosity is commonly expressed as a fraction or percentage.

A material can have substantial pore space without transmitting water efficiently. The size, shape, distribution, and connection of pores matter.

### 4.1 Primary porosity

Primary porosity develops as the material forms.

Examples:
- spaces between sand grains in unconsolidated sediment;
- intergranular pores in sandstone;
- vesicles formed by gas bubbles in volcanic rock.

### 4.2 Secondary porosity

Secondary porosity develops after the material forms.

Examples:
- fractures and joints;
- cracks created by stress or unloading;
- dissolution openings in limestone and other soluble rocks;
- enlarged openings produced by weathering or other alteration.

Primary and secondary porosity can occur together. A sandstone can contain intergranular pores and fractures. A limestone can contain small pores, fractures, and enlarged solution channels.

### 4.3 Total porosity and effective porosity

Not all pore space contributes equally to groundwater flow.

Some pores are isolated or poorly connected. Some hold water that moves extremely slowly or is not readily released under ordinary hydraulic conditions.

Effective porosity concerns the interconnected pore space available for fluid transmission. Its definition and measurement depend on the application and model.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Total porosity describes void volume; effective porosity addresses the connected void space relevant to flow.

**LIMIT:** A high measured porosity does not, by itself, establish high groundwater yield or rapid flow.

## 5. Permeability: whether connected openings transmit fluid

Permeability describes the ability of a porous material to transmit fluid through connected openings. In its intrinsic sense, it is a property of the medium and its pore geometry, not simply a measure of how much empty space exists.

The connection among pore size, shape, tortuosity, and continuity matters.

For example:
- well-connected sand and gravel can transmit water readily;
- clay can have considerable total porosity but very low permeability because its pores are extremely small and flow pathways are restricted;
- a dense, unfractured crystalline rock can transmit little water;
- the same general rock type may transmit more water where fractures are open and connected;
- limestone may transmit water through fractures and dissolution-enlarged conduits.

The distinction is essential:

**Porosity is not permeability.**

A sponge-like material may hold considerable water but release it slowly. A fractured rock may have relatively little total void space but transmit water rapidly through a small number of connected openings.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Water storage capacity and water-transmission capacity are different material properties.

**LIMIT:** A general rock name cannot establish permeability. Measurements and geological context are necessary.

## 6. Hydraulic conductivity: the material–fluid relationship

Hydraulic conductivity describes how readily a saturated porous material transmits water under a hydraulic gradient. It depends on the medium's intrinsic permeability and on fluid properties, including density and viscosity.

A conceptual relationship is:

`K = k ρ g / μ`

Where:
- `K` is hydraulic conductivity;
- `k` is intrinsic permeability;
- `ρ` is fluid density;
- `g` is gravitational acceleration;
- `μ` is dynamic viscosity.

The expression assumes the conventional conditions and definitions used in Darcy-flow descriptions. Detailed calculations and unit conventions belong to the relevant hydrogeological work.

Hydraulic conductivity is commonly expressed in units of length per time. Intrinsic permeability has units of area.

The distinction matters because two fluids moving through the same geological medium can have different hydraulic conductivities if their physical properties differ.

Hydraulic conductivity can also vary within one formation. This heterogeneity may occur because grain size, cementation, fractures, layering, and mineral alteration vary spatially.

It may also vary with direction. Such directional dependence is called anisotropy.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Hydraulic conductivity is a combined property of the geological medium and the fluid, and it is not identical to intrinsic permeability.

**LIMIT:** A single laboratory sample may not represent the effective hydraulic behavior of a large, heterogeneous aquifer.

## 7. Hydraulic head and the driving force for groundwater movement

Groundwater flow is driven by differences in hydraulic head.

Hydraulic head represents the mechanical energy per unit weight of water, commonly expressed as an equivalent water elevation. It includes elevation and pressure contributions, with velocity head generally negligible in ordinary groundwater applications.

Groundwater tends to move from higher hydraulic head toward lower hydraulic head, subject to the geometry and hydraulic properties of the system.

The hydraulic gradient is the change in hydraulic head over a distance in a specified direction.

A sloping land surface does not necessarily determine the direction of groundwater flow. Groundwater flow is controlled by the hydraulic-head distribution, not simply by the visible slope of the ground.

Hydraulic head is estimated from water levels in appropriately constructed and measured wells. In a confined aquifer, the water level in a well can rise above the top of the aquifer because of pressure.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Groundwater flow direction is governed by hydraulic-head gradients and the hydraulic properties of the connected geological materials.

**LIMIT:** A surface topographic map alone cannot establish subsurface groundwater-flow direction. Water-level measurements at appropriate depths are needed.

## 8. Darcy's law: a relationship between flow, material, and gradient

Darcy's law describes flow through porous media under conditions where the law is applicable.

In one common form:

`Q = -K A (dh/dl)`

Where:
- `Q` is volumetric flow rate;
- `K` is hydraulic conductivity;
- `A` is cross-sectional area;
- `dh/dl` is hydraulic-head change per unit distance;
- the negative sign indicates flow toward decreasing head.

This relation shows that flow depends on more than the existence of water or pore space. It depends on hydraulic conductivity, the cross-sectional area available for flow, and the hydraulic gradient.

Darcy's law is foundational in groundwater hydraulics. Its use requires attention to assumptions, scale, flow regime, and whether the medium can be treated as a continuum.

In highly heterogeneous or conduit-dominated systems, such as some karst aquifers, a simple porous-medium representation may not adequately capture all flow behavior.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Groundwater flow is related to hydraulic conductivity and hydraulic gradient, with flow also depending on the geometry and cross-sectional area of the system.

**LIMIT:** Darcy's law is not a universal substitute for site characterization. Its assumptions and applicability must be assessed for the system being modeled.

## 9. Aquifers, aquitards, and confining units

An **aquifer** is a saturated geological formation or body of material capable of transmitting usable quantities of groundwater to wells or springs.

An **aquitard** is a relatively low-hydraulic-conductivity unit that restricts groundwater movement between or within aquifers. It may transmit water slowly.

An **aquiclude** is sometimes used for a unit considered to transmit negligible water in a practical context. The term should not imply that absolute impermeability has been demonstrated.

A **confining unit** is a geological body that restricts flow into or out of an adjacent aquifer.

The important distinction is functional: an aquifer can transmit useful quantities of water, while a confining unit restricts transmission relative to the aquifer.

A unit's role can depend on scale and context. A layer that acts as a confining unit in one setting may transmit water in another, especially where it is fractured or discontinuous.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Aquifers and confining units are distinguished by their hydraulic behavior in a particular groundwater system.

**LIMIT:** Geological labels alone do not establish the actual hydraulic connection between units. Measurements and subsurface characterization are required.

## 10. Unconfined and confined aquifers

### 10.1 Unconfined aquifers

An unconfined aquifer has the water table as its upper saturated boundary. Water can recharge it from above through infiltration and percolation, subject to soil, sediment, and geological conditions.

The water table can rise or fall with changes in recharge, discharge, pumping, and other conditions.

### 10.2 Confined aquifers

A confined aquifer is overlain by a confining unit that restricts vertical groundwater movement. Water within it is under pressure relative to atmospheric pressure at the aquifer's elevation.

When a well penetrates a confined aquifer, water rises in the well to the level corresponding to the hydraulic head. If that level is above the land surface, the well may flow without pumping.

An artesian condition refers to water under sufficient pressure to rise in a well above the top of the confined aquifer. A flowing artesian well occurs when the potentiometric level is above the land surface.

### 10.3 Potentiometric surface

The potentiometric surface is the surface representing hydraulic head in a confined aquifer, commonly inferred from water levels in wells open to that aquifer.

It is not necessarily the physical top of the aquifer or a surface where groundwater is actually present.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Unconfined and confined aquifers differ in their upper hydraulic boundary and pressure conditions.

**LIMIT:** A well's water level cannot be interpreted correctly without knowing which aquifer or interval the well actually measures.

## 11. Recharge, discharge, and groundwater connection with surface water

**Recharge** is the addition of water to a groundwater system. It can occur through infiltration and percolation from precipitation, irrigation, streams, lakes, or other sources, depending on the geological and hydrological setting.

**Discharge** is water leaving a groundwater system. It can occur through:
- springs and seeps;
- groundwater flow into rivers and streams;
- discharge to lakes, wetlands, and coastal waters;
- evapotranspiration where groundwater is accessible to roots;
- pumping from wells.

Groundwater and surface water are often connected parts of one hydrological system. A stream can gain water from groundwater, lose water to groundwater, or do both along different reaches or at different times.

A gaining stream receives groundwater discharge. A losing stream contributes water to the subsurface.

The direction and magnitude of exchange depend on hydraulic-head relationships, streambed properties, geology, and changing hydrological conditions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Groundwater and surface water can exchange water, and the direction of exchange is controlled by hydraulic conditions and the properties of the interface.

**LIMIT:** A stream's appearance or flow at one moment does not establish its groundwater relationship throughout the year or along its entire channel.

## 12. Fractured-rock aquifers

In many crystalline rocks, primary pore space is limited. Groundwater may therefore move mainly through secondary openings such as fractures, joints, and faults.

The presence of a fracture does not guarantee significant flow. Relevant properties include:
- aperture or opening width;
- length and continuity;
- connectivity with other fractures;
- orientation relative to hydraulic gradients;
- mineral filling or sealing;
- stress conditions and whether fractures remain open;
- connection to recharge and discharge boundaries.

A rock mass may contain many fractures that are poorly connected. Another may contain fewer but more continuous fractures that transmit substantial water.

Fracture networks can be strongly anisotropic: groundwater may move more readily in some directions than others.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** In fractured-rock aquifers, the connectivity and hydraulic properties of fractures can be more important for flow than the total number of visible fractures.

**LIMIT:** Surface fracture observations do not necessarily represent fracture connectivity at depth. Borehole imaging, pumping tests, tracer studies, and other investigations may be needed.

## 13. Karst aquifers and dissolution pathways

Karst develops where soluble rocks, commonly limestone and dolomite, are dissolved by water over time.

Dissolution can enlarge fractures and bedding-plane openings into conduits, caves, and interconnected channels.

Karst groundwater systems can contain several flow domains:
- small pores and fractures;
- enlarged fractures and solution openings;
- large conduits and caves.

These domains can behave very differently. Water may move slowly through smaller openings but rapidly through connected conduits.

Karst aquifers can be highly productive, but they can also be especially vulnerable to contamination because water and contaminants may move rapidly through openings with limited filtration or attenuation.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Dissolution can create secondary porosity and highly connected flow pathways in soluble rock.

**LIMIT:** Not every limestone is a productive karst aquifer, and the presence of caves does not alone establish the groundwater flow paths or vulnerability of an entire region.

## 14. Groundwater storage, yield, and sustainability

Groundwater storage and groundwater yield are not the same thing.

A formation may contain a large volume of water but transmit it too slowly to supply a well at a desired rate. Conversely, a highly transmissive formation may supply water readily while having limited storage or being strongly connected to a surface-water source.

Important concepts include:
- **Specific yield:** the fraction of a saturated material's bulk volume that drains under gravity, in the relevant context.
- **Specific storage:** water released from or taken into storage per unit volume of aquifer per unit change in hydraulic head.
- **Storativity:** the volume of water released from storage per unit area of aquifer per unit decline in hydraulic head.
- **Transmissivity:** the capacity of the full saturated thickness of an aquifer to transmit water horizontally, commonly related to hydraulic conductivity and saturated thickness.

These terms are not interchangeable. Their definitions and measurement depend on aquifer conditions.

Pumping changes hydraulic heads and can create a cone of depression around a well. If pumping exceeds replenishment and connected inflows over a relevant period, groundwater levels can decline. Effects can include:
- reduced well yields;
- drying of shallow wells;
- reduced groundwater discharge to streams and wetlands;
- land subsidence in susceptible formations;
- movement of saline or contaminated water into freshwater zones.

Sustainable groundwater management requires understanding recharge, storage, discharge, water quality, ecological connections, and the time scale of use.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The presence of groundwater does not establish that a particular withdrawal rate is sustainable.

**LIMIT:** Recharge estimates and pumping impacts are uncertain and vary with climate, geology, land use, aquifer boundaries, and management. A simple annual recharge number may not capture local ecological or long-term effects.

## 15. Groundwater age and residence time

Groundwater age describes the time since water entered the subsurface at recharge, as estimated using appropriate environmental tracers and models.

Groundwater can move slowly. Travel times may range from days or years to centuries or millennia, depending on the system and flow path.

A sample from a well may contain water of mixed ages. Therefore, the measured or inferred age of a sample is not necessarily the age of every water molecule entering the well.

Groundwater age can help investigate:
- recharge history;
- flow paths;
- mixing;
- vulnerability to recent contamination;
- the time scale over which groundwater responds to land-use or climate changes.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Groundwater age provides information about recharge and subsurface transport history, but it is inferred through tracer evidence and models.

**LIMIT:** Groundwater age is not simply the depth of a well, and one age value should not be treated as a complete description of a mixed groundwater system.

## 16. Groundwater quality and geological interaction

Groundwater chemistry reflects interactions among recharge water, geological materials, residence time, redox conditions, biological activity, and human inputs.

Relevant processes include:
- mineral dissolution;
- ion exchange;
- oxidation and reduction reactions;
- precipitation of secondary minerals;
- adsorption and desorption;
- mixing of waters from different sources;
- contamination from surface or subsurface activities.

Geological materials can contribute dissolved calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate, silica, iron, manganese, and other constituents. Their presence and concentration depend on the mineral assemblage and chemical conditions.

Contaminants may enter groundwater from agriculture, septic systems, industrial activities, mining, leaking storage, road salts, and other sources. Their movement depends on the contaminant's chemistry and the hydrogeological pathway.

Groundwater is not automatically clean because it is underground. Some geological settings provide filtration and attenuation, while others allow rapid contaminant transport.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Groundwater quality is produced by both natural water–rock interactions and external inputs, mediated by the properties and flow paths of the groundwater system.

**LIMIT:** A clear-looking water sample is not proof of potability. Chemical and microbiological testing are required for drinking-water decisions.

## 17. Groundwater systems as connected geological and hydrological domains

A groundwater system can be investigated through its:
- three-dimensional geometry;
- geological boundaries;
- saturated and unsaturated zones;
- aquifers and confining units;
- hydraulic properties;
- recharge and discharge areas;
- flow directions and rates;
- connections with streams, lakes, wetlands, and coastal waters;
- water chemistry and temperature;
- human withdrawals and other stresses.

This system description joins several disciplines without making them interchangeable.

**Geology** examines the materials, structures, and histories that establish the framework.

**Hydrology** examines water occurrence, movement, storage, and exchange.

**Hydrogeology** investigates the interaction of groundwater with geological materials and structures.

**Geochemistry** examines water–rock reactions and dissolved constituents.

**Ecology** examines how groundwater discharge, water chemistry, and water availability influence organisms and habitats.

**Engineering and public health** examine wells, water supply, contamination, treatment, and safe use.

The unity is not a claim that these disciplines use identical methods. It is a recognition that they investigate connected aspects of a shared physical system.
