## 1. First discriminating question

How do bedrock, weathered material, soil horizons, groundwater, surface water, atmospheric exchange, roots, microbes, and landforms interact across Earth's near-surface environment—and how do these connected processes influence water availability, nutrient cycling, carbon movement, ecosystem function, and landscape change?

The previous records examined soil formation, geological structure, and groundwater. This investigation considers the larger environment in which those domains interact.

**Central distinction:** The Critical Zone is not simply another name for soil. It is a scientific framework for investigating the interconnected near-surface environment in which rock, soil, water, air, and living organisms interact.

The Critical Zone's exact lower boundary is not universally fixed. Depending on the question and landscape, researchers may define it by the base of active weathering, the transition to relatively fresh bedrock, or the depth of actively circulating groundwater. These boundaries must be stated rather than silently treated as identical.

## 3. Defining the Critical Zone

The Critical Zone is commonly described as Earth's heterogeneous, near-surface environment in which rock, soil, water, air, and living organisms interact to regulate natural habitats and influence the availability of life-sustaining resources.

Its components can include:
- vegetation canopy and organisms above the ground;
- the land surface and surface waters;
- soil and organic horizons;
- weathered rock and regolith;
- the unsaturated or vadose zone;
- saturated groundwater;
- the transition toward relatively unweathered bedrock.

The Critical Zone is not a single material, organism, or layer. It is a framework for studying interacting materials and processes across a near-surface environment.

### 3.1 Its boundaries are research-dependent

The upper boundary is often represented by the top of the vegetation canopy, while the lower boundary varies with the scientific definition and the system being studied.

A study focused on root water uptake may use a different effective depth from one examining deep chemical weathering or groundwater circulation.

The boundary is therefore not always a sharp physical line. Weathering, fractures, water movement, and biological activity can change gradually with depth.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The Critical Zone is an interconnected near-surface system, not merely the soil profile.

**LIMIT:** There is no single universally applicable depth that defines its lower boundary for every landscape and research question.

## 4. Bedrock, regolith, saprolite, and soil

These terms must remain distinct.

### 4.1 Bedrock

Bedrock is consolidated rock underlying soil, sediment, or other surficial material. It may be relatively fresh or may be weathered and fractured.

### 4.2 Regolith

Regolith is the layer of loose or weathered material above relatively unweathered bedrock. Depending on context, it can include soil, sediment, saprolite, and weathered rock.

### 4.3 Saprolite

Saprolite is deeply weathered rock that has been chemically altered and physically weakened while retaining recognizable features or fabric of its original parent rock.

It is not simply another word for soil. It can retain the structure of the original rock even though its minerals and physical properties have changed.

### 4.4 Soil

Soil is a biologically and physically active natural body containing mineral particles, organic matter, water, air, and organisms. It develops through interacting processes including additions, losses, transformations, and transfers.

Soil is part of the Critical Zone, but the Critical Zone extends beyond soil.

### 4.5 Why the distinctions matter

A subsurface profile may include:
- an organic-rich surface horizon;
- mineral soil horizons;
- weathered parent material;
- saprolite;
- fractured and weathered bedrock;
- relatively fresh bedrock.

These units may transition gradually, and not every landscape contains all of them in a simple, uniform sequence.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Soil, regolith, saprolite, and bedrock refer to distinguishable materials or material domains, although their boundaries can be gradual.

**LIMIT:** A generic soil-profile diagram cannot be assumed to describe every geological setting.

## 5. Weathering, erosion, and landscape formation

Weathering alters rock and minerals in place. Erosion removes and transports material. They interact, but they are not identical.

### 5.1 Physical weathering

Physical weathering breaks or weakens rock without requiring a change in mineral composition.

Examples include:
- freeze–thaw action;
- thermal expansion and contraction;
- unloading and stress-related fracturing;
- abrasion;
- root growth in fractures.

### 5.2 Chemical weathering

Chemical weathering changes minerals through reactions with water, dissolved substances, gases, and organic compounds.

Examples include:
- dissolution;
- hydrolysis;
- oxidation and reduction;
- hydration;
- precipitation of secondary minerals.

### 5.3 Biological influence

Roots, fungi, bacteria, lichens, and other organisms can affect weathering through physical penetration, organic compounds, changes in local chemistry, and nutrient acquisition.

Biological activity does not replace physical and chemical mechanisms; it can interact with and modify them.

### 5.4 Erosion and denudation

Erosion transports soil and rock particles by water, wind, ice, and gravity. Denudation is a broader term for the lowering of the land surface through weathering and removal of material.

Weathering can produce material faster or slower than it is removed. The resulting regolith thickness depends on the balance among production, transport, and landscape history.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The Critical Zone evolves through coupled weathering, material transport, biological activity, and landscape processes.

**LIMIT:** A weathering rate measured at one site cannot be generalized to every climate, rock type, or landscape.

## 6. Water movement through the Critical Zone

Water connects the atmosphere, surface, soil, weathered rock, and groundwater.

Relevant processes include:
- precipitation;
- interception by vegetation;
- infiltration into the ground;
- redistribution through soil;
- evaporation;
- plant transpiration;
- percolation through the vadose zone;
- groundwater recharge;
- subsurface flow;
- discharge to springs, streams, lakes, and wetlands;
- runoff across the land surface.

### 6.1 Infiltration is not the same as recharge

Infiltration is water entering the soil surface.

Recharge is water reaching and adding to a groundwater system.

Water that infiltrates may be stored in soil, taken up by roots, evaporated, or moved laterally before it reaches the water table.

### 6.2 Soil water is not automatically groundwater

Soil moisture occupies the unsaturated soil environment. Groundwater is associated with saturated subsurface materials.

The two are connected through infiltration, percolation, capillary processes, and changing water-table conditions, but they should not be treated as identical.

### 6.3 Water storage and water flux

Water storage describes the amount present within a defined volume or system at a given time.

Water flux describes movement across a defined area or boundary over time.

A large water store does not necessarily imply a large rate of movement. A rapid flux does not necessarily imply a large long-term store.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Water links Critical Zone compartments through transfers and storage changes, but each process and water domain retains its own definition.

**LIMIT:** Rainfall alone does not determine groundwater recharge. Soil properties, vegetation, geology, topography, antecedent moisture, and climate all matter.

## 7. Roots and vegetation: biological access to water and minerals

Plants connect the atmosphere, soil, and subsurface through roots, leaves, stems, and biological processes.

Roots:
- acquire water and dissolved nutrients;
- alter soil structure;
- create channels and openings;
- release compounds into the surrounding soil;
- interact with fungi and microorganisms;
- influence soil stability and erosion;
- contribute organic matter through growth and decay.

Plants return water to the atmosphere through transpiration. This movement is part of evapotranspiration, which combines evaporation from surfaces and transpiration from vegetation.

Root depth and distribution vary by species, soil, climate, water availability, and landscape conditions. Some roots can enter fractures and access water below the ordinary soil profile.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Vegetation affects water movement, soil structure, nutrient cycling, and weathering, while depending on the same environmental conditions it can modify.

**LIMIT:** Root presence does not establish a particular groundwater connection. Root depth and water source require evidence.

## 8. Microorganisms and the transformation of materials

Microorganisms are active participants in soil and subsurface environments.

Bacteria, archaea, fungi, and other organisms can:
- decompose organic matter;
- transform nitrogen compounds;
- participate in carbon cycling;
- alter oxidation–reduction conditions;
- mobilize or immobilize nutrients;
- influence mineral dissolution and precipitation;
- form associations with plant roots;
- affect the availability and movement of elements.

Microbial activity depends on environmental conditions including moisture, temperature, oxygen availability, pH, nutrient supply, and the availability of suitable substrates.

Microbial processes can occur below the visible soil surface, including in weathered rock and groundwater environments, though the abundance and activity vary substantially.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Microorganisms mediate important chemical transformations within the Critical Zone and connect biological processes with mineral and water chemistry.

**LIMIT:** Detecting microbial genetic material does not by itself establish active metabolism or the rate of a particular chemical transformation.

## 9. Nutrient stocks, nutrient fluxes, and availability

A nutrient stock is the amount of a nutrient held within a defined compartment at a given time.

A nutrient flux is the amount transferred across a boundary per unit time.

Nutrients can be:
- held in minerals;
- dissolved in soil water;
- adsorbed to mineral surfaces;
- incorporated into organic matter;
- held in living biomass;
- transported in runoff or groundwater;
- lost through erosion, leaching, or gaseous pathways.

Total elemental abundance is not identical to biological availability. The chemical form, location, pH, redox state, mineral association, and biological activity affect whether an element can be accessed by organisms.

Weathering can release elements from minerals. Organisms can take them up and later return them through decomposition. Water can transport dissolved or particulate material between compartments.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Nutrient cycling involves transfers and transformations among geological, aqueous, and biological compartments.

**LIMIT:** The total quantity of an element in soil or rock does not establish how much is biologically available.

## 10. Carbon cycling across the Critical Zone

Carbon occurs in multiple forms and compartments:
- atmospheric carbon dioxide;
- plant biomass;
- roots and soil organic matter;
- dissolved organic carbon;
- dissolved inorganic carbon;
- carbonate minerals;
- groundwater and surface-water carbon;
- sedimentary and geological carbon.

Plants take up atmospheric carbon dioxide through photosynthesis and incorporate carbon into organic compounds. Respiration and decomposition return carbon to the atmosphere or transfer it into soil and water.

Water transports dissolved and particulate carbon. Chemical weathering can consume carbon dioxide in some reactions and move dissolved products toward streams and oceans. The long-term carbon consequences depend on the minerals, reaction pathways, and eventual fate of the transported carbon.

Not every weathering reaction has the same carbon effect. Carbonate and silicate weathering have different chemical pathways and long-term implications.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The Critical Zone participates in carbon exchange among atmosphere, vegetation, soil, water, and geological materials.

**LIMIT:** It is not scientifically sound to claim that all weathering removes atmospheric carbon dioxide permanently. Reaction type, timescale, and downstream processes matter.

## 11. Energy and the Critical Zone

Critical Zone processes are also connected by energy transfers.

Relevant energy sources and influences include:
- solar radiation;
- gravitational potential;
- thermal conditions;
- chemical energy;
- biological metabolism;
- tectonic and geological processes.

Solar energy drives much of the surface water cycle and photosynthesis. Gravity influences runoff and groundwater flow. Chemical energy supports microbial metabolism. Earth's internal processes influence tectonics, uplift, and the creation of geological conditions that shape landscapes over long periods.

Energy availability influences rates of physical, chemical, and biological processes, but does not by itself determine their outcomes. Material properties, constraints, and environmental conditions also matter.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The Critical Zone is a domain of coupled material and energy transfers.

**LIMIT:** Energy source, energy transfer, material transformation, and biological function are distinct concepts and should not be used interchangeably.

## 12. Interfaces and feedbacks

The Critical Zone is especially useful for examining interfaces where one domain affects another.

Examples include:

- **Atmosphere–vegetation:** radiation, carbon dioxide, water vapor, and transpiration.
- **Vegetation–soil:** roots, organic matter, nutrient uptake, and soil structure.
- **Soil–water:** infiltration, storage, drainage, and dissolved transport.
- **Soil–rock:** weathering, mineral supply, and soil development.
- **Groundwater–surface water:** discharge, recharge, and ecological exchange.
- **Microbe–mineral:** mineral alteration and chemical transformations.
- **Landform–water:** runoff pathways, erosion, deposition, and residence time.

A feedback occurs when a change in one part of a system affects another part, which in turn influences the first or a connected part.

For example, vegetation can influence infiltration and erosion. Changes in soil moisture can affect vegetation. Vegetation changes can then alter interception, transpiration, organic inputs, and erosion. The direction and strength of these effects depend on the site and conditions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The Critical Zone contains coupled interfaces and feedbacks among physical, chemical, and biological processes.

**LIMIT:** A feedback diagram is a conceptual representation. It does not quantify the strength, direction, or timescale of a feedback without measurements or a validated model.

## 13. Why Critical Zone observatories matter

A Critical Zone observatory studies connected processes using measurements across multiple compartments and depths.

Depending on the research question, investigations may include:
- geological mapping and boreholes;
- soil profiles and mineral analysis;
- water-level monitoring;
- precipitation and streamflow measurements;
- water chemistry;
- geophysical imaging;
- vegetation and root observations;
- microbial sampling;
- carbon and nutrient measurements;
- erosion and sediment transport;
- meteorological observations.

The value of co-located measurements is that they can reveal relationships that would be difficult to establish from isolated observations.

For example, a change in stream chemistry may be investigated alongside precipitation, soil-water chemistry, groundwater levels, bedrock composition, and biological activity.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Coordinated observations across domains can test connections among water, rock, soil, atmosphere, and organisms.

**LIMIT:** Co-location and correlation alone do not establish causation. Experimental design, temporal coverage, process understanding, and alternative explanations remain necessary.
