How do carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur move among atmosphere, water, rocks, soils, sediments, and living organisms—and how do biological uptake, decomposition, mineral weathering, chemical transformation, transport, burial, and geological processes connect these cycles without making their mechanisms interchangeable?

## 3. What is a biogeochemical cycle?

A biogeochemical cycle describes the movement and transformation of chemical elements among living organisms and nonliving parts of Earth.

The term combines three domains:
- **Bio:** living organisms and biological processes.
- **Geo:** rocks, minerals, soil, sediments, and geological processes.
- **Chemical:** elemental forms, compounds, reactions, and transformations.

Major reservoirs include:
- atmosphere;
- oceans and other surface waters;
- groundwater;
- soils and sediments;
- vegetation and animals;
- microorganisms;
- rocks and the deeper geological environment.

A reservoir is a defined store of an element or chemical form. A pathway is a route by which material moves or is transformed. A flux is the amount transferred across a defined boundary per unit time.

A stock is an amount present in a defined reservoir at a particular time. A concentration is an amount relative to a defined volume or mass. A flux is a transfer rate. These quantities must not be confused.

### 3.1 Matter and energy

Chemical elements are conserved in ordinary chemical and biological transformations, although their compounds and locations change. Elements can also enter or leave a defined system through its boundaries.

Energy, by contrast, moves through ecosystems and is transformed; it is not recycled in the same way matter is.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Biogeochemical cycles connect biological, geological, hydrological, and chemical processes through elemental transfers and transformations.

**LIMIT:** A cycle diagram is a simplified representation. It does not show every species, reaction, reservoir, pathway, or timescale.

## 4. Carbon cycle

Carbon is present in atmospheric carbon dioxide and methane, dissolved forms in water, organic matter, living biomass, soils, sediments, carbonate minerals, and fossil carbon.

The carbon cycle includes comparatively rapid biological exchanges and much slower geological processes.

### 4.1 Rapid carbon exchanges

Photosynthetic organisms take up carbon dioxide and incorporate carbon into organic compounds.

Carbon moves through food webs as organisms consume other organisms or organic material.

Respiration by plants, animals, and microorganisms returns carbon dioxide to the environment. Decomposition transforms dead organic matter and can release carbon dioxide or, under suitable oxygen-limited conditions, methane.

Carbon also exchanges between atmosphere and ocean through dissolution and release, influenced by temperature, circulation, chemistry, and biological activity.

### 4.2 Soil and terrestrial carbon

Carbon enters soils through plant litter, roots, dead organisms, and other organic inputs.

Decomposition transforms organic material. Some carbon is returned to the atmosphere; some becomes incorporated into soil organic matter; some is transported in dissolved or particulate form.

Soil carbon storage varies with climate, vegetation, soil properties, disturbance, drainage, and management.

### 4.3 Slow geological carbon cycle

Chemical weathering can consume carbon dioxide through reactions involving water and minerals. Dissolved products can be transported by rivers to the ocean.

Carbon can be stored in carbonate minerals and buried organic matter. Geological processes, including tectonic uplift, subduction, and volcanism, participate in long-term carbon movement.

The slow carbon cycle operates on geological timescales, substantially longer than most ecological and human planning intervals.

### 4.4 Carbon sources and sinks

A source transfers more carbon into a specified reservoir than it removes over the defined interval. A sink removes more carbon from that reservoir than it adds over the same interval.

These terms are relative to a defined reservoir, boundary, and timescale. A system may be a sink over one interval and a source over another.

Human activities—including fossil-fuel combustion, cement production, and land-use change—alter carbon transfers and atmospheric concentrations.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Carbon cycles through biological, atmospheric, oceanic, soil, and geological reservoirs, with distinct rapid and slow pathways.

**LIMIT:** Photosynthetic uptake does not by itself establish permanent carbon storage. Storage duration, decomposition, disturbance, transport, and burial determine longer-term outcomes.

## 5. Nitrogen cycle

Nitrogen is essential to proteins, nucleic acids, and other biological molecules. The atmosphere contains a large reservoir of molecular nitrogen (N₂), but most organisms cannot directly use atmospheric N₂.

Nitrogen must undergo transformations into biologically accessible forms.

### 5.1 Nitrogen fixation

Nitrogen fixation converts molecular nitrogen into reduced nitrogen compounds, commonly ammonia/ammonium, through biological or abiotic pathways.

Biological fixation is carried out by specialized microorganisms, including free-living organisms and organisms associated with plants. Some cyanobacteria fix nitrogen in aquatic environments.

Industrial fixation also converts atmospheric nitrogen into reactive nitrogen compounds, notably for fertilizer production.

Lightning and combustion can produce reactive nitrogen compounds that enter atmospheric and terrestrial pathways.

### 5.2 Assimilation

Plants and microorganisms take up available inorganic nitrogen and incorporate it into organic molecules. Animals obtain nitrogen through food.

Assimilation moves nitrogen into biomass; it does not create the element.

### 5.3 Mineralization and ammonification

Decomposers transform organic nitrogen in dead material and waste into inorganic forms, commonly ammonium.

This process returns nitrogen from organic matter to the soil or water environment.

### 5.4 Nitrification

Under suitable oxygen conditions, microorganisms oxidize ammonium to nitrite and then nitrate.

The stages involve different microbial groups and chemical reactions. Nitrification is not simply the movement of nitrogen into a plant.

### 5.5 Denitrification

In oxygen-limited or reducing environments, microorganisms can use nitrate in respiratory pathways and convert it through intermediate forms toward gaseous products, including molecular nitrogen and nitrous oxide.

Denitrification can return nitrogen to the atmosphere and can also produce nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas.

### 5.6 Other pathways

Anammox (anaerobic ammonium oxidation) combines ammonium and nitrite to produce nitrogen gas under anoxic conditions.

Dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium is another pathway by which nitrate can be transformed into ammonium rather than gaseous nitrogen.

Volatilization can transfer ammonia from soil or water to the atmosphere. Leaching and runoff can transport dissolved nitrogen into groundwater, streams, lakes, and oceans.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Nitrogen cycling depends heavily on microbial transformations among molecular nitrogen, ammonium, nitrite, nitrate, and organic nitrogen.

**LIMIT:** The nitrogen cycle is not one fixed sequence. Different pathways dominate under different oxygen, moisture, pH, substrate, and microbial conditions.

## 6. Phosphorus cycle

Phosphorus is essential for nucleic acids, phospholipids, ATP and other biological molecules. Unlike carbon and nitrogen, phosphorus has no major atmospheric gas reservoir under ordinary environmental conditions.

Its large reservoirs occur in rocks, minerals, soils, sediments, and water, as well as living organisms.

### 6.1 Geological release

Weathering of phosphorus-bearing minerals, including apatite, releases phosphate into soils and waters.

The release rate depends on mineral composition, weathering conditions, acidity, water movement, and biological interactions.

### 6.2 Biological uptake and return

Plants and microorganisms take up phosphate and incorporate phosphorus into biological material. Animals obtain phosphorus through food.

Decomposition and mineralization return phosphorus from organic matter to inorganic forms.

### 6.3 Retention and transport

Phosphate can bind to mineral surfaces, associate with iron and aluminum compounds, precipitate into minerals, or move with water and eroded particles.

Its mobility depends on soil and water chemistry, mineralogy, pH, redox conditions, and hydrological pathways.

### 6.4 Sediment and geological storage

Phosphorus can be transported into aquatic systems, incorporated into biomass or particles, deposited in sediments, and eventually incorporated into sedimentary rocks.

Geological uplift and weathering can return some phosphorus-bearing material to terrestrial environments over long timescales.

### 6.5 Human alteration

Mining phosphate rock and using phosphate fertilizers redistribute phosphorus from geological stores into agricultural and aquatic systems.

Excess phosphorus entering lakes and other waters can contribute to eutrophication, harmful algal growth, oxygen depletion, and ecological change.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Phosphorus cycling is strongly connected to mineral weathering, soil chemistry, biological uptake, erosion, aquatic transport, and sedimentary storage.

**LIMIT:** Phosphorus is not universally immobile, nor does every soil retain it in the same way. Its mobility depends on local chemical and geological conditions.

## 7. Sulfur cycle

Sulfur occurs in rocks and minerals, ocean water, soils, sediments, organisms, and atmospheric gases and aerosols.

It is important in amino acids and proteins and participates in biological and geochemical transformations.

### 7.1 Geological and aqueous sources

Weathering of sulfur-bearing minerals can release sulfur compounds. Sulfate is abundant in seawater and can also occur in terrestrial waters and soils.

Volcanic activity can release sulfur gases. Human activities, particularly fossil-fuel combustion and some industrial processes, can also release sulfur compounds into the atmosphere.

### 7.2 Sulfur oxidation

Reduced sulfur compounds, including hydrogen sulfide, can be oxidized to elemental sulfur or sulfate through chemical and biological pathways.

Some microorganisms obtain energy through sulfur oxidation.

### 7.3 Sulfate reduction

In oxygen-limited environments, sulfate-reducing microorganisms can use sulfate in anaerobic metabolism and produce reduced sulfur compounds, including hydrogen sulfide.

This process can occur in sediments, wetlands, and other reducing environments.

### 7.4 Biological assimilation and decomposition

Organisms incorporate sulfur into organic compounds. Decomposition returns sulfur to inorganic or reduced forms, depending on environmental conditions and microbial pathways.

### 7.5 Atmospheric and aquatic pathways

Sulfur compounds can move through the atmosphere, undergo chemical transformation, and return to land and water through wet or dry deposition.

Sulfur dioxide emissions can contribute to atmospheric acidity and acid deposition after chemical transformation.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Sulfur cycling links mineral weathering, atmospheric chemistry, aquatic and sedimentary processes, and microbial oxidation–reduction pathways.

**LIMIT:** Sulfur transformations are strongly dependent on chemical form and environmental conditions. Sulfur dioxide, sulfate, elemental sulfur, sulfide, and organic sulfur are not interchangeable.

## 9. Water as a connecting transport medium

Water links the cycles by transporting dissolved and particulate materials among soil, groundwater, streams, lakes, oceans, and organisms.

Examples include:
- dissolved inorganic carbon moving through groundwater and rivers;
- nitrate leaching from soil into groundwater;
- phosphate transported with eroded sediment;
- sulfate moving in groundwater and surface water;
- dissolved organic matter transported from soil to streams.

Water also participates in chemical reactions, including hydrolysis and dissolution.

However, water transport does not determine the chemical fate of every element. Retention, adsorption, precipitation, biological uptake, redox reactions, and other processes can alter or interrupt a pathway.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The hydrological cycle provides transport pathways that connect multiple biogeochemical cycles.

**LIMIT:** A shared transport medium does not make the cycles identical. Each element retains its own chemical transformations and controls.

## 10. Microorganisms as chemical transformers

Microorganisms are important participants in carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus cycling.

They can:
- decompose organic matter;
- fix nitrogen;
- oxidize ammonium;
- reduce nitrate;
- oxidize or reduce sulfur compounds;
- mineralize organic phosphorus;
- alter local chemical conditions;
- influence mineral dissolution and precipitation.

Microbial activity is sensitive to oxygen availability, moisture, temperature, pH, substrate supply, and community composition.

The same soil or sediment may contain adjacent microenvironments with different oxygen conditions. Consequently, different transformations can occur close together or in sequence as water and chemical conditions change.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Microbial metabolism mediates many elemental transformations and links chemical conditions with biological activity.

**LIMIT:** The presence of a microbial species or gene does not alone prove that a pathway is active at a particular rate in the field.

## 11. Stocks, fluxes, residence times, and budgets

These concepts are essential to interpreting cycle diagrams and measurements.

- **Stock:** amount of an element in a defined reservoir at a specified time.
- **Concentration:** amount relative to a specified mass or volume.
- **Flux:** amount transferred across a defined boundary per unit time.
- **Residence time:** a characteristic estimate of how long material remains in a reservoir, given the chosen model and conditions.
- **Budget:** accounting of inputs, outputs, and changes in storage over a defined boundary and interval.

A reservoir can have a large stock and a small flux. Another can have a small stock and a rapid turnover.

A simple mass-balance expression is:

`Change in storage = Inputs - Outputs`

This is a conceptual accounting relationship. In a real system, all important inputs, outputs, internal transfers, transformations, and measurement uncertainties must be considered.

Internal transformations can change chemical form without changing the total amount of the element inside the chosen boundary. Transfers across the boundary change the reservoir's stock.

Residence time is not necessarily the age of every atom or molecule in a mixed reservoir. It is a model-dependent characterization of turnover.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Elemental budgets require explicit reservoirs, boundaries, units, time intervals, and accounting of inputs and outputs.

**LIMIT:** Global cycle diagrams and local ecosystem budgets cannot be compared responsibly unless their boundaries, units, and reference periods are compatible.

## 12. Human alteration of biogeochemical cycles

Human activities alter the rates and pathways of elemental transfers.

Examples include:
- fossil-fuel combustion and cement production changing carbon transfers;
- deforestation and land-use change altering carbon storage and fluxes;
- fertilizer production and use increasing reactive nitrogen and phosphorus inputs;
- livestock and waste systems redistributing nitrogen and phosphorus;
- mining and fertilizer use redistributing phosphorus from geological reservoirs;
- industrial processes and fossil-fuel combustion releasing sulfur compounds;
- drainage, irrigation, and groundwater pumping changing water-mediated transport;
- erosion and sediment disturbance redistributing nutrients and organic matter.

Potential consequences include:
- eutrophication;
- oxygen depletion in aquatic systems;
- nitrate contamination of groundwater;
- altered soil nutrient balances;
- changes in atmospheric greenhouse gases;
- acid deposition;
- altered ecosystem productivity and species composition.

The effects depend on chemical form, location, quantity, timing, transport pathways, and the receiving environment.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Human activities can substantially change elemental inputs, outputs, chemical forms, and transport rates.

**LIMIT:** A global description does not determine the effect at a particular farm, watershed, aquifer, lake, or ecosystem. Local evidence is needed.
