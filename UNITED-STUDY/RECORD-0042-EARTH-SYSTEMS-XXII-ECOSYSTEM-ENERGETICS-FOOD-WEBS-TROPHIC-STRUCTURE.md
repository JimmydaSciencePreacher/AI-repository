## 1. First discriminating question

How does energy enter ecosystems, become captured and transformed by primary producers, pass through consumers and decomposers, and ultimately dissipate as heat—while matter is transferred among organisms and environmental reservoirs?

Record 0041 examined the movement and transformation of elements through biogeochemical cycles. This record investigates how organisms obtain energy, how energy moves through trophic relationships, and how those relationships connect to the movement and recycling of matter.

**Central distinction:** Energy flow and matter cycling are connected, but they are not the same process. Energy moves through ecosystems and is transformed, with usable energy dissipated as heat. Chemical elements are transferred among organisms and environmental reservoirs and can be reused in biological and geological processes.

This study does not treat energy as destroyed, nor does it imply that matter is recycled without inputs, outputs, or boundary conditions.

## 3. Ecosystem and its components

An ecosystem includes a biological community and the physical and chemical environment with which it interacts.

Its components can be considered in two broad groups:

- **Biotic components:** primary producers, consumers, decomposers, detritivores, and other organisms.
- **Abiotic components:** light, temperature, water, air, minerals, soil, sediments, and other physical or chemical conditions.

These categories are useful for investigation, but organisms and their environments are not independent compartments. Organisms modify environmental conditions, and environmental conditions affect organism activity, survival, and distribution.

Ecosystems also exchange matter and energy with surrounding environments. A stream receives leaf litter from its watershed; a forest receives atmospheric deposition; a coastal ecosystem may receive nutrients and organic matter from rivers. A system boundary must therefore be stated before a complete budget can be constructed.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Ecosystems are interacting biological and physical-chemical systems, often open to exchanges across their boundaries.

**LIMIT:** An ecosystem boundary is often a research choice. It must not be assumed that every material or energy pathway remains within the selected site.

## 4. Energy entering ecosystems

### 4.1 Photosynthetic energy capture

In most ecosystems, sunlight is the principal external energy source.

Photoautotrophs—including plants, algae, and photosynthetic bacteria—capture light energy and use photosynthesis to build organic compounds from inorganic carbon sources. The captured energy becomes associated with chemical bonds in organic matter and can support biological work and growth.

Photosynthesis does not create energy. It converts incoming radiant energy into chemical energy through a series of biochemical processes.

### 4.2 Chemosynthetic energy capture

Some ecosystems are not primarily supported by sunlight reaching their producers.

Certain bacteria and archaea use energy from chemical reactions involving inorganic substances to support carbon fixation and organic matter production. Such chemoautotrophic production is important in environments including some hydrothermal-vent systems and other chemically distinctive habitats.

This does not mean every organism at a hydrothermal vent is a chemosynthetic producer. Consumers may feed on chemoautotrophs or on organisms supported by their production.

### 4.3 Autotroph and heterotroph

An **autotroph** obtains carbon for biosynthesis from inorganic carbon sources, using an external energy source such as light or chemical energy.

A **heterotroph** obtains organic carbon by consuming or absorbing organic material.

These terms describe carbon acquisition, not a simple distinction between organisms that need energy and those that do not. All organisms require energy to maintain and operate their biological systems.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Primary production introduces energy into most food webs as organic matter through photosynthesis; in some ecosystems, chemical energy supports primary production instead.

**LIMIT:** Primary producers do not make energy from nothing. They transform energy from an external source into forms that can be used in biological processes.

## 5. Primary productivity

Productivity is a rate, not simply an amount of biomass present.

### 5.1 Gross primary productivity

**Gross primary productivity (GPP)** is the total rate at which primary producers capture energy or fix carbon through primary production, expressed using a defined measurement convention.

### 5.2 Net primary productivity

Primary producers use some of the captured energy in their own respiration.

**Net primary productivity (NPP)** is the rate of production remaining after autotrophic respiration is accounted for:

`NPP = GPP - R_a`

where `R_a` is autotrophic respiration.

NPP represents the rate at which producers accumulate organic matter available for growth, reproduction, consumers, and detrital pathways, subject to the ecosystem's actual transfers and losses.

### 5.3 Units and measurement

Productivity may be expressed as:
- mass of carbon per unit area per unit time;
- dry biomass per unit area per unit time;
- energy per unit area per unit time.

The units must be stated. A biomass stock measured on one date is not a productivity rate.

### 5.4 Primary production is not identical to standing biomass

A fast-growing phytoplankton community can have a small standing biomass at any one moment while supporting substantial production over time. A forest can hold a large standing biomass while its annual production rate is a different quantity.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** GPP and NPP describe rates of primary production, while standing biomass describes an amount present at a specified time.

**LIMIT:** NPP is not automatically equal to the amount eaten by herbivores. Some production is not consumed directly and enters detrital pathways, remains stored, or is transported outside the selected system.

## 6. Trophic levels and consumers

A trophic level describes an organism's feeding position in a specified food chain or food web.

Common categories include:

1. **Primary producers:** organisms that generate organic matter from inorganic carbon using light or chemical energy.
2. **Primary consumers:** organisms that feed on primary producers.
3. **Secondary consumers:** organisms that feed on primary consumers.
4. **Tertiary and higher-level consumers:** organisms that feed on consumers at lower trophic positions.

These categories are useful summaries, not permanent labels for every species. Omnivores may feed at more than one trophic level. A species can occupy different trophic positions depending on its diet, life stage, season, or ecosystem.

### 6.1 Consumption is not complete assimilation

When an organism consumes food, not all of the material is digested or absorbed.

Some material is not ingested. Some is ingested but not assimilated and leaves as waste. Some assimilated energy is used in respiration and other biological processes. Some is incorporated into new biomass.

Therefore, the amount consumed is not the same as the amount converted into consumer production.

### 6.2 Secondary production

Secondary production is the rate at which heterotrophic organisms produce new biomass from assimilated resources.

It depends on food availability and quality, ingestion, assimilation, respiration, excretion, growth, reproduction, and other processes.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Trophic position describes feeding relationships, and consumer production depends on how ingested and assimilated resources are allocated.

**LIMIT:** A species should not be assigned one universal trophic level without specifying the food web, diet, life stage, and context.

## 7. Food chains and food webs

### 7.1 Food chain

A food chain is a simplified linear representation of feeding relationships.

For example:

`Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk`

The arrows point from the food resource toward the organism that consumes it. In this example, the arrow indicates the direction of material and energy transfer through consumption.

A food chain is useful for isolating a pathway, but it does not represent all the feeding relationships in a real ecosystem.

### 7.2 Food web

A food web represents multiple interconnected feeding relationships.

An organism may consume multiple species and may itself be consumed by multiple predators. Omnivory and shared resources connect pathways across trophic positions.

Food webs therefore represent ecosystem feeding relationships more realistically than a single linear chain, although every food web remains a model that may omit species, interactions, seasonal changes, or external subsidies.

### 7.3 Trophic position is relational

The trophic position of an organism depends on what it eats and the trophic positions of its food sources. It is not simply a measure of size, strength, dominance, or moral status.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Food webs describe interconnected feeding relationships through which organic matter and energy are transferred.

**LIMIT:** A diagram cannot be assumed to contain every interaction. Its species, links, spatial scale, and time period must be documented.

## 8. Grazing and detrital food webs

Two broad pathways are often distinguished.

### 8.1 Grazing food web

A grazing pathway begins with living primary producers and proceeds through organisms that consume living plant, algal, or microbial material.

Example:

`Living grass → Herbivorous insect → Insect-eating bird → Predator`

### 8.2 Detrital food web

A detrital pathway begins with dead organic material, including fallen leaves, dead roots, carcasses, and organic waste.

Detritus supports organisms that consume, fragment, or chemically transform it. These include:
- detritivores, which ingest dead organic material;
- decomposer microorganisms, including many bacteria and fungi;
- predators that consume detritivores or decomposer-associated organisms.

Example:

`Fallen leaf → Fungi and bacteria → Detritivore or microbial consumer → Predator`

The actual pathway is more complex than this simplified chain. Microbial colonization changes the quality and composition of detritus, and detrital material can be transported, fragmented, leached, or consumed by multiple organisms.

### 8.3 The two pathways interact

The grazing and detrital pathways are not separate ecosystems. Living production supplies detritus through leaf fall, root turnover, death, and waste. Detrital processing releases or transforms nutrients that may become available to primary producers. Detritivores and microbial consumers can themselves become food for organisms in grazing pathways.

A large share of primary production in many ecosystems does not pass directly through herbivores. It enters detrital pathways instead. The relative importance varies among ecosystems.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Grazing and detrital food webs are interacting pathways, both important to ecosystem structure and function.

**LIMIT:** It is incorrect to treat decomposers as a final, isolated box outside the food web. Decomposers and detritivores participate in trophic relationships and nutrient transformations.

## 9. Decomposition and nutrient return

Decomposition is the breakdown and transformation of dead organic matter and waste.

It involves interacting physical, chemical, and biological processes, including:

- **Fragmentation:** breaking larger material into smaller pieces through physical processes and detritivore activity.
- **Leaching:** dissolving and transporting soluble substances in water.
- **Microbial transformation:** enzymatic breakdown and metabolism by bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms.
- **Mineralization:** conversion of organic nutrients into inorganic forms.
- **Immobilization:** incorporation of inorganic nutrients into microbial or other biological biomass.

Decomposition can release carbon dioxide and, under oxygen-limited conditions, other gaseous products. It also transforms and redistributes nutrients.

The rate and products of decomposition depend on temperature, moisture, oxygen availability, substrate chemistry, nutrient content, organism communities, and physical conditions.

### 9.1 Decomposition is not the same as energy recycling

Decomposer organisms obtain energy and nutrients from organic material. They use some of the chemical energy in metabolism, and energy is ultimately dissipated as heat.

Chemical elements in the decomposed material can return to inorganic reservoirs or become incorporated into decomposer biomass and other organisms.

Thus, decomposition participates in nutrient cycling while energy continues to be transferred and dissipated.

### 9.2 Detritus is not inert

Dead organic material is a resource and habitat. It can host microorganisms, provide food for detritivores, retain nutrients, and influence the timing and location of material transfers.

Its composition changes during decomposition. Its role depends on the surrounding environment and biological community.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Decomposition links dead organic matter to microbial and detritivore food webs and to the return and redistribution of nutrients.

**LIMIT:** Decomposition does not mean that all organic material is immediately converted into inorganic nutrients. Transformation can be partial, selective, and prolonged; some material may be transported or stored.

## 10. Energy transfer and ecological efficiency

At each trophic transfer, only part of the energy available in one trophic position becomes production at the next.

Energy may:
- remain uneaten;
- be consumed but not assimilated;
- leave as waste;
- be used in respiration and biological work;
- be dissipated as heat;
- become incorporated into new biomass.

A common measure is trophic-level transfer efficiency:

`TLTE = (Production at trophic level n / Production at trophic level n-1) × 100%`

The production terms must be defined consistently, using compatible area, time, and energy or biomass units.

Other efficiencies answer different questions:
- **Consumption efficiency:** proportion of production at one level that is consumed by the next.
- **Assimilation efficiency:** proportion of ingested material that is assimilated.
- **Net production efficiency:** proportion of assimilated energy converted into new consumer production.

These efficiencies must not be treated as interchangeable.

### 10.1 The “ten percent rule” is not a law

A frequently repeated teaching approximation says that roughly ten percent of energy at one trophic level becomes production at the next. It is a rough generalization, not a universal fixed percentage.

Actual transfer efficiencies vary with organism type, food quality, temperature, ecosystem conditions, and the definitions and measurements used.

### 10.2 Why food chains have limited length

As energy is transferred through trophic levels, less usable energy generally remains available for additional production at higher levels. This constrains the number and abundance of organisms that can be supported at higher trophic positions.

It does not establish one universal maximum number of trophic levels. Food-web structure varies across ecosystems.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Trophic transfer is incomplete, and energy available for production generally decreases through successive transfers.

**LIMIT:** No single transfer percentage or maximum food-chain length applies to every ecosystem.

## 11. Ecological pyramids

Ecologists use ecological pyramids to represent trophic structure.

### 11.1 Pyramid of energy

An energy pyramid represents energy production or transfer across trophic levels for a defined area and time.

Because energy is dissipated at each transfer and cannot be recycled as usable biological energy through trophic levels, an energy pyramid is upright when measured consistently.

### 11.2 Pyramid of biomass

A biomass pyramid represents the standing biomass at each trophic level at a specified time or over a defined sampling interval.

It can be upright or inverted. In aquatic ecosystems, producers such as phytoplankton can have low standing biomass but high turnover and production, supporting a greater standing biomass of consumers.

### 11.3 Pyramid of numbers

A numbers pyramid represents the number of organisms at each trophic level. It may be upright, inverted, or irregular, depending on organism size and population structure.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Energy, biomass, and numbers pyramids represent different quantities and can have different shapes.

**LIMIT:** A biomass pyramid must not be interpreted as an energy pyramid. A snapshot of standing biomass does not by itself establish productivity or energy transfer rate.

## 12. Energy conservation and dissipation

Energy is conserved; it is not created or destroyed in ordinary ecosystem processes.

However, energy changes form and becomes less available to perform biological work as it is transferred and transformed. Organisms use chemical energy to maintain gradients, synthesize molecules, move, grow, reproduce, and perform other functions. Much of the energy involved in these processes ultimately dissipates as heat to the surroundings.

The ecosystem must receive continuing energy input to maintain biological activity and production.

The correct distinction is:

- **Energy conservation:** the total energy is not destroyed.
- **Energy transformation:** energy changes form.
- **Energy dissipation:** energy spreads into forms and distributions less available to perform useful work.
- **Matter transfer and transformation:** atoms and compounds move among organisms and environmental reservoirs and may be reused.

An ecosystem does not recycle energy in the same sense that it cycles chemical elements.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Ecosystems require energy input, and energy transfers ultimately dissipate usable energy as heat while matter can be transferred and reused.

**LIMIT:** Saying energy is “lost” at a trophic level is shorthand for loss from the measured biological production pathway, not destruction of energy.

## 13. Connections to Record 0041 — Biogeochemical cycles

Record 0041 examined carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur reservoirs and transformations.

The present study connects those cycles to ecosystem energy and feeding relationships.

- Carbon in organic matter carries chemical energy available to consumers and decomposers.
- Nitrogen and phosphorus availability can constrain biological production in some environments.
- Sulfur compounds can support chemosynthetic production in certain environments.
- Decomposition transforms organic matter and returns or redistributes nutrient elements.
- Nutrients can be reused in primary production, while energy dissipates through biological transformations.
- Water and sediment transport can move organic matter and nutrients across ecosystem boundaries.

**Critical distinction:** Nutrient availability and energy availability are related but not interchangeable. An organism may have abundant light but be limited by nutrients, or have available nutrients but lack an adequate energy source or suitable environmental conditions.

## 14. Connections to Records 0035–0040

### Record 0035 — Soil ecology and belowground networks

Roots, fungi, bacteria, detritivores, and other soil organisms participate in belowground food webs and decomposition.

**Connection:** Belowground trophic interactions connect plant production, organic matter decomposition, nutrient transformations, and consumer relationships.

**Boundary:** Not every soil organism is a decomposer, and not every belowground interaction is captured by a simple soil food-web diagram.

### Record 0036 — Water in the living Earth

Water transports dissolved and particulate organic matter and nutrients and creates habitats for aquatic food webs.

**Connection:** Hydrological movement connects terrestrial production and detrital material to streams, wetlands, lakes, and oceans.

**Boundary:** Transport does not itself establish assimilation, consumption, or a specific trophic relationship.

### Record 0037 — Sediment and watershed transport

Erosion, deposition, and stream transport move organic material and organisms between terrestrial and aquatic systems.

**Connection:** Watersheds can supply leaf litter, dissolved organic matter, and particulate material that support aquatic consumers and decomposers.

**Boundary:** The quantity and biological availability of transported material depend on source, timing, chemistry, and receiving conditions.

### Record 0038 — Geological structure, rock cycles, weathering, and soil formation

Mineral weathering supplies nutrients and influences the chemical conditions under which organisms live.

**Connection:** Geological processes influence the nutrient supply supporting primary production.

**Boundary:** A mineral reservoir is not itself a food-web energy source unless a biological pathway uses the chemical energy or released materials.

### Record 0039 — Groundwater geology and aquifers

Groundwater can transport nutrients and organic compounds and connect terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems.

**Connection:** Subsurface pathways can affect production and microbial food webs in springs, wetlands, streams, and aquifers.

**Boundary:** Groundwater transport alone does not establish the biological use or trophic fate of a compound.

### Record 0040 — The Critical Zone

The Critical Zone brings together rock, soil, water, air, roots, vegetation, and microorganisms.

**Connection:** It contains many interfaces where primary production, decomposition, nutrient availability, and food-web interactions occur.

**Boundary:** Ecosystem energy pathways also extend into marine, deep subsurface, and other environments beyond the near-surface Critical Zone.
