## 1. First discriminating question

How do interactions among organisms and environmental conditions influence population sizes, community composition, and ecosystem processes—and how do feedbacks, disturbances, resistance, resilience, and ecological succession affect persistence or change?

Record 0042 examined energy flow and trophic relationships. This record examines how populations and communities respond to one another and to changing environmental conditions.

**Central distinction:** Ecological regulation does not mean that an ecosystem is motionless, perfectly balanced, or directed toward one inevitable final condition. Population sizes and ecosystem processes vary through time. Some interactions constrain or amplify change; disturbances can alter structure and function; recovery may occur, may be partial, or may lead to a different ecological state.

This record does not treat every ecosystem as self-correcting, every disturbance as beneficial, or every recovery as a return to an earlier condition.

## 3. Levels of ecological organization

Ecological regulation can be investigated at several related levels:

- **Organism:** an individual living entity and its responses to environmental conditions.
- **Population:** individuals of the same species in a defined area and time.
- **Community:** populations of different species living and interacting in an area.
- **Ecosystem:** a community together with the physical and chemical environment with which it interacts.

These levels are connected, but they are not interchangeable.

An individual's survival or reproduction can influence population growth. Interactions among populations can alter community composition. Community changes can influence ecosystem processes such as productivity, decomposition, nutrient cycling, and habitat structure.

The effect may also run in the other direction: environmental conditions influence organisms, populations, communities, and ecosystem processes.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Ecology integrates organismal, population, community, and ecosystem perspectives. Interactions occur within and across these levels.

**LIMIT:** A relationship observed at one level does not automatically establish a mechanism at another level. Population-level patterns may arise from multiple individual and environmental processes.

## 4. Population size, abundance, and density

A population is not described by one number alone.

Important measures include:

- **Population size:** total number of individuals in the defined population.
- **Abundance:** number of individuals recorded in a specified area or sample, depending on usage.
- **Density:** number of individuals per unit area or volume.
- **Dispersion:** spatial arrangement of individuals.
- **Age structure:** distribution among age classes.
- **Birth rate and death rate:** rates of additions through reproduction and losses through mortality.
- **Immigration and emigration:** movement into and out of the defined population.

A basic population accounting relationship is:

`Change in population size = births + immigration - deaths - emigration`

This is a bookkeeping identity for a defined population and interval. It is not a complete causal model. It does not explain why births, deaths, immigration, or emigration occur.

Population estimates also depend on detection. Individuals may be hidden, mobile, seasonal, or difficult to distinguish. An observed count is not necessarily the true population size.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Population change can be described by accounting for births, deaths, immigration, and emigration.

**LIMIT:** A population count or density estimate does not by itself identify the causes of population change.

## 5. Population growth and carrying capacity

Under simplified conditions, populations may increase rapidly when resources are abundant and limiting factors are weak.

A logistic growth model represents growth that slows as population size approaches a modeled carrying capacity.

Carrying capacity is commonly represented as the population size that a particular environment can support under specified conditions and assumptions.

It is not necessarily:
- a fixed number for all time;
- a directly visible boundary;
- identical for every species;
- independent of climate, resource supply, disturbance, or interactions;
- proof that population size will remain near one value.

Environmental conditions change. Resource availability can vary seasonally and over longer intervals. Predators, pathogens, competitors, migration, and human activities can alter population trajectories.

The carrying capacity concept is therefore useful, but its application requires an explicit species, place, period, and model.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Population growth is influenced by resource availability and limiting factors; carrying capacity is a model-dependent description of environmental support.

**LIMIT:** A simple logistic curve does not capture every population's behavior and should not be treated as a universal prediction.

## 6. Density-dependent and density-independent influences

### 6.1 Density-dependent factors

A density-dependent factor has an effect that changes with population density.

Examples can include:
- competition for food, water, nesting sites, or other resources;
- transmission of infectious disease;
- parasite effects;
- predation where encounter rates depend on prey density;
- accumulation of waste;
- crowding effects on reproduction or survival.

As density changes, the strength of these effects may change. The relationship is not necessarily linear, and different factors can operate together.

### 6.2 Density-independent factors

A density-independent factor affects population survival or growth without its effect being determined by the population's density at the outset.

Examples may include:
- severe storms;
- freezes;
- drought;
- heat waves;
- floods;
- some forms of fire or other physical disturbance.

The classification depends on the particular mechanism. A disturbance can have both density-independent and density-dependent consequences. For example, a storm may affect individuals regardless of density while subsequent competition for limited surviving habitat may depend on density.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Population regulation can involve density-dependent biological interactions and density-independent environmental influences.

**LIMIT:** These are analytical categories, not mutually exclusive descriptions of every real event. A single event can initiate multiple interacting effects.

## 7. Interactions among species

Populations within a community interact in different ways. The sign and strength of an interaction can depend on species, place, life stage, resources, and environmental conditions.

### 7.1 Competition

Competition occurs when organisms use a shared resource that is limited relative to demand.

It can occur:
- within a species (intraspecific competition);
- between species (interspecific competition).

Competition can influence growth, survival, reproduction, distribution, and resource use.

Resource partitioning can reduce direct competition when species use different resources, locations, or times. The competitive exclusion principle describes a limiting case in which species competing for the same limiting resources under sufficiently similar conditions cannot indefinitely coexist in the same niche. Real communities can be more complex because conditions vary and species differ in resource use.

### 7.2 Predation

Predation occurs when one organism consumes another organism.

Predator and prey populations can influence one another. In some systems, changes in prey abundance are followed by changes in predator abundance. However, predator–prey relationships do not necessarily produce regular cycles.

Other influences—including food availability, disease, climate, habitat, alternative prey, and density-dependent reproduction—can contribute to observed patterns.

The classic lynx–snowshoe hare record is an example of population variation associated with predator–prey dynamics, but it is not proof that predation alone explains every cycle.

### 7.3 Herbivory

Herbivory is the consumption of plants or plant material by animals.

It can influence plant survival, growth, reproduction, and community structure. Plants may have physical or chemical defenses, while herbivores may possess adaptations that allow them to feed on particular plants.

Herbivory can also affect seed dispersal and nutrient transfer.

### 7.4 Parasitism

A parasite benefits through an association with a host, while the host experiences a cost.

The effects vary with parasite burden, host condition, environment, and the particular relationship. Parasites can influence host survival, reproduction, behavior, and population dynamics.

### 7.5 Mutualism

Mutualism is an interaction in which both participating species benefit under the conditions being examined.

Examples include some plant–pollinator relationships and some associations between plants and microorganisms.

A mutualistic interaction may be conditional. Its benefits can vary with environmental conditions, partner abundance, and the availability of alternative resources.

### 7.6 Commensalism

Commensalism describes an interaction in which one species benefits while the other is not measurably helped or harmed under the stated conditions.

Determining that the second species is unaffected can be difficult. The label should not be used merely because an effect has not yet been measured.

### 7.7 Interaction categories are not permanent labels for all contexts

A species pair may have different effects under different conditions. A relationship classified as beneficial in one setting may impose costs in another. Effects may also differ across life stages or over time.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Competition, predation, herbivory, parasitism, mutualism, and commensalism describe distinct interaction patterns that can influence population and community dynamics.

**LIMIT:** These categories do not establish the magnitude, direction, or persistence of an interaction in every environment. Field evidence is required.

## 8. Direct and indirect effects

An ecological interaction can influence a second population directly or through one or more intermediate populations or environmental processes.

For example:
- a predator directly affects prey through consumption;
- a predator may indirectly affect plants by changing herbivore abundance or behavior;
- a plant may affect soil organisms through litter and root inputs;
- soil organisms may alter nutrient availability and thereby influence plant growth.

An indirect effect can be difficult to identify because several pathways may operate at once.

A correlation between two populations does not by itself establish a direct interaction. Both populations may respond to a third factor, or the apparent relationship may be influenced by time lags and unmeasured variables.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Ecological networks include direct and indirect interactions, and effects can propagate through communities.

**LIMIT:** Correlation, temporal sequence, and plausible mechanism are different forms of evidence. Causal claims require appropriate evidence and study design.

## 9. Feedback and regulation

Feedback occurs when a change in a system influences processes that subsequently affect the original change or a related variable.

### 9.1 Negative feedback

Negative feedback occurs when a response tends to oppose an initial change in the variable being regulated.

A simplified ecological example might be:
1. A population increases.
2. Resource competition becomes stronger.
3. Average reproduction or survival decreases.
4. Population growth slows.

This is a possible mechanism, not a universal sequence. The strength and timing of the response must be measured.

### 9.2 Positive feedback

Positive feedback occurs when a response reinforces an initial change.

A simplified example might be:
1. Vegetation loss exposes soil.
2. Increased erosion removes soil and reduces conditions for vegetation recovery.
3. Further vegetation loss becomes more likely.

This pathway can amplify change, but it is not inevitable. Other processes may interrupt or reverse it.

### 9.3 Feedback loops can include delays

Ecological responses often occur after a delay:
- reproductive responses take time;
- organisms may mature before reproducing;
- nutrients may be stored before becoming available;
- vegetation recovery may require several growing seasons;
- predator abundance may respond after prey abundance changes.

A delayed feedback can produce oscillations, overshoot, or unstable behavior in some models. It should not be assumed that every negative feedback produces immediate stability.

### 9.4 Feedback is not synonymous with purpose

In ecology, feedback is a description of causal relationships within a system. The term does not by itself establish intention, foresight, authorship, or theological purpose.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Feedback mechanisms can dampen or amplify changes, and time delays can alter their effects.

**LIMIT:** The feedback label must be supported by an identified pathway and evidence. A circular diagram or verbal description alone does not establish an operating mechanism.

## 10. Top-down and bottom-up influences

### 10.1 Top-down influences

Top-down influences originate through consumers and other higher trophic relationships and can affect organisms or processes at lower trophic levels.

Predators may affect prey abundance, prey behavior, or the distribution of feeding pressure. These effects can sometimes propagate through multiple trophic levels.

### 10.2 Bottom-up influences

Bottom-up influences originate through resource availability and environmental conditions, such as:
- light;
- water;
- mineral nutrients;
- primary production;
- habitat structure.

Changes in primary production or resource supply can affect consumers and higher trophic levels.

### 10.3 Both can operate together

An ecosystem is not necessarily controlled by only one direction of influence. Resource supply, consumer interactions, disturbance, and physical conditions can operate together.

The relative importance of top-down and bottom-up processes varies among ecosystems and over time.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Ecosystem processes can be influenced through both trophic interactions and resource availability.

**LIMIT:** Neither top-down nor bottom-up control should be assumed universal or exclusive.

## 11. Disturbance

A disturbance is an event or process that disrupts organisms, populations, communities, or ecosystem processes relative to a defined condition or reference.

Examples include:
- fire;
- storms;
- floods;
- drought;
- volcanic activity;
- landslides;
- insect outbreaks;
- disease;
- pollution;
- land clearing;
- invasive species introductions.

Disturbances differ in:
- intensity;
- severity;
- frequency;
- duration;
- spatial extent;
- timing;
- predictability;
- interaction with prior conditions.

A disturbance may remove biomass, alter habitat, change resource availability, redistribute nutrients, modify hydrology, or change species interactions.

Not every disturbance is harmful in every context, and not every disturbance is beneficial. Some ecosystems and species are associated with recurring disturbance regimes, while other disturbances can exceed the conditions under which those systems persist.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Disturbance is a major driver of ecological change, and its effects depend on its characteristics and the condition of the system before and during the event.

**LIMIT:** Calling an event a disturbance does not predict whether the ecosystem will recover, reorganize, or cross a threshold into another state.

## 12. Resistance, resilience, recovery, and persistence

These terms describe different properties or outcomes. Definitions can vary across ecological literature, so the study must state how each term is being used.

### 12.1 Resistance

Resistance describes the degree to which a system changes little in response to a disturbance or stressor.

A system with high resistance may show relatively small changes in a selected variable or function during an event.

Resistance is always relative to:
- the disturbance being considered;
- the variable measured;
- the spatial scale;
- the time interval.

### 12.2 Resilience

Resilience is commonly used to describe the capacity of a system to recover, reorganize, or adapt following disturbance. Some ecological frameworks distinguish the speed or degree of return from the capacity to remain within a particular regime despite change.

The precise definition must be stated because resilience is used in more than one way.

A system can be resistant to one disturbance and vulnerable to another. A system can change substantially yet retain or regain important functions. Recovery of one variable does not prove that all aspects of the ecosystem have recovered.

### 12.3 Recovery

Recovery is a measured change following disturbance toward a stated reference condition or set of functions.

Questions include:
- Which variable is recovering?
- What reference condition is used?
- Over what period?
- Is the recovery complete or partial?
- Has species composition returned?
- Have ecosystem functions returned?
- Is the new state stable over the observation period?

### 12.4 Persistence

Persistence describes continued existence or maintenance of a population, species, function, or system over a defined interval.

Persistence does not require the absence of change. A population can persist while its abundance fluctuates. An ecosystem can persist while species composition and processes vary.

### 12.5 Alternative states and thresholds

Some ecosystems can reorganize into different persistent states. A threshold may be crossed when gradual pressure produces a disproportionately large or difficult-to-reverse change.

Examples discussed in ecological research include shifts in vegetation structure, aquatic clear-water and turbid-water conditions, or forest transitions under combined stressors.

The existence of alternative states is context-specific. A threshold should be supported by evidence rather than inferred from any abrupt change.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Resistance, resilience, recovery, persistence, and threshold behavior describe related but distinct aspects of ecological response.

**LIMIT:** No single metric captures all dimensions of resilience. Recovery to a prior state is not guaranteed, and a new state is not automatically evidence of system failure.

## 13. Succession and ecological reorganization

Ecological succession describes changes in community composition and structure through time.

### 13.1 Primary succession

Primary succession occurs where ecological development begins on a substrate with little or no developed soil, such as newly exposed rock or certain newly formed surfaces.

Organisms can contribute to weathering, organic matter accumulation, and changing conditions that allow other organisms to establish.

### 13.2 Secondary succession

Secondary succession occurs where a disturbance affects an existing ecosystem but some biological legacies—such as soil, seeds, roots, organisms, or organic matter—remain.

The sequence of recovery depends on disturbance severity, surviving organisms, environmental conditions, and incoming propagules.

### 13.3 Succession is not always one fixed sequence

Successional pathways can differ by:
- climate;
- substrate;
- disturbance type;
- disturbance frequency;
- species availability;
- soil and hydrological conditions;
- interactions among organisms;
- human land use.

A single universal sequence should not be assumed.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Communities can change in composition and structure following disturbance, and ecological succession is one framework for examining those changes.

**LIMIT:** Succession does not guarantee a single predetermined endpoint or a return to the exact pre-disturbance community.

## 14. Diversity and stability

Species diversity is often investigated in relation to ecosystem functioning and stability. However, the relationship is not reducible to the statement that more species always means more stability.

Different species may respond differently to environmental variation. Some may perform overlapping functions; others may have distinctive effects. Species interactions and environmental conditions influence whether diversity contributes to resistance, recovery, or sustained ecosystem function.

Researchers distinguish several possible dimensions:
- temporal stability of total biomass or productivity;
- stability of individual populations;
- resistance to a particular disturbance;
- recovery rate after disturbance;
- persistence of ecosystem functions;
- variability among species and functional groups.

A community can show stable total productivity while individual species fluctuate. Conversely, some species may remain stable while total ecosystem function changes.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Diversity can influence ecosystem function and stability, but effects depend on which stability dimension, ecosystem, and disturbance are examined.

**LIMIT:** Species richness alone is not a sufficient measure of resilience or stability.

## 15. Human influence and management

Human activities can alter ecological regulation and disturbance regimes through:
- habitat fragmentation and conversion;
- extraction of organisms and resources;
- pollution;
- nutrient enrichment;
- altered fire regimes;
- introduction of non-native species;
- climate change;
- dams, drainage, irrigation, and other hydrological changes;
- selective removal or protection of species.

Management interventions can also change population dynamics and ecosystem processes.

Examples include restoring habitat connectivity, reducing pollution, managing invasive species, protecting keystone or foundation species, restoring wetlands, and adjusting disturbance management.

Interventions require evidence about local conditions, ecological trade-offs, and unintended consequences. An intervention that benefits one species or process may affect another.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Human actions can change ecological interactions, resource availability, disturbance patterns, and ecosystem trajectories.

**LIMIT:** General ecological principles do not determine the outcome of a specific intervention without site-specific evidence and monitoring.
