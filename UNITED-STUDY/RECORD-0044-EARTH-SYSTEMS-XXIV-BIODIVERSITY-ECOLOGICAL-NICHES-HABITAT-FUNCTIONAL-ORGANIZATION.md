## 1. First discriminating question

How are organisms distributed across habitats, how do ecological niches and environmental constraints shape those distributions, and how do species with different characteristics contribute to the organization and functioning of ecological communities?

Record 0043 examined population and community responses to interactions and disturbance. This record examines biodiversity, species distribution, ecological niches, functional characteristics, and the relationships between organisms and the places they inhabit.

**Central distinction:** Habitat describes the place or environmental setting in which an organism lives. An ecological niche describes a multidimensional set of conditions, resources, and interactions associated with the organism's survival, reproduction, and ecological relationships. A place is not the same thing as the role and requirements of an organism within it.

This record does not assume that every species has one simple role, that every habitat supports every organism, or that the number of species alone describes the full organization of a community.

## 3. Biodiversity: more than a species count

Biodiversity refers broadly to biological variety. It can be considered at several levels, including:
- genetic variation within species;
- diversity among species;
- diversity of ecosystems and ecological communities;
- variation in biological forms, traits, and ecological functions.

These dimensions are related but not interchangeable.

### 3.1 Species richness

Species richness is the number of different species recorded in a defined area or sample.

Richness is sensitive to sampling:
- the area sampled;
- the time and season of sampling;
- the methods used;
- the detectability of organisms;
- the taxonomic resolution;
- the effort spent searching.

A site with more recorded species may have been sampled more intensively than another site. Comparisons therefore require appropriate sampling design or statistical treatment.

### 3.2 Species evenness

Evenness describes how equally individuals are distributed among the species in a community.

Two communities can have identical species richness but different evenness. One may have similar numbers of individuals across species; another may be dominated by one or two species.

### 3.3 Species diversity

Species diversity measures generally combine richness and relative abundance or evenness. Different indices emphasize these components differently.

A diversity value is not self-explanatory. The index, sampling unit, time, spatial scale, and data must be reported.

### 3.4 Genetic and ecosystem diversity

Species-level counts do not describe all biodiversity. Genetic variation can influence a population's capacity to respond to changing conditions. Ecosystem diversity describes variation among habitats, ecological communities, and ecological processes across a landscape or region.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Biodiversity has multiple dimensions. Species richness, evenness, genetic variation, and ecosystem diversity describe different aspects of biological variety.

**LIMIT:** A single species count or diversity index cannot represent every dimension of biodiversity or establish ecosystem health by itself.

## 4. Habitat: the place and environmental setting

A habitat is the place or environmental setting in which an organism or population lives.

Habitat descriptions can include:
- temperature and climate;
- water availability;
- light;
- soil or substrate;
- elevation and topography;
- shelter and nesting locations;
- food and other resources;
- vegetation structure;
- salinity, acidity, and other chemical conditions;
- disturbance patterns;
- the presence of other organisms.

Habitats can be described at different scales. A forest may be a habitat for one investigation, while a particular tree cavity, stream margin, soil layer, or leaf surface may be the relevant habitat for another.

A habitat is not necessarily a sharply bounded area. Organisms may use different places for feeding, reproduction, shelter, migration, or different stages of their life cycle.

### 4.1 Habitat quality

Habitat quality concerns how well a location supplies the conditions and resources required by an organism or population.

Area alone does not establish quality. A large habitat may lack essential resources or be degraded. A smaller habitat may provide important resources but remain vulnerable to isolation, disturbance, or limited population size.

### 4.2 Habitat is species-relative

The same physical location can be suitable for one species and unsuitable for another. Requirements vary with physiology, behavior, life stage, and ecological interactions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Habitat is a place or environmental setting whose characteristics influence the organisms that can use it.

**LIMIT:** Habitat labels such as forest, wetland, grassland, or reef are broad categories. They do not by themselves establish suitability for a particular species.

## 5. Ecological niche: conditions, resources, and interactions

The ecological niche is not simply another word for habitat.

A niche can be described as the multidimensional combination of environmental conditions, resources, and biological interactions associated with a species' ability to survive and reproduce.

Potential niche dimensions include:
- temperature range;
- moisture or water availability;
- light conditions;
- food type and availability;
- nesting or reproductive requirements;
- timing of activity;
- depth or elevation;
- salinity or soil chemistry;
- competitors, predators, parasites, mutualists, and other interacting organisms.

The niche concept is useful because species occupying the same habitat may use different resources, occupy different microhabitats, or interact with other organisms in different ways.

### 5.1 Fundamental niche

The fundamental niche is commonly used to describe the set of environmental conditions and resources under which a species can maintain populations, considered apart from specified limiting biotic interactions and other constraints in the model.

The precise definition depends on the ecological framework. Researchers must state which interactions and limitations are included or excluded.

### 5.2 Realized niche

The realized niche describes the conditions and resources a species actually occupies or uses under the combined influence of environmental conditions, biological interactions, dispersal, history, and other constraints.

Competition and predation can restrict where a species occurs. Facilitation or mutualism can sometimes enable occupation of conditions that would otherwise be difficult to use.

### 5.3 The distinction is a model, not a directly visible boundary

The fundamental niche is often difficult to measure directly because it may require experiments or inference about conditions not currently occupied. The realized niche is also difficult to measure completely because observations can miss rare, seasonal, or poorly detected occurrences.

The traditional expectation that a realized niche is a subset of a fundamental niche is useful in many frameworks, but real-world estimation is complicated by dispersal, historical range limits, positive interactions, environmental change, and how niche dimensions are defined.

A geographic range is not identical to a niche. Suitable environmental conditions may exist beyond a species' current range, while unsuitable locations may occur within its broad geographic range.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Ecological niche theory distinguishes potential environmental and resource conditions from the conditions and resources actually used under real ecological and geographic constraints.

**LIMIT:** Niche estimates depend on definitions, selected variables, sampling, experiments, spatial scale, and assumptions. No single map of observed occurrences captures every niche dimension.

## 6. Habitat, niche, range, and distribution: four different descriptions

These terms should not be collapsed:

- **Habitat:** the place or environmental setting used by an organism.
- **Ecological niche:** the multidimensional conditions, resources, and interactions associated with its persistence and ecological relationships.
- **Geographic range:** the geographic area across which a species occurs.
- **Distribution:** the spatial pattern of occurrence or abundance within a defined region and time.

A species can occupy a broad geographic range but occur only in suitable patches within it. Two species can share a habitat while occupying different niches. A species may have suitable habitat beyond its current range but be unable to reach it.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Habitat, niche, range, and distribution answer different questions and must be measured and interpreted separately.

**LIMIT:** Maps of species occurrence are not complete descriptions of habitat quality, niche breadth, or long-term population viability.

## 7. Adaptation, acclimation, and plasticity

### 7.1 Adaptation

In evolutionary biology, adaptation refers to heritable characteristics shaped by natural selection over generations because they influence reproductive success in particular environments.

An adaptation is not an intentional decision by an individual organism. Nor does the term mean that every trait is perfectly suited to every condition.

### 7.2 Acclimation

Acclimation describes physiological or functional adjustment by an individual organism in response to environmental conditions, often over a relatively short period.

Acclimation does not necessarily involve genetic change or evolution across generations.

### 7.3 Phenotypic plasticity

Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity of a genotype to produce different observable characteristics under different environmental conditions.

Plasticity can include developmental, physiological, or behavioral responses. It is not automatically beneficial in every environment.

### 7.4 Adaptation and acclimation are not interchangeable

An organism's immediate response to temperature, light, water, or other conditions is not, by itself, evidence of evolutionary adaptation. Demonstrating adaptation requires evidence concerning heritable variation and differential reproductive success across generations.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Evolutionary adaptation, individual acclimation, and phenotypic plasticity describe different biological processes and timescales.

**LIMIT:** A trait's appearance or usefulness alone does not establish its evolutionary history or adaptive function. Mechanism and evidence must be examined.

## 8. Functional traits and ecological roles

Functional traits are measurable characteristics of organisms that influence their performance, interactions, or effects on ecosystem processes.

Examples include:
- leaf size and structure;
- root depth and architecture;
- seed size and dispersal characteristics;
- body size;
- feeding morphology;
- growth rate;
- lifespan;
- reproductive timing;
- drought or salinity tolerance;
- photosynthetic pathway;
- mobility and dispersal ability.

Traits can help researchers examine how organisms respond to environmental conditions and how they influence ecological processes.

### 8.1 Functional roles

Organisms can contribute to ecological processes through different functions:
- primary production;
- pollination;
- seed dispersal;
- herbivory;
- predation;
- decomposition;
- nutrient transformation;
- habitat creation or modification;
- filtration and water movement;
- soil formation and stabilization.

A species may contribute to several functions, and a single function may be performed by many species.

### 8.2 Functional redundancy

Functional redundancy describes overlap in the contributions of different species to a particular ecological function.

It does not mean species are identical or interchangeable in every respect. Species that perform similar functions may differ in environmental tolerance, timing, location, interaction partners, or response to disturbance.

### 8.3 Functional diversity

Functional diversity concerns the range, distribution, and variation of traits and functional strategies within a community.

It differs from species richness. Two communities with the same number of species may have very different functional trait distributions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Functional traits provide a way to examine how organism characteristics relate to environmental responses and ecosystem processes.

**LIMIT:** A trait association does not automatically establish the mechanism or magnitude of a species' ecosystem effect. Functional categories depend on the question and scale.

## 9. Keystone species and foundation species

These terms describe different kinds of ecological importance.

### 9.1 Keystone species

A keystone species has an ecological effect on community structure or function that is disproportionately large relative to its abundance or biomass.

The classic sea-star example involves predation on mussels. Removing the sea star can allow mussels to dominate space and alter community composition.

Keystone effects can arise through predation, herbivory, mutualistic relationships, or other ecological mechanisms.

### 9.2 Foundation species

A foundation species contributes substantially to the physical structure or environmental conditions of a community, often by creating habitat or modifying local conditions.

Examples can include corals, kelp, mangroves, and trees, depending on the ecosystem and the specific function being studied.

### 9.3 Ecosystem engineers

Ecosystem engineers are organisms that create, modify, or maintain habitat by changing physical or chemical conditions.

Examples include beavers constructing dams, corals building reef structures, and organisms that alter soil or sediment.

An ecosystem engineer may also be a foundation or keystone species, but the terms describe different aspects of ecological influence and are not synonyms.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Some species exert particularly strong effects on community structure, habitat, or ecosystem processes. Keystone species, foundation species, and ecosystem engineers describe distinguishable forms of influence.

**LIMIT:** These labels are context-dependent. A species' status can vary among ecosystems, and the label requires evidence of the relevant ecological effect.

## 10. Pollination, seed dispersal, and specialized relationships

Pollination and seed dispersal provide important examples of ecological functions that can depend on interactions among species.

### 10.1 Pollination

Pollination is the transfer of pollen to a receptive part of a flower or plant reproductive structure. In animal-pollinated systems, organisms such as insects, birds, and bats can contribute to pollen transfer.

Different plants and pollinators may exhibit specialized or generalized relationships. The degree of specialization varies, and a plant may depend on one pollinator group or receive visits from multiple groups.

A pollinator's presence does not automatically prove effective pollination. Researchers distinguish visitation, pollen transfer, fertilization, and successful seed production.

### 10.2 Seed dispersal

Seed dispersal moves seeds away from the parent plant. It can occur through wind, water, gravity, or animals.

Animal-mediated dispersal can involve seeds carried externally or consumed and later deposited. The effectiveness of dispersal depends on seed survival, destination, germination conditions, and establishment.

### 10.3 Specialized relationships are not universal templates

A specialized interaction may be important to the organisms involved, but it should not be assumed that every species depends on one exclusive partner. Relationships must be established for the species and context being examined.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Pollination and seed dispersal are ecological processes that can connect species, reproduction, movement, and community organization.

**LIMIT:** Observing an interaction does not alone establish its reproductive effectiveness, exclusivity, or evolutionary origin.

## 11. Native, endemic, nonnative, and invasive species

These terms describe different geographic and ecological facts.

### 11.1 Native species

A native species occurs in a region through natural processes rather than being introduced there by human activity.

The geographic region and historical reference period should be specified when the designation matters.

### 11.2 Endemic species

An endemic species is naturally restricted to a defined geographic area. Endemism is relative to the area being described: a species can be endemic to an island, mountain range, watershed, or country.

### 11.3 Nonnative or introduced species

A nonnative species occurs outside its natural historical range as a result of introduction, often through human activity.

Nonnative status alone does not establish that a species is harmful.

### 11.4 Invasive species

Invasive species terminology is used in different policy and scientific contexts. In general, it refers to nonnative organisms whose establishment or spread produces or threatens adverse ecological, economic, or other impacts, with the exact definition depending on the governing framework.

Not every introduced species becomes invasive. Establishment, spread, impact, and the relevant legal or scientific definition must be distinguished.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Native, endemic, nonnative, and invasive describe different relationships between species, geography, introduction history, and impact.

**LIMIT:** A geographic label does not by itself quantify ecological impact. A species must be assessed in its actual ecological and regulatory context.

## 12. Habitat fragmentation and ecological connectivity

Habitat fragmentation occurs when a habitat is divided into smaller or more isolated patches, often through land conversion, roads, infrastructure, or other changes.

Potential consequences include:
- reduced movement among populations;
- reduced gene flow;
- altered migration routes;
- smaller local populations;
- increased edge effects;
- altered predator or competitor access;
- reduced access to seasonal resources;
- greater vulnerability to local extinction.

Effects vary among species. A barrier to one organism may be passable to another. A corridor that benefits one species may not benefit all species.

### 12.1 Structural connectivity

Structural connectivity describes the physical arrangement of habitat patches and intervening landscape features.

It can be estimated from maps, remote sensing, and landscape measurements.

### 12.2 Functional connectivity

Functional connectivity concerns how effectively organisms, genes, gametes, propagules, or other ecological flows actually move through a landscape.

A landscape that appears connected on a map may not be functionally connected for a particular species.

### 12.3 Ecological corridors

An ecological corridor is a defined geographic space managed to maintain or restore ecological connectivity.

Corridors can support movement between habitats, but their effectiveness depends on species behavior, corridor design, surrounding land use, threats, and management.

### 12.4 Connectivity is not simply proximity

Two habitat patches may be geographically close but functionally isolated by roads, unsuitable land, barriers, or risk. Conversely, a species may move through a landscape that does not appear continuously suitable.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Ecological connectivity concerns movement of organisms and the flow of natural processes. Structural and functional connectivity are distinct concepts.

**LIMIT:** A corridor's presence does not guarantee successful dispersal, gene flow, migration, or population persistence. Outcomes require species-specific evidence and monitoring.

## 13. Conservation: status, function, and evidence

Conservation examines the maintenance or recovery of biological diversity, species, habitats, and ecological processes.

Relevant evidence can include:
- population size and trend;
- geographic range;
- habitat area and quality;
- reproductive success;
- genetic diversity;
- connectivity;
- mortality and recruitment;
- threats and pressures;
- ecosystem functions;
- monitoring over time.

A conservation status category is not identical to an ecological function measurement. A species may be threatened while performing an important ecosystem function; a common species may also have a substantial ecological effect.

Conservation priorities require explicit objectives, evidence, time horizons, and consideration of trade-offs.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Conservation assessment integrates information about species, populations, habitats, threats, and ecological processes.

**LIMIT:** No single status label, abundance estimate, or habitat-area figure is sufficient to determine every conservation outcome.
