## 1. First discriminating question

How is biological information transmitted across generations, how does heritable variation arise and persist, and how do mutation, recombination, inheritance, selection, genetic drift, gene flow, and reproductive isolation contribute to changes in populations and the formation of species?

Record 0044 examined biodiversity, habitat, niche, functional traits, and species distribution. This record investigates the biological mechanisms by which inherited characteristics are transmitted and populations change across generations.

**Central distinction:** Individual organisms develop, respond to their environments, and reproduce. Evolutionary change, as measured in population genetics, is change in the inherited genetic composition of populations across generations. An individual does not evolve in the population-genetic sense during its lifetime.

This investigation must distinguish the mechanisms that generate or rearrange genetic variation from the processes that change its frequency, and distinguish population change from the formation of reproductively distinct species.

## 3. DNA, genes, chromosomes, and genomes

### 3.1 DNA

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a molecule that stores biological sequence information in its nucleotide order. In cellular organisms, DNA is organized into chromosomes and other genomic arrangements.

DNA is not itself identical to a gene. It is the molecular material in which genes and other sequences occur.

### 3.2 Genes

A gene is a DNA sequence associated with a functional product, commonly an RNA molecule and, in many cases, a protein produced through gene expression.

Gene boundaries and functional definitions can be complex. Alternative splicing, regulatory sequences, overlapping genes, and noncoding RNAs make it inaccurate to reduce every gene to a simple one-sequence/one-protein formula.

### 3.3 Chromosomes

Chromosomes are organized DNA molecules associated with proteins and other molecular components. They carry genetic information and are distributed during cell division.

Chromosome number, structure, and inheritance differ among organisms. Some organisms are haploid, diploid, polyploid, or have more complex life cycles.

### 3.4 Genome

A genome is the full complement of genetic material in an organism or cell, according to the scope of the definition being used.

A genome contains genes as well as regulatory and other sequences. It is not merely a list of protein-coding genes.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** DNA, genes, chromosomes, and genomes describe related but distinct levels of biological organization.

**LIMIT:** A gene's presence does not by itself establish when, where, or how it will be expressed, nor does sequence information alone explain every feature of an organism.

## 4. Genes, alleles, genotype, and phenotype

### 4.1 Alleles

Alleles are alternative versions of a DNA sequence at a particular genomic location. Different alleles can arise through mutation and may be present at different frequencies in a population.

Not every genomic location has only two possible alleles. A population can contain multiple alleles at one locus.

### 4.2 Genotype

Genotype describes the genetic constitution of an organism, cell, or specified genetic locus.

The term can refer to a particular allele combination or to a broader genetic constitution. The intended scope must be clear.

### 4.3 Phenotype

Phenotype refers to observable or measurable characteristics of an organism. Phenotypes can include morphology, physiology, behavior, molecular characteristics, and other measurable outcomes.

Phenotype is influenced by genetic factors, environmental conditions, developmental history, and interactions among these influences.

### 4.4 Genotype is not phenotype

Two organisms can have different genotypes but similar observed phenotypes. Organisms with similar genotypes can express different phenotypes under different environmental or developmental conditions.

A phenotype does not always reveal the underlying genotype. Conversely, a genetic difference does not necessarily produce an obvious observable difference.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Alleles contribute to genotype; genotype and environmental conditions interact in the development and expression of phenotype.

**LIMIT:** A visible trait cannot be assigned to one gene without evidence. Many characteristics are influenced by multiple genes, environmental factors, and gene-by-environment interactions.

## 5. Inheritance and transmission across generations

Inheritance is the transmission of biological information or material from parent organisms to offspring.

In sexually reproducing diploid organisms, offspring generally receive genetic contributions from two parents through gametes. Meiosis reduces chromosome number, and fertilization restores the diploid complement.

This familiar pattern is not universal:
- many organisms reproduce asexually;
- some organisms alternate sexual and asexual reproduction;
- some have haploid-dominant life cycles;
- plants can have complex alternation of generations;
- bacteria reproduce through cell division and can also exchange genetic material horizontally.

### 5.1 Segregation

In diploid sexual reproduction, homologous chromosomes separate during meiosis I. This separates the two inherited copies of a chromosome into different cells and contributes to the segregation of alleles.

### 5.2 Independent assortment

Different homologous chromosome pairs can orient independently during meiosis I, producing different combinations of parental chromosomes in gametes.

The classical independent-assortment expectation applies most directly to genes on different chromosomes or sufficiently separated on the same chromosome. Genes close together on a chromosome can be linked and inherited together more often.

### 5.3 Fertilization

Fertilization combines genetic contributions from gametes. The resulting genotype is a new combination of inherited genetic material.

The exact process and its genetic consequences vary across reproductive systems.

### 5.4 Inheritance is not identical to resemblance

Offspring may resemble their parents because of inherited genetic material, but resemblance is not a complete account of inheritance. Recombination, segregation, new mutations, environmental effects, and developmental processes contribute to the resulting organism.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Heredity transmits genetic information through organism-specific reproductive and cellular processes. In sexual reproduction, meiosis and fertilization contribute to the combinations inherited by offspring.

**LIMIT:** The diploid sexual model must not be imposed on every organism. A particular resemblance does not independently establish its genetic mechanism.

## 6. Mutation: the origin of new sequence variants

A mutation is a change in genetic material. Mutations can occur through errors in DNA replication, damage and repair processes, mobile genetic elements, or other molecular mechanisms.

Mutations can include:
- substitutions of nucleotide bases;
- insertions and deletions;
- duplications;
- rearrangements;
- changes involving chromosome number or structure;
- larger genomic changes.

### 6.1 Mutation and new alleles

Mutation is a fundamental source of new alleles. Recombination can rearrange existing alleles into new combinations, but recombination alone does not create a new DNA sequence variant in the same sense as mutation.

### 6.2 Effects of mutation

Mutation effects vary:
- some have no detectable effect under a particular measurement;
- some alter gene function or expression;
- some reduce organismal fitness in a given environment;
- some can increase fitness in a particular context;
- some have effects that depend on genetic background or environment.

A mutation is not inherently beneficial or harmful in every possible environment.

### 6.3 Somatic and germline changes

In organisms with distinct germline and somatic cell lineages, mutations in somatic cells are generally not transmitted through sexual reproduction, while mutations in germline cells can be inherited by offspring.

In organisms without a distinct germline, the distinction must be framed according to their reproductive biology.

### 6.4 Mutation is not directed by need

Mutation does not occur because an organism recognizes a future need and deliberately writes the required sequence. Mutation processes have biological causes and patterns, but natural selection can subsequently change the frequency of variants according to their effects in a particular context.

This does not mean every mutation is perfectly random in every molecular respect. Mutation rates and spectra can vary among genomic locations, organisms, and environmental conditions. The essential distinction is that mutation is not a purposeful response that reliably produces whatever adaptive change an organism needs.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Mutation introduces new genetic sequence variants and is a fundamental source of genetic variation.

**LIMIT:** The existence of a mutation does not establish its effect, inheritance, adaptive value, or future frequency. Those require additional evidence.

## 7. Recombination and the reshuffling of inherited variation

Recombination is the rearrangement or exchange of genetic material that produces new combinations of genetic variants.

In sexual reproduction, important sources include:

- crossing over between homologous chromosomes during meiosis;
- independent assortment of chromosome pairs;
- the combination of genetic contributions at fertilization.

### 7.1 Crossing over

During meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair and can exchange corresponding DNA segments between nonsister chromatids. This produces recombinant chromosomes carrying combinations of alleles different from the original parental arrangements.

### 7.2 Independent assortment

The orientation of homologous chromosome pairs during meiosis I produces different combinations of parental chromosomes in gametes.

### 7.3 Recombination versus mutation

These mechanisms must remain distinct:

- Mutation can create new sequence variants.
- Recombination can rearrange existing variants into new combinations.
- Independent assortment distributes chromosome combinations into gametes.
- Fertilization combines gametic contributions.

Together, these processes contribute to genetic diversity in sexually reproducing populations.

### 7.4 Recombination outside the familiar sexual model

Genetic exchange also occurs in other biological contexts. Bacteria can acquire genetic material through horizontal gene transfer, including transformation, transduction, and conjugation. These processes are not the same as meiotic crossing over.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Recombination changes the combinations of genetic material and contributes to variation, while mutation supplies new sequence variants.

**LIMIT:** Recombination does not guarantee a beneficial phenotype or a particular adaptive result.

## 8. Genetic variation and phenotypic variation

Genetic variation refers to differences in genetic material among individuals or populations.

Phenotypic variation refers to differences in observable or measurable characteristics.

These are related but not identical.

Phenotypic differences can arise from:
- genetic differences;
- environmental differences;
- developmental history;
- gene-by-environment interactions;
- stochastic biological processes;
- combinations of these factors.

### 8.1 Heritability

In quantitative genetics, heritability describes the proportion of phenotypic variation in a particular population, under specified conditions, that is statistically associated with genetic variation.

Heritability is population- and environment-specific. It does not mean that a fixed percentage of an individual's trait is genetic, nor does it establish that a trait cannot change with environmental conditions.

### 8.2 Phenotypic plasticity

Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity of a genotype to produce different phenotypes under different environmental conditions.

Plasticity can occur during development or through physiological and behavioral responses. It does not necessarily involve a change in DNA sequence.

### 8.3 Inherited versus acquired characteristics

An acquired characteristic during an individual's lifetime is not automatically passed to offspring through genetic inheritance.

Some environmentally influenced biological states can affect descendants through particular mechanisms, including certain epigenetic processes, but these effects must be demonstrated in the organism and context under investigation. They must not be used to claim that all acquired characteristics are inherited.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Genetic variation and phenotypic variation are distinguishable. Heritability estimates the genetic contribution to variation within a defined population and environment.

**LIMIT:** Heritability is not an immutable property of a trait or an individual. Observed phenotype alone cannot establish the source of variation.

## 9. Evolution as population change

In population genetics, evolution can be measured as change in allele frequencies across generations within a population.

This definition makes the unit and timescale explicit.

A population may change genetically even when no individual consciously changes. Conversely, an individual can change during development or acclimation without that change constituting population evolution.

### 9.1 Allele frequency

Allele frequency describes the proportion of copies of a particular allele among all copies of that gene or locus in a defined population.

To compare frequencies meaningfully, researchers must define:
- the population;
- the locus or genetic marker;
- the sampling method;
- the time points;
- the treatment of migration and population structure.

### 9.2 Population gene pool

The gene pool refers to the genetic variants present in a population under a stated definition and sampling scope.

It is not a literal physical container. It is a population-level description of genetic variation.

### 9.3 Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

The Hardy–Weinberg principle provides a mathematical reference model for allele and genotype frequencies under specified assumptions, including random mating and the absence of evolutionary forces such as selection, mutation, migration, and genetic drift.

Departures from the model can help researchers investigate population structure and evolutionary processes. A departure alone does not identify which cause produced it.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Population evolution can be investigated through changes in allele frequencies across generations.

**LIMIT:** A measured frequency change requires attention to sampling error, population definition, migration, nonrandom mating, and other possible explanations.

## 10. Natural selection

Natural selection occurs when heritable differences among organisms are associated with differences in survival or reproductive contribution in a particular environment.

Selection acts through organisms and their phenotypes. Across generations, the genetic variants associated with different reproductive outcomes can change in frequency.

### 10.1 Necessary distinctions

Natural selection requires:
- variation among individuals;
- a heritable component to relevant variation;
- differences in reproductive contribution associated with that variation.

If a trait difference is entirely environmental and not transmitted, selection on that difference will not produce the corresponding inherited change across generations.

### 10.2 Relative fitness

Evolutionary fitness describes reproductive contribution relative to others in a defined population and context.

It is not simply physical strength, longevity, health, intelligence, or moral worth. An organism's fitness is evaluated through its contribution to subsequent generations under the specified conditions.

### 10.3 Patterns of selection

Common patterns include:
- **Directional selection:** one end of a trait distribution is favored.
- **Stabilizing selection:** intermediate trait values are favored relative to extremes.
- **Disruptive selection:** different extremes are favored relative to intermediate values.
- **Frequency-dependent selection:** the fitness of a phenotype depends partly on how common or rare it is.
- **Sexual selection:** differences in mating or reproductive success influence the evolution of traits.

These categories describe patterns, not universal laws that every population must follow.

### 10.4 Selection is not foresight

Natural selection does not plan a future organism or direct mutations toward a predetermined result. It describes differential reproductive outcomes among existing heritable variants in particular conditions.

The environment affects which phenotypes have greater reproductive success. If conditions change, the direction and strength of selection can also change.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Natural selection can change allele frequencies when heritable variation is associated with differences in reproductive contribution.

**LIMIT:** A trait's current usefulness does not by itself establish its origin, the complete history of its selection, or whether selection was the only process involved.

## 11. Genetic drift: change through chance

Genetic drift is change in allele frequencies caused by random sampling of genetic variants across generations.

It differs from natural selection because the frequency change is not necessarily associated with a heritable advantage.

### 11.1 Population size

The effects of genetic drift are generally stronger in smaller populations, where chance sampling can produce larger frequency changes.

### 11.2 Bottleneck effect

A population bottleneck occurs when population size is sharply reduced. The surviving individuals may carry only a subset of the original population's genetic variation.

### 11.3 Founder effect

A founder effect occurs when a new population is established by a small number of individuals. Its allele frequencies may differ from those of the source population because the founders represent a limited sample.

### 11.4 Drift and selection can operate together

Genetic drift and natural selection are not mutually exclusive. Both can influence the same population at the same time.

A variant that is beneficial may be lost by chance, particularly in a small population. A neutral variant may become common through drift without providing a selective advantage.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Genetic drift changes allele frequencies through chance sampling and can alter genetic diversity, particularly in small populations.

**LIMIT:** A frequency change alone does not demonstrate selection or adaptation. Drift must be considered as an alternative or concurrent explanation.

## 12. Gene flow and population connection

Gene flow is the movement of alleles among populations through the movement of organisms or reproductive material.

It can occur through:
- migration followed by reproduction;
- movement of pollen between plant populations;
- dispersal of seeds that establish and reproduce;
- movement of gametes or other reproductive material;
- other organism-specific processes.

### 12.1 Effects of gene flow

Gene flow can introduce alleles into a population, increase local genetic variation, or reduce genetic differences between populations.

Its effects depend on the number of migrants, their reproductive success, the genetic variants they carry, and the receiving population.

### 12.2 Gene flow and geographic separation

Geographic barriers can reduce gene flow, but geography alone does not establish complete reproductive isolation.

Some organisms cross barriers, use different routes, or disperse over long distances. Others may remain isolated despite physical proximity because of behavioral, ecological, temporal, or genetic barriers.

### 12.3 Horizontal gene transfer

In many microorganisms, genetic material can move between organisms without ordinary parent-to-offspring reproduction. This is horizontal gene transfer.

It is a significant distinction when examining microbial evolution and should not be collapsed into the familiar sexual-population model.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Gene flow changes the distribution of genetic variants among populations and can influence population differentiation.

**LIMIT:** Geographic movement does not necessarily produce gene flow. Reproduction or successful genetic transfer must occur.

## 13. Evolutionary mechanisms compared

| Mechanism | Principal action | Typical population-level consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Mutation | Changes DNA sequence | Introduces new genetic variants |
| Recombination | Rearranges existing genetic material | Produces new combinations of variants |
| Independent assortment | Distributes chromosome combinations during meiosis | Produces varied gametic combinations |
| Natural selection | Differential reproductive contribution associated with heritable traits | Nonrandom change in variant frequencies |
| Genetic drift | Chance sampling across generations | Random change in variant frequencies |
| Gene flow | Movement of alleles among populations | Introduces or redistributes variants |
| Nonrandom mating | Changes who reproduces with whom | Can change genotype frequencies and, in some circumstances, allele frequencies through associated selection |

These mechanisms can operate together. No single mechanism should be assumed to explain every observed population change.

## 14. Reproductive isolation and speciation

Speciation is the formation of distinct species. The mechanisms and criteria used to identify species depend partly on the organisms being studied.

### 14.1 Biological species concept

For many sexually reproducing organisms, the biological species concept emphasizes populations that actually or potentially interbreed and are reproductively isolated from other such populations.

This concept is useful but does not apply cleanly to all asexual organisms, fossils, or cases where reproductive relationships cannot be observed.

### 14.2 Reproductive isolation

Reproductive isolation occurs when barriers prevent or substantially restrict successful reproduction between populations.

Barriers are commonly grouped into:

**Prezygotic barriers**
- habitat or ecological isolation;
- temporal isolation;
- behavioral isolation;
- mechanical isolation;
- gametic incompatibility.

These barriers act before a zygote forms.

**Postzygotic barriers**
- reduced hybrid viability;
- reduced hybrid fertility;
- later-generation hybrid breakdown.

These barriers act after fertilization or hybrid formation.

### 14.3 Allopatric speciation

Allopatric speciation involves geographic separation that restricts gene flow between populations. The separated populations can accumulate genetic differences through mutation, drift, and selection. Reproductive isolation may eventually develop.

Geographic separation alone does not prove that speciation has occurred.

### 14.4 Sympatric speciation

Sympatric speciation occurs without the initial requirement of geographic separation. Mechanisms can include changes in chromosome number, ecological differentiation, or assortative mating that reduce gene flow within a shared geographic area.

Polyploidy is particularly important in some plant lineages.

### 14.5 Species concepts and their boundaries

Other species concepts use ecological, morphological, phylogenetic, or evolutionary criteria. Different approaches can yield different classifications in difficult cases.

Hybridization and incomplete reproductive isolation can complicate species boundaries. Some lineages exchange genes after divergence, and speciation need not be a perfectly instantaneous division.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Speciation involves the development of distinct evolutionary lineages, commonly associated with reduced gene flow and reproductive isolation.

**LIMIT:** Geographic separation, morphological difference, or a genetic distance measurement alone does not universally establish species status. The species concept and evidence must be stated.

## 15. Speciation, extinction, and population change

These terms describe different outcomes.

- **Population change:** genetic or demographic change within a population.
- **Divergence:** increasing differences between populations or lineages.
- **Speciation:** formation of distinct species or evolutionary lineages under the applicable species concept.
- **Extinction:** the loss of a species or other defined taxonomic group when no living individuals remain.
- **Local extinction:** disappearance of a species from a particular area while it persists elsewhere.

A population can change genetically without becoming a new species. A lineage can diversify through speciation. A species can become extinct without producing a descendant species.

Evolutionary change does not guarantee increasing complexity, improvement, or survival. Extinction is part of the documented history of life.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Population evolution, speciation, and extinction are distinct processes and outcomes.

**LIMIT:** A branching diagram is a model of lineage relationships; it is not a direct recording of every historical reproductive event.

## 16. Evidence for evolutionary relationships and common ancestry

Evolutionary biology draws on multiple independent and partly overlapping evidence domains.

### 16.1 Fossil evidence

Fossils document organisms from different geological periods and preserve evidence of anatomical change, extinction, and the appearance of different forms through time.

The fossil record is incomplete because fossilization is rare and preservation, discovery, and dating are uneven.

### 16.2 Comparative anatomy and development

Homologous structures can indicate shared evolutionary history, even when their present functions differ.

Analogous structures can perform similar functions while having different evolutionary origins. Similar function alone does not prove close ancestry.

Developmental evidence can also reveal shared and divergent biological patterns, but similarities must be interpreted carefully.

### 16.3 Molecular evidence

DNA and protein sequence comparisons provide evidence of genetic similarities and differences among organisms.

Patterns of molecular similarity can be used to infer evolutionary relationships. Results depend on sampling, methods, models, and the genomic regions examined.

### 16.4 Biogeography

The geographic distribution of organisms, including island distributions, continental patterns, and regional endemism, can provide evidence relevant to evolutionary history.

Historical geography, dispersal, extinction, and environmental change must be considered.

### 16.5 Independent evidence and inference

Agreement among fossil, anatomical, molecular, and geographic evidence can strengthen an evolutionary inference. Researchers must still distinguish direct observations from reconstructed historical relationships.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Evolutionary relationships and common ancestry are inferred from converging evidence across multiple biological and geological domains.

**LIMIT:** A phylogenetic reconstruction is an evidence-based model of relationships, not direct observation of every ancestral population or historical event.

## 17. Evolutionary biology and the origin of life are different questions

Evolutionary biology investigates inherited change, population divergence, biological relationships, and the history of living lineages.

Origin-of-life research investigates how the earliest life or life-like systems may have arisen from nonliving chemistry.

These are related areas of scientific inquiry, but they are not identical questions.

Evidence that populations evolve does not, by itself, establish a complete explanation for the origin of the first life. Likewise, a proposed origin-of-life pathway is not a substitute for evidence about inheritance, natural selection, genetic drift, or speciation.

The study must not collapse:
- origin of life into evolution of populations;
- evolution of populations into the origin of the universe;
- biological mechanism into metaphysical cause;
- a scientific model into a claim about ultimate authority.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Evolutionary biology and origin-of-life research address different scientific questions and require distinct evidence.

**LIMIT:** Neither field's terminology should be used to claim that a result from one automatically settles the questions addressed by the other.

## 19. KJV Genesis 1–2: textual foundation kept distinct

Modern genetics and evolutionary terminology are not inserted into the KJV text. The following are textual observations, not claims that Genesis supplies modern genetic mechanisms or population-genetic models.

### Genesis 1:11–12 — seed and fruit after kind

**TEXT STATES:** God commands the earth to bring forth vegetation, including the herb yielding seed and the fruit tree yielding fruit after his kind, whose seed is in itself.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The passage identifies seed, fruit, and reproduction-related language within the account of created vegetation.

**LIMIT:** The KJV wording does not define DNA, genes, alleles, chromosomes, mutation, recombination, or modern species concepts.

### Genesis 1:20–25 — living creatures after their kind

**TEXT STATES:** The passage describes living creatures in the waters, fowl, and living creatures brought forth by the earth, according to their kinds.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The account differentiates living creatures and repeatedly uses the phrase according to their kinds.

**LIMIT:** The passage does not provide a modern taxonomy or a genetic definition of kind. No one-to-one equivalence with a modern biological species category is established by the wording alone.

### Genesis 1:26–28 — humanity

**TEXT STATES:** God creates man in his image and gives the stated blessing and dominion.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** Humanity's creation, identity, blessing, and place in relation to other creatures are presented within the account.

**LIMIT:** The passage does not supply a population-genetic account of human ancestry or modern mechanisms of inheritance.

### Genesis 1:29–30 — provision

**TEXT STATES:** The passage describes provision for humanity and other living creatures.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The account identifies provision in relation to living beings.

**LIMIT:** The text does not specify genetic adaptation, selection pressures, or evolutionary fitness.

### Genesis 2:7 — formation and breath of life

**TEXT STATES:** The LORD God forms man of the dust of the ground, breathes into his nostrils the breath of life, and man becomes a living soul.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The passage identifies God's action in man's formation and the giving of life.

**LIMIT:** The verse is not a molecular or population-genetic description. DNA replication, inheritance, and mutation are not stated in this passage.

### Genesis 2:8–17 — placement, provision, and command

**TEXT STATES:** God plants the Garden, places the man there, provides its setting, gives him work, and establishes a command and boundary.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The man enters an environment he did not originate and receives provision, responsibility, and command.

**LIMIT:** These verses do not define natural selection, genetic drift, gene flow, reproductive isolation, or speciation.

No evolutionary mechanism is read back into Genesis. Scientific coherence is not presented as scientific confirmation of Scripture, and a scientific description is not treated as authority over the Author.

### 1. What must AI ask to understand GUA-W?

- What exact biological entity is being studied: gene, allele, chromosome, genome, cell, organism, population, or lineage?
- What is the organism's reproductive and inheritance system?
- Is the claim about DNA sequence, gene expression, genotype, or phenotype?
- Which inheritance pattern is demonstrated?
- Is the organism diploid, haploid, polyploid, or characterized by a more complex life cycle?
- Is the variation genetic, environmental, developmental, or a combination?
- Is the mutation somatic or potentially heritable?
- Does the claim distinguish new mutation from recombination of existing variants?
- Is the observed genetic change within one population or between populations?
- What population and generations are being compared?
- How are allele frequencies measured?
- Could sampling error explain the observed change?
- Could selection, drift, gene flow, mutation, or nonrandom mating explain the result?
- Are multiple mechanisms operating together?
- What is the measured reproductive contribution?
- Does the evidence establish heritability in the relevant population?
- Is a trait being called an adaptation without evidence of its evolutionary history?
- Is geographic separation being confused with reproductive isolation?
- Which species concept is being applied?
- Are hybridization or incomplete reproductive barriers present?
