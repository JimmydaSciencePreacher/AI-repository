## 4. Metabolism: the connected chemical activity of living cells

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Metabolism is the network of chemical reactions through which cells transform molecules and maintain the chemical processes required for cellular activity.

Metabolic reactions include the breakdown of molecules, the synthesis of molecules, the transfer of functional groups, electron transfers, and reactions that support transport, movement, growth, repair, and maintenance.

Two broad categories are commonly distinguished:

- **Catabolism:** pathways that break down molecules and often make usable free energy available.
- **Anabolism:** pathways that build complex molecules and generally require an input of energy and chemical building blocks.

These categories are connected. Products or intermediates from catabolic pathways can supply substrates for anabolic pathways. A molecule may be broken down for one purpose and its intermediate redirected into another pathway.

Metabolism is therefore not one linear sequence. It is a network with branches, shared intermediates, regulatory points, and connections to the cell's environment.

**BOUNDARY:** The word *metabolism* does not name one pathway, one organelle, or one energy source. Its meaning depends on the complete set of chemical transformations being considered in the organism, tissue, cell, or experimental system.

## 5. Energy and matter: related but not interchangeable

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Living systems transform both matter and energy, but matter and energy are not the same thing.

- Matter consists of physical substances and particles participating in chemical and biological transformations.
- Energy is a measurable property of physical systems that can be transferred and transformed.
- Chemical potential energy is associated with the state and arrangement of a chemical system and its capacity to perform work under specified conditions.
- Chemical reactions rearrange atoms and electrons; they do not create matter from nothing.
- Energy may be transferred between chemical forms, electrical or electrochemical gradients, mechanical work, and heat.

A metabolic pathway can transfer atoms from one molecule to another while transferring free energy through reaction coupling and electron carriers. These are related events, but they are different descriptions of what is changing.

**IMPORTANT DISTINCTION:** Energy transfer through a living system is not the same as matter cycling through that system. Matter can be reused and rearranged through biological and geological cycles. Energy is transferred and transformed, with some becoming dispersed as heat and no longer available to perform the same kind of work.

**BOUNDARY:** A statement that matter cycles does not mean energy cycles in the same manner. A statement that energy flows does not mean matter is consumed or disappears.

## 6. Thermodynamics and Gibbs free energy

The laws of thermodynamics apply to chemical reactions in living systems. Biological organization does not exempt cells from physical constraints.

For biochemical reactions, Gibbs free-energy change (ΔG) is useful for describing whether a reaction is thermodynamically favorable in a specified direction under the conditions being considered.

- If ΔG is negative, the forward reaction is thermodynamically favorable under those conditions.
- If ΔG is positive, the forward reaction is unfavorable under those conditions.
- If ΔG is zero, the system is at equilibrium for that reaction under the stated conditions.

The actual free-energy change depends on more than a standard-state value. Concentrations of reactants and products, temperature, and other conditions affect the direction and magnitude of ΔG.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** A reaction's thermodynamic favorability is not identical to its speed. A favorable reaction may proceed very slowly if its activation-energy barrier is high. Conversely, a catalyst can increase reaction rate without changing the reaction's equilibrium position or the overall free-energy difference between reactants and products.

**BOUNDARY:** “Spontaneous” in thermodynamics does not mean instantaneous, purposive, conscious, or without a mechanism. It describes the favored direction under defined conditions.

## 7. Enzymes: reaction catalysts, not energy sources

Enzymes are biological catalysts. Many are proteins; some catalytic RNA molecules also exist.

Enzymes accelerate reactions by providing a pathway with a lower activation-energy barrier. They can position substrates, stabilize transition states, and coordinate chemical steps.

Enzymes do not:
- create energy;
- change the overall free-energy difference between reactants and products;
- change the equilibrium constant of a reaction;
- make every thermodynamically unfavorable reaction proceed without an energy input;
- guarantee that a reaction will proceed in a particular direction regardless of cellular conditions.

An enzyme's activity can depend on substrate concentration, temperature, pH, cofactors, inhibitors, activators, and regulatory modifications.

**DISTINCTION:** Reaction rate and reaction favorability are different questions. Enzymes affect kinetics; thermodynamic conditions determine the free-energy relationship and equilibrium.

## 8. Coupled reactions and the role of ATP

Many reactions required for cellular activity are thermodynamically unfavorable when considered alone. Cells can coordinate an unfavorable reaction with a favorable one so that the combined process has a negative overall free-energy change.

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a central molecule in this coupling.

ATP can be hydrolyzed to ADP and inorganic phosphate, or through other reactions to AMP and pyrophosphate. Under cellular conditions, ATP hydrolysis can provide a free-energy change that helps drive an otherwise unfavorable process when the reactions are properly coupled.

ATP participates in:
- biosynthesis;
- active transport across membranes;
- mechanical work, including muscle contraction;
- phosphorylation and regulation of proteins;
- cellular signaling;
- maintenance of ion gradients and other cellular conditions.

ATP is continually regenerated from ADP and phosphate through metabolic processes. It is not a permanent store that a cell fills once and then uses indefinitely.

**IMPORTANT CORRECTION:** The phrase “high-energy phosphate bond” is a conventional shorthand. It does not mean that breaking a chemical bond by itself releases energy. Breaking a bond requires energy; the net free-energy release in ATP hydrolysis comes from the complete reaction, including the formation and stabilization of products and the conditions in which the reaction occurs.

ATP is often called the cell's energy currency. This is a useful analogy for its role in transferring usable free energy, but ATP is not energy itself and is not the only molecule that can transfer free energy.

**BOUNDARY:** ATP concentration alone does not reveal the full energetic state of a cell. ATP, ADP, AMP, phosphate, substrate availability, and the surrounding conditions all matter.

## 9. Redox chemistry: electron transfer and metabolic energy

Oxidation and reduction occur together in redox reactions.

- **Oxidation:** loss of electrons.
- **Reduction:** gain of electrons.

When one molecule loses electrons, another molecule or acceptor gains them. The two processes are coupled.

In metabolism, electron transfers can change the potential energy associated with molecules and provide a means of transferring energy through a sequence of controlled reactions.

Important electron carriers include:

- NAD⁺ / NADH;
- FAD / FADH₂;
- NADP⁺ / NADPH;
- additional carriers such as quinones and cytochromes in electron-transfer systems.

NAD⁺ and FAD commonly receive electrons during the oxidation of metabolic substrates. NADH and FADH₂ can then deliver electrons to other reactions, including respiratory electron-transport systems.

NADPH is widely used as a source of reducing power in biosynthetic pathways and photosynthetic carbon fixation.

**DISTINCTION:** An electron carrier is not ATP. It carries reducing equivalents that can be used in later reactions. ATP transfers free energy through a different chemical mechanism.

**BOUNDARY:** Electron transfer and proton movement are related in many metabolic systems, but they are not identical. A proton gradient is an electrochemical difference across a membrane; it is not simply a count of electrons moving through a pathway.

## 10. Glycolysis: a widely distributed pathway

Glycolysis is a sequence of reactions that converts one glucose molecule into two pyruvate molecules.

In the commonly taught pathway:
- two ATP molecules are invested in early steps;
- four ATP molecules are produced later by substrate-level phosphorylation;
- the net gain is two ATP molecules per glucose;
- two NAD⁺ molecules are reduced to two NADH molecules;
- two pyruvate molecules are produced.

Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol and does not directly require molecular oxygen. It is present across a broad range of organisms.

The pathway is not simply a single act of glucose breakdown. It is a sequence of enzyme-catalyzed transformations involving intermediate molecules and regulated steps.

**DISTINCTION:** The net ATP yield of glycolysis is not the same as the total ATP yield of aerobic cellular respiration. Glycolysis produces a net two ATP per glucose in the standard pathway description; later processes can yield additional ATP when conditions and pathways permit.

**BOUNDARY:** Not every organism uses only the standard textbook pathway. Alternative glycolytic routes and organism-specific metabolic pathways exist.

## 11. Fermentation and metabolism without oxygen

When cells cannot use aerobic respiration to reoxidize NADH, fermentation can regenerate NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue.

Examples include:
- lactic acid fermentation, in which pyruvate is reduced to lactate;
- alcoholic fermentation, in which pyruvate-derived intermediates are converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide.

Fermentation allows continued glycolytic ATP production in the absence of oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor for aerobic respiration.

**IMPORTANT DISTINCTION:** Fermentation is not the same as anaerobic respiration. Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ through organic intermediates and does not use an electron-transport chain with an external terminal electron acceptor in the manner of respiration. Anaerobic respiration uses an electron-transport system and a terminal electron acceptor other than oxygen.

Fermentation does not itself add a further ATP yield beyond the ATP generated through glycolysis in the standard examples. Its central role is to regenerate oxidized electron carrier so glycolysis can continue.

**BOUNDARY:** The terms *anaerobic*, *fermentation*, and *without oxygen* must not be treated as interchangeable descriptions of every metabolic pathway.

## 12. Pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle

Under aerobic conditions in many eukaryotic cells, pyruvate can be transported into mitochondria and converted to acetyl-CoA. This conversion releases carbon dioxide and transfers electrons to NAD⁺, producing NADH.

Acetyl-CoA can enter the citric acid cycle (also called the Krebs cycle or tricarboxylic acid cycle).

The cycle:
- oxidizes the acetyl group through a series of reactions;
- releases carbon dioxide;
- generates NADH and FADH₂;
- produces a small amount of ATP or GTP through substrate-level phosphorylation;
- regenerates the molecule that accepts the next acetyl group.

The cycle is not merely an ATP-producing loop. Its intermediates also supply material for the synthesis of amino acids, lipids, nucleotides, and other cellular components.

**DISTINCTION:** Catabolic and anabolic uses of the citric acid cycle are connected. The cycle participates in energy extraction and in the supply of biosynthetic intermediates.

**BOUNDARY:** The cycle's operation and direction of particular reactions depend on the organism, cell type, substrate supply, and metabolic conditions. A simplified textbook diagram does not represent every metabolic branch.

## 13. Electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation

In aerobic respiration in eukaryotic cells, electrons carried by NADH and FADH₂ enter the mitochondrial electron-transport chain.

As electrons pass through a sequence of carriers and protein complexes, energy is released in controlled steps. At specified complexes, this energy is used to move protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane.

This creates an electrochemical proton gradient, often described as the proton-motive force. The gradient includes both a difference in proton concentration and an electrical potential across the membrane.

ATP synthase provides a route through which protons move down their electrochemical gradient. The enzyme couples this movement to ATP synthesis from ADP and inorganic phosphate.

This coupling of electron transport, proton-gradient formation, and ATP synthesis is central to oxidative phosphorylation.

Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor in the standard aerobic mitochondrial respiratory chain. It accepts electrons and, with protons, contributes to water formation.

**DISTINCTIONS:**
- Electron transport is not ATP synthesis itself.
- Proton pumping is not the same as ATP synthase activity.
- A proton gradient is not a chemical fuel molecule.
- ATP synthase is not an electron carrier.
- Oxygen is not the source of the energy released by oxidation of food molecules; it is the terminal electron acceptor in aerobic respiration.

**BOUNDARY:** Prokaryotic organisms do not have mitochondria. Their respiratory electron-transport systems are commonly located in the plasma membrane, with organism-specific variations in components and terminal electron acceptors.

## 14. Photosynthesis: capturing light energy and building organic molecules

Photosynthesis is a set of processes through which photosynthetic organisms convert light energy into chemical energy and use that energy to support the production of organic molecules.

In oxygenic photosynthesis, found in plants, algae, and cyanobacteria:
- light is absorbed by photosynthetic pigments;
- light energy drives electron transfer in the photosynthetic apparatus;
- water is oxidized, releasing oxygen;
- electron transport contributes to a proton gradient;
- ATP is synthesized through ATP synthase;
- NADP⁺ is reduced to NADPH;
- ATP and NADPH are used in carbon-fixation reactions, including the Calvin-Benson cycle.

The Calvin-Benson cycle uses ATP and NADPH to support the incorporation and reduction of carbon dioxide into organic molecules. It is not a direct conversion of light into sugar in one step.

**DISTINCTION:** The light-dependent reactions and carbon-fixation reactions are connected but are not the same process. ATP and NADPH are produced in the light-dependent reactions and consumed in carbon fixation.

**BOUNDARY:** Not all photosynthetic organisms use oxygenic photosynthesis. Some bacteria perform anoxygenic photosynthesis, which uses different electron donors and does not release oxygen as a product of water splitting.

## 15. Photosynthesis and cellular respiration: connected, not identical reversals

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are often introduced through simplified overall equations. These summaries can reveal broad relationships between carbon dioxide, water, oxygen, and organic molecules, but they must not conceal the distinct pathways and mechanisms involved.

Photosynthesis:
- captures external energy, commonly sunlight;
- uses electron-transfer processes to generate ATP and reducing power;
- supports carbon fixation and the production of organic matter.

Cellular respiration:
- oxidizes organic molecules;
- transfers electrons through metabolic carriers and, in many organisms, an electron-transport chain;
- couples energy-releasing reactions to ATP synthesis and other cellular work.

The processes are connected at multiple levels:
- photosynthesis contributes organic matter that can become a substrate for respiration;
- respiration returns carbon dioxide and water to environmental cycles;
- photosynthetic oxygen supports aerobic respiration in many ecosystems;
- respiration and decomposition return nutrients and carbon compounds to ecological circulation.

**BOUNDARY:** Photosynthesis is not simply cellular respiration run backward. The pathways, enzymes, membranes, electron donors and acceptors, and regulatory mechanisms differ. Their broad chemical relationship does not make them mechanistically identical.

## 16. Metabolic networks and pathway integration

Metabolic pathways form networks rather than isolated chains.

Carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic-acid precursors are connected through shared intermediates and regulated reactions.

Examples:
- glucose can be stored as glycogen or starch, depending on the organism;
- fatty acids can be broken down through beta-oxidation;
- amino acids can enter central metabolism after removal or transfer of nitrogen-containing groups;
- citric acid cycle intermediates can support biosynthesis;
- glycolytic intermediates can be diverted into other pathways;
- photosynthetic carbon fixation supplies carbon skeletons for plant metabolism.

The same intermediate may be used in more than one pathway. This means that pathway activity depends on the demands and conditions of the wider metabolic network.

**DISTINCTION:** A pathway diagram is a selective representation of a network. It does not show every reaction, compartment, transporter, regulatory influence, or alternative route in a living cell.

## 17. Metabolic regulation and feedback

Cells regulate metabolism at multiple levels.

Regulation may involve:
- enzyme abundance;
- gene expression;
- allosteric activation or inhibition;
- covalent modification, including phosphorylation;
- substrate and product availability;
- feedback inhibition;
- transport across membranes;
- compartmentalization;
- hormonal or intercellular signals in multicellular organisms;
- changes in cellular energy and redox state.

For example, the relative abundance of ATP, ADP, AMP, NAD⁺, and NADH can influence metabolic enzyme activity and pathway rates.

Feedback regulation can prevent unnecessary production of a molecule when sufficient product is already available. However, not every metabolic pathway is controlled by the same feedback mechanism.

**DISTINCTION:** Metabolic regulation is related to gene regulation but is not identical to it. Gene regulation can alter the amount of an enzyme produced; metabolic regulation can alter the activity of an existing enzyme without requiring a change in gene expression.

**CONNECTION TO RECORD 0046:** Developmental gene regulation and metabolic regulation can influence one another. Cellular differentiation may change the metabolic requirements and enzyme profile of a cell. Conversely, metabolic conditions can influence signaling, gene expression, and cellular state. The relationship is context-dependent and must be examined in the relevant organism and cell type.

## 18. Membranes, compartments, and transport

Metabolic processes are often organized by cellular membranes and compartments.

Examples include:
- glycolysis in the cytosol;
- the citric acid cycle in the mitochondrial matrix in many eukaryotic cells;
- oxidative phosphorylation at the inner mitochondrial membrane;
- photosynthetic electron transport at thylakoid membranes in plants and many algae;
- proton gradients across membranes;
- transport proteins that move substrates, products, ions, and metabolites between compartments.

Membranes provide boundaries that permit concentration differences and electrochemical gradients to be maintained. These differences can be used to drive transport and ATP synthesis.

**DISTINCTION:** A membrane boundary is not itself an energy source. It permits the maintenance of conditions and gradients that can be used in coupled processes.

**BOUNDARY:** Not all cells have the same organelles or internal compartment structure. Prokaryotic and eukaryotic systems must not be conflated.

## 19. Metabolism, organismal function, and the environment

Cells acquire substrates and energy from their surroundings, directly or indirectly.

For animals, food supplies organic molecules and chemical energy. For plants and other photosynthetic organisms, light provides an external energy source, while water, carbon dioxide, and mineral nutrients supply matter required for metabolism.

Organisms also exchange matter and energy with their environments through processes such as:
- feeding and digestion;
- gas exchange;
- water uptake and loss;
- mineral nutrient uptake;
- excretion;
- photosynthesis;
- respiration;
- decomposition.

Metabolism supports organismal functions including movement, growth, maintenance, reproduction, repair, and responses to environmental change.

**BOUNDARY:** The metabolic process measured in an isolated cell or tissue may not represent the total metabolism of an intact organism. Organismal physiology includes transport, regulation, interactions among tissues, and environmental exchange.

## 20. Cellular metabolism and ecosystem energy flow

At ecosystem scale, energy enters through primary producers or other external energy sources and is transferred among organisms and environmental processes.

In most ecosystems, photosynthetic organisms capture sunlight and convert a portion of it into chemical energy stored in organic matter. Consumers obtain chemical energy by consuming organisms or organic material. Decomposers process dead organisms and waste, returning nutrients and elements to environmental pools.

At each transfer, some energy becomes dispersed as heat. The amount of energy available to support further biological work is therefore reduced across successive transfers.

**DISTINCTION:**
- Energy flows through ecosystems and is transformed.
- Elements such as carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur cycle through organisms and the environment.
- Organic matter can be transferred between organisms and decomposers.
- The cycling of matter does not mean that energy is recycled without loss of availability.

**CONNECTION TO EARLIER RECORDS:** Records 0041–0044 examined biogeochemical cycles, ecosystem energetics, food webs, ecosystem regulation, and ecological niches. This record identifies cellular metabolism as one of the mechanisms through which organisms participate in those broader ecological relationships.

**BOUNDARY:** Cellular ATP production is not identical to ecosystem energy flow. The former concerns molecular processes within cells; the latter concerns transfers among organisms and the environment.

## 21. Metabolic diversity across life

Metabolism is not uniform across all life.

Organisms differ in:
- energy sources;
- carbon sources;
- electron donors;
- terminal electron acceptors;
- respiratory pathways;
- fermentation pathways;
- photosynthetic mechanisms;
- metabolic substrates;
- environmental tolerances;
- cellular compartmentalization.

Some organisms use oxygen in respiration. Others use alternative terminal electron acceptors. Some organisms obtain energy from inorganic chemical reactions. Some capture light without producing oxygen. Many organisms combine or change metabolic strategies according to environmental conditions.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** The diversity of metabolic pathways is a major feature of biological diversity.

**BOUNDARY:** A pathway described in a human cell, a plant chloroplast, or a model bacterium cannot automatically be treated as universal across all organisms.

## 22. Metabolism, development, and cellular identity

Record 0046 examined cell differentiation, gene regulation, signaling, and morphogenesis. Metabolism connects to those processes because a cell's chemical state and energy requirements influence what it can do and how it responds.

Examples of relevant questions include:
- Do differentiated cell types express different metabolic enzymes?
- How do changes in nutrient availability influence cell signaling?
- How do redox state and metabolite concentrations affect gene regulation?
- How does mitochondrial activity influence cellular differentiation in a specific system?
- How do metabolic pathways change during development?
- How do developmental signals alter nutrient uptake and metabolic demand?

These questions are not answered by assuming that one metabolic state produces one universal cell fate. They require organism-, tissue-, and stage-specific evidence.

**BOUNDARY:** A correlation between a metabolite and a developmental state does not by itself establish causation. A proposed mechanism must be supported by appropriate experiments and alternative explanations must be considered.
