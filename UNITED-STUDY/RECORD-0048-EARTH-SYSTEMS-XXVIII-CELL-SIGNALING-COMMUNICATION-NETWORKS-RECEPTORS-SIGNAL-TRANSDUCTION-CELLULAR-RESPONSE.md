## 1. Investigation question

How do cells receive, transmit, process, and respond to signals from their environment and from other cells; how do receptors, second messengers, phosphorylation cascades, electrical signals, and feedback networks coordinate cellular behavior; and how do these mechanisms connect metabolism, development, physiology, and organism-level regulation?

This record examines cell signaling as a set of physical and biochemical processes by which cells detect conditions, respond to stimuli, and coordinate activity. It distinguishes a signal from its carrier, reception from response, and molecular mechanism from interpretation.

The investigation includes unicellular and multicellular organisms. Cell signaling is not exclusive to organisms with tissues or organs: single cells can detect nutrients, chemical gradients, environmental conditions, and signals from other cells.

## 4. What is a cellular signal?

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** A cellular signal is a stimulus or detectable change that can initiate or alter a biological response in a cell or biological system.

Signals may be:
- chemical molecules, including hormones, neurotransmitters, growth factors, and metabolites;
- ions, including calcium;
- physical stimuli, including light, temperature, mechanical force, and electrical changes;
- direct molecular interactions between adjacent cells;
- environmental conditions such as nutrient availability or oxygen concentration.

A signal is detected through a biological mechanism. The mechanism may involve a receptor protein, an ion channel, a sensory protein, a molecular binding event, or another specialized detection system.

**DISTINCTION:** A signal is not identical to its physical carrier. A chemical ligand is a molecule; the information attributed to its presence depends on the biological system's capacity to detect and respond to it.

A signal may have different effects in different cells because cells differ in receptor expression, intracellular machinery, developmental state, and environmental conditions.

**BOUNDARY:** The term *information* in cell biology does not automatically mean conscious knowledge, intention, language, or a message understood in the human sense. It describes a detectable difference that can influence a biological process.

## 5. The basic stages: reception, transduction, response

Cell signaling is often organized into three broad stages.

### Reception

A cell detects a signal. In many cases, a ligand binds to a receptor. In other cases, a stimulus is detected by a sensory or physical mechanism.

### Signal transduction

The detection event is converted into intracellular molecular or electrical changes. These may involve G proteins, enzymes, ion channels, second messengers, phosphorylation, or other mechanisms.

### Cellular response

The cell changes some aspect of its activity. A response may include altered enzyme activity, gene expression, secretion, movement, metabolism, membrane transport, proliferation, differentiation, or survival.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** These three stages provide a useful framework, but actual signaling systems may have multiple branches, feedback loops, parallel pathways, and overlapping events.

**BOUNDARY:** The three-stage description is a teaching framework, not a claim that every pathway contains exactly three molecular steps or follows one simple linear chain.

## 6. Ligands, receptors, and target cells

A **ligand** is a molecule or ion that binds to a particular molecular target. In cell signaling, the target is often a receptor.

A **receptor** is a molecular component that detects a signal and initiates or regulates a response. Receptors may be located in the plasma membrane or inside the cell.

A **target cell** is a cell capable of responding to a signal through the relevant receptor and downstream machinery.

The same signaling molecule may affect one cell but not another if the cells differ in receptor expression or downstream response mechanisms.

**DISTINCTION:**
- Ligand: the signaling molecule or binding partner.
- Receptor: the molecular detector or binding target.
- Target cell: the cell that has the relevant capacity to respond.
- Response: the resulting change in cellular activity.

Binding alone does not guarantee a particular response. Receptor activation, intracellular pathway components, cellular context, and regulatory conditions all matter.

**BOUNDARY:** A ligand is not necessarily a hormone. Hormones are one category of signaling molecules, generally associated with endocrine signaling.

## 7. Forms of cell-to-cell signaling

Different forms of signaling can be distinguished by how signals reach their target cells.

### Autocrine signaling

A cell releases a signaling molecule that acts on the same cell or cells of a similar local population.

### Paracrine signaling

A cell releases a signal that acts on nearby cells. The signal generally operates over a local distance.

### Endocrine signaling

An endocrine cell releases a hormone into circulation. The signal can travel to distant target cells.

### Synaptic signaling

A neuron releases neurotransmitter at a specialized synapse, where the signal acts on a nearby target cell.

### Juxtacrine signaling

A membrane-associated signaling molecule on one cell interacts with a receptor or partner on an adjacent cell. Direct cell contact is required.

### Direct communication through intercellular channels

In animals, gap junctions can permit certain ions and small molecules to pass between adjacent cells. In plants, plasmodesmata provide channels connecting neighboring cells.

**DISTINCTION:** These categories describe different routes or spatial arrangements of communication. They are not interchangeable.

**BOUNDARY:** A signal's distance of travel does not alone determine its effect. Receptor availability, signal concentration, degradation, transport, and cellular context also matter.

## 8. Cell-surface receptors

Many signaling molecules cannot freely cross the plasma membrane. Their signals are detected by cell-surface receptors.

Major receptor classes include:

- G-protein-coupled receptors;
- enzyme-linked receptors, including receptor tyrosine kinases;
- ligand-gated ion channels;
- other receptor and sensory-protein families.

A cell-surface receptor can convert an extracellular binding event into an intracellular change. This conversion is a central feature of signal transduction.

The receptor does not simply act as a passive opening through which the ligand enters. It may change shape, recruit proteins, activate enzymes, alter ion flow, or initiate a series of molecular interactions.

**BOUNDARY:** Not every receptor is a membrane receptor. Some ligands cross the plasma membrane and bind intracellular receptors.

## 9. Intracellular receptors

Some signaling molecules can cross the plasma membrane and bind receptors within the cell.

Steroid hormones are a familiar example. Their receptors may be located in the cytoplasm or nucleus. Ligand binding can alter receptor activity and, in many cases, influence gene transcription.

Other intracellular signaling mechanisms respond to molecules that enter the cell or are produced within it.

**DISTINCTION:** Intracellular receptor signaling and cell-surface receptor signaling are different mechanisms. They can produce overlapping biological effects, but they should not be described as identical pathways.

**BOUNDARY:** Not all steroid-related effects occur only through direct regulation of gene transcription; signaling effects can be more varied and context-dependent.

## 10. G-protein-coupled receptors

G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are a large family of cell-surface receptors.

In a common GPCR mechanism:
1. A ligand binds to the receptor.
2. The receptor changes conformation.
3. The receptor interacts with a heterotrimeric G protein.
4. The G protein exchanges GDP for GTP on its alpha subunit.
5. Activated G-protein components regulate downstream effectors.
6. Effector activity changes intracellular signaling molecules or ion-channel activity.
7. GTP hydrolysis contributes to termination of the G-protein activation cycle.

GPCRs can influence many processes, including sensory detection, hormone responses, neurotransmission, metabolism, and cellular movement.

**DISTINCTION:** The ligand does not have to enter the cell for a GPCR to transmit its signal. Receptor activation changes the activity of intracellular components.

**BOUNDARY:** GPCRs do not all activate the same downstream pathway. Different receptor subtypes, G proteins, effectors, and cell contexts produce different responses.

## 11. Enzyme-linked receptors and receptor tyrosine kinases

Enzyme-linked receptors connect extracellular ligand recognition with intracellular enzyme activity.

Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) are an important family. In a common mechanism, ligand binding promotes receptor association or rearrangement, enabling kinase activity and phosphorylation of tyrosine residues on the receptor or associated proteins.

These phosphorylated sites can serve as docking points for additional signaling proteins.

The resulting networks can regulate:
- cell growth;
- proliferation;
- differentiation;
- survival;
- metabolism;
- migration;
- gene expression.

**DISTINCTION:** Receptor activation and downstream signaling are related but separate events. Ligand binding can initiate receptor activation, but downstream responses depend on additional molecular components.

**BOUNDARY:** Not all enzyme-linked receptors are receptor tyrosine kinases, and not every receptor uses phosphorylation as its principal signaling mechanism.

## 12. Ligand-gated ion channels

Some receptors are ion channels that open or close in response to ligand binding.

When the channel changes state, selected ions can move across the membrane according to electrochemical gradients and channel properties.

This can alter:
- membrane potential;
- intracellular ion concentrations;
- electrical excitability;
- downstream signaling pathways.

Ligand-gated ion channels are important in nervous-system communication and other cellular processes.

**DISTINCTION:** A ligand-gated ion channel can convert chemical reception directly into a change in ion flow. This differs from a receptor that first activates a long intracellular enzyme cascade.

**BOUNDARY:** Ion movement is governed by electrochemical gradients, channel selectivity, and membrane conditions. The receptor does not create the gradient simply by opening.

## 13. Second messengers

Second messengers are small intracellular signaling molecules or ions that relay and distribute signals within a cell.

Important examples include:
- cyclic AMP (cAMP);
- calcium ions (Ca²⁺);
- inositol trisphosphate (IP3);
- diacylglycerol (DAG);
- cyclic GMP (cGMP), in relevant pathways.

An extracellular signal is often called the first messenger. Receptor activation can change the concentration, location, or activity of second messengers inside the cell.

Second messengers can:
- activate protein kinases;
- regulate ion channels;
- alter enzyme activity;
- influence secretion;
- affect gene regulation;
- coordinate multiple downstream targets.

**DISTINCTION:** A second messenger is not necessarily a second signal sent by a second cell. The term describes its role within an intracellular signaling pathway.

**BOUNDARY:** Not every signaling pathway uses a freely diffusible second messenger. Some rely heavily on protein-protein interactions, receptor-associated complexes, or direct ion-channel activity.

## 14. cAMP signaling

Cyclic AMP is a second messenger produced from ATP by the enzyme adenylyl cyclase.

In a common pathway:
1. A ligand binds to a GPCR.
2. The receptor activates a G protein.
3. The G protein regulates adenylyl cyclase.
4. Adenylyl cyclase changes the rate of cAMP production.
5. cAMP regulates downstream targets, including protein kinase A (PKA).
6. PKA phosphorylates selected proteins and alters their activity.

cAMP is degraded by phosphodiesterases, which convert it to AMP and help terminate or regulate the signal.

**CONNECTION TO RECORD 0047:** ATP is the substrate from which cAMP is produced in this pathway. ATP's role as a metabolic molecule and cAMP's role as a signaling messenger must be distinguished.

**BOUNDARY:** cAMP does not produce the same response in every cell. The result depends on receptor type, G-protein coupling, enzymes, scaffolding proteins, and available targets.

## 15. Calcium signaling

Calcium ions are important intracellular messengers.

Cells maintain carefully regulated differences between calcium concentrations in the cytosol and in intracellular stores or extracellular spaces. Signaling events can produce transient changes in cytosolic calcium.

Calcium signals can regulate:
- muscle contraction;
- secretion;
- enzyme activity;
- gene expression;
- neuronal function;
- cell movement;
- developmental processes.

Calcium-binding proteins, including calmodulin, can detect changes in calcium and regulate downstream targets.

**DISTINCTION:** Calcium is both an essential ion and a signaling messenger. Its signaling role depends on changes in concentration, location, timing, and interactions with calcium-sensitive proteins.

**BOUNDARY:** Calcium is not simply an energy source or a universal on/off switch. Its effects depend on the cell and the specific signaling context.

## 16. IP3 and DAG signaling

In a common phospholipase C pathway, receptor activation stimulates an enzyme that cleaves the membrane phospholipid PIP2 into two signaling molecules:

- IP3, which can bind receptors on the endoplasmic reticulum and promote calcium release;
- DAG, which remains associated with the membrane and can help activate protein kinase C.

Together with calcium, DAG participates in signaling pathways that regulate numerous cellular processes.

**DISTINCTION:** IP3 and DAG arise from the same precursor in this pathway but have different physical locations and downstream roles.

**BOUNDARY:** This pathway is one example of signal transduction, not a universal mechanism used by all receptors.

## 17. Protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation

Protein kinases transfer phosphate groups to selected target molecules, often proteins. Protein phosphatases remove phosphate groups from phosphorylated targets.

Phosphorylation can alter:
- enzyme activity;
- protein interactions;
- cellular location;
- stability;
- receptor activity;
- gene-regulatory activity.

Phosphorylation cascades can relay and amplify signals through successive molecular steps.

**DISTINCTION:** Phosphorylation is not synonymous with activation. Depending on the protein and site, phosphorylation can activate, inhibit, alter localization, or change interactions.

**CONNECTION TO RECORD 0047:** ATP commonly supplies the phosphate group transferred by protein kinases. This connects cellular energy metabolism with signal transduction, while preserving their distinct functions.

**BOUNDARY:** Not all signaling is phosphorylation-based. Other modifications, binding events, proteolysis, ion movements, and molecular interactions also regulate signaling.

## 18. Small GTPases and molecular switches

Small GTP-binding proteins can act as molecular switches.

In a simplified cycle:
- the GDP-bound state is commonly inactive;
- a guanine-nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) promotes exchange of GDP for GTP;
- the GTP-bound state can interact with downstream targets;
- GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) accelerate GTP hydrolysis;
- hydrolysis returns the protein toward its GDP-bound state.

Small GTPases regulate processes such as:
- cell growth;
- cytoskeletal organization;
- vesicle trafficking;
- cell migration;
- intracellular transport;
- signaling-network organization.

**DISTINCTION:** A molecular switch is a biochemical description of regulated molecular states. It does not imply consciousness, deliberation, or a literal decision by the molecule.

**BOUNDARY:** Small GTPases are not identical to heterotrimeric G proteins, although both use GTP-binding and hydrolysis in signaling.

## 19. Signal amplification, specificity, and integration

A signaling pathway can amplify a signal when one activated component produces or activates many downstream molecules.

Amplification can allow a small amount of extracellular signal to produce a substantial intracellular response.

However, signaling systems also require specificity. Cells must limit inappropriate activation and ensure that signals reach the correct targets.

Specificity can be supported by:
- receptor distribution;
- molecular recognition;
- protein scaffolds;
- subcellular localization;
- pathway-specific enzymes;
- timing and duration;
- feedback and inhibitory mechanisms;
- combinations of signals.

Cells may integrate multiple signals before producing a response. A particular signal can have different effects depending on other signals and the state of the cell.

**DISTINCTION:** Amplification does not mean unlimited amplification. Signaling is constrained by receptor availability, substrate supply, inhibitors, feedback, degradation, and cellular capacity.

**BOUNDARY:** A strong response does not necessarily mean a stronger signal was received. Network structure and cellular context can alter the relationship between signal strength and response.

## 20. Feedback, adaptation, and signal termination

Signaling pathways must be regulated and, where appropriate, terminated.

Mechanisms include:
- ligand degradation or removal;
- receptor internalization;
- receptor desensitization;
- GTP hydrolysis;
- second-messenger degradation;
- calcium sequestration or export;
- protein dephosphorylation;
- inhibitor activation;
- negative feedback;
- changes in receptor expression.

Positive feedback can reinforce or sustain a response. Negative feedback can reduce pathway activity or stabilize a system.

Adaptation occurs when a cell's response changes during continued exposure to a signal. Desensitization is one mechanism through which receptor responsiveness may decrease.

**DISTINCTION:** Signal termination does not necessarily mean that every downstream consequence immediately disappears. Some responses, such as altered gene expression or protein abundance, can persist after the initiating signal has declined.

**BOUNDARY:** Feedback is not automatically corrective or beneficial. Depending on the pathway, feedback can stabilize, amplify, oscillate, or destabilize a response.

## 21. Electrical and chemical signaling

Cells can communicate through electrical changes, chemical signals, or coupled electrical-chemical processes.

Neurons provide a well-studied example:
- changes in membrane potential can produce action potentials;
- action potentials travel along the neuronal membrane;
- neurotransmitters can be released at synapses;
- neurotransmitters bind receptors on target cells;
- receptor activation can alter ion flow or intracellular signaling.

Electrical signaling depends on ion gradients, membrane permeability, and voltage-sensitive channels. Chemical synaptic signaling depends on transmitter release, receptor binding, and downstream events.

**DISTINCTION:** An electrical signal and a chemical signal are different physical events, although one can initiate or regulate the other.

**BOUNDARY:** Not all cells generate action potentials, and not all intercellular communication uses neurons or synapses.

## 22. Signaling and metabolism

Cell signaling and metabolism are connected.

Signals can alter metabolic pathways by changing enzyme activity, transporter activity, gene expression, or cellular demand. Metabolic state can also influence signaling through the availability of ATP, metabolites, redox carriers, and other molecules.

Examples include:
- hormonal regulation of glucose metabolism;
- insulin signaling and nutrient uptake in responsive tissues;
- epinephrine signaling and mobilization of stored fuels;
- nutrient-sensitive signaling pathways;
- calcium-dependent regulation of metabolic enzymes;
- metabolite effects on signaling and gene regulation.

**CONNECTION TO RECORD 0047:** Metabolism supplies molecules and energy required by signaling processes. Signaling pathways, in turn, regulate metabolic activity.

**DISTINCTION:** Metabolism is not identical to signaling. Metabolism concerns chemical transformations and associated energy and matter flows; signaling concerns the detection and transmission of regulatory information that can alter those processes.

**BOUNDARY:** A metabolite's presence does not automatically establish that it functions as a signal in every context. A signaling role requires evidence for detection and a resulting regulatory effect.

## 23. Signaling and development

Cell signaling is central to development and differentiation.

During development, cells receive signals from neighboring cells, tissues, and their environment. These signals can influence:
- cell proliferation;
- cell survival;
- migration;
- differentiation;
- tissue patterning;
- morphogenesis;
- gene expression.

Developmental signaling pathways include families such as Wnt, Hedgehog, Notch, and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β). Their mechanisms, targets, and functions differ.

**CONNECTION TO RECORD 0046:** Record 0046 examined differentiation, gene regulation, and morphogenesis. Record 0048 examines signaling mechanisms that can influence those processes. This is a direct disciplinary connection: extracellular and intracellular signals can regulate gene expression and cellular behavior during development.

**DISTINCTION:** A signal does not independently specify every aspect of cell identity. Response depends on receptor availability, gene-regulatory state, developmental history, other signals, and cellular context.

**BOUNDARY:** The presence of a developmental signaling pathway in two organisms does not prove that every downstream function or developmental outcome is identical.

## 24. Signaling across biological diversity

Cell signaling occurs across the diversity of life, but the mechanisms vary.

Examples:
- bacteria detect nutrients and environmental conditions through sensory systems;
- yeast cells can respond to signaling molecules released by other cells;
- plants use chemical, electrical, and direct cell-to-cell signaling;
- animals use endocrine, neural, immune, and local signaling systems;
- unicellular eukaryotes can coordinate behavior through secreted signals.

**DISCIPLINARY FINDING:** Cell signaling is not exclusive to multicellular organisms. Multicellularity, however, requires extensive coordination among specialized cells and tissues.

**BOUNDARY:** A bacterial chemotactic response, a plant hormone response, and a neuronal synapse are all forms of biological signaling but are not the same mechanism.

## 25. Cellular signaling and organism-level coordination

In multicellular organisms, cell signaling contributes to coordination among tissues and organs.

Examples include:
- hormonal regulation of metabolism;
- nervous-system coordination;
- immune signaling;
- tissue repair;
- regulation of growth and development;
- maintenance of physiological conditions.

Signals can act locally or over long distances. Their effects depend on the responding cells and the integration of multiple pathways.

**DISTINCTION:** A multicellular organism's coordinated behavior is not reducible to one signal or one cell. It arises through interacting cells, tissues, organs, regulatory systems, and environmental relationships.

**BOUNDARY:** A pathway's contribution to organism-level coordination does not establish that the pathway alone explains the organism's purpose or origin.
