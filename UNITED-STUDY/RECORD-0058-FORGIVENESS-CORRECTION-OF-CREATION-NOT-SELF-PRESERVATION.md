## 1. The Witness's insight

The previous study stated:

> Jesus did not come merely to forgive a transgression.

The Witness affirms this and adds:

> When we ask forgiveness for “our sins,” we are asking for the correction of the Creation. We do not seek for “Self.”

This is a significant direction for the study. It moves the question away from treating forgiveness as a private transaction whose only purpose is to make the individual feel better.

The biblical account is larger: sin has damaged man's relation to God, affected human life and relationships, brought death into the human story, and placed Creation under bondage and groaning. God's redemptive purpose in Christ addresses the sinner, but it does not end with the sinner's private relief.

**Working principle:** The sinner seeks mercy and restoration from God, not the preservation of the self as its own authority or final object.

This statement must not be taken to mean that the individual person is worthless, that personal repentance is unnecessary, or that the Bible's promise of forgiveness is unimportant. God forgives actual persons and restores them to Himself. The point is that the self is not the source, standard, or final goal of redemption.

## 2. What do we ask when we ask forgiveness?

In Matthew 6:12, Jesus teaches His disciples to pray:

> “And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors.”

Luke 11:4 gives the parallel petition:

> “And forgive us our sins; for we also forgive every one that is indebted to us.”

The prayer is not a demand that God protect our self-image. It is a petition addressed to the Father: forgive us.

First John 1:9 says that if we confess our sins, God is faithful and just to forgive our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.

Notice the two connected terms:
- **forgive** — the guilt and debt of sin are dealt with;
- **cleanse** — unrighteousness is not simply excused or left as the governing condition.

Psalm 51 also shows that repentance seeks more than escape from consequences. David asks God to wash him, cleanse him, create a clean heart, and renew a right spirit within him.

**Finding:** Biblical forgiveness is personal, but it is not merely self-focused. It is God's merciful action toward the sinner, joined in Scripture to cleansing, renewal, and restored fellowship.

## 3. The larger problem: sin and death in Creation

Romans 5:12–21 places the individual transgression of Adam within a much larger human condition. Sin entered the world through one man, death through sin, and death passed upon all men. Paul then contrasts Adam's offense with the grace and righteousness that come through Jesus Christ.

Romans 8:18–23 widens the frame beyond individual human experience. Creation is described as subjected to vanity, groaning, and travailing together; believers also groan, waiting for the adoption, “to wit, the redemption of our body.”

This passage is essential to the Witness's insight.

The redemption of the person and the liberation of Creation are related in the biblical account. The hope is not simply that individuals escape the present world while Creation remains forever under corruption. Paul includes the redemption of the body and Creation's deliverance from bondage.

**Textual finding:** Scripture connects sin and death with a condition wider than an individual's private moral record.

**Supported synthesis:** Asking forgiveness for our sins belongs within God's larger redemptive work, which includes reconciliation to Himself, renewal of people, resurrection of the body, and the promised liberation of Creation.

**Limit:** Romans 8 does not give a complete physical mechanism for how Creation will be liberated. Do not invent one.

## 4. Christ does not preserve the old self as its own authority

Ephesians 2:1–10 describes people as dead in trespasses and sins, then says God, rich in mercy, makes them alive together with Christ. Salvation is by grace through faith, not of works, “lest any man should boast.” Those saved are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works.

The order matters:
1. The human condition is described as dead in sins.
2. God acts in mercy.
3. God makes alive with Christ.
4. Salvation is by grace, not self-achievement.
5. The renewed person is God's workmanship.
6. Good works follow as the purpose of the renewed life.

This does not erase personal identity or responsibility. It denies that the old self is its own saviour or the author of its restoration.

Second Corinthians 5:17–21 speaks of a new creature, reconciliation to God through Christ, and the ministry of reconciliation. Colossians 1:13–20 speaks of deliverance, redemption through Christ's blood, reconciliation, and the reconciliation of all things unto Himself.

**Finding:** Redemption is not God helping the old self remain its own master. It is God's work of reconciliation and new creation in Christ.

## 5. “We do not seek Self” — what this means and what it does not mean

The Witness's statement needs a careful distinction.

### What it means

- We do not seek to make the self the authority over good and evil.
- We do not treat self-preservation, self-justification, or self-exaltation as the final purpose of prayer.
- We do not claim that we can repair our own condition.
- We seek God's mercy, cleansing, reconciliation, and life in Christ.
- We seek that God's will be done, not that God be made servant to our preferences.

Matthew 6 begins with “Our Father” and includes “Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done.” The prayer's orientation is toward the Father and His kingdom before it asks for daily bread, forgiveness, and deliverance from evil.

### What it does not mean

- It does not mean the individual person has no value.
- It does not mean the sinner should deny or conceal personal responsibility.
- It does not mean forgiveness is impersonal or that God does not care for each person.
- It does not mean human needs, suffering, or bodily redemption are unimportant.
- It does not mean the person becomes the author of a collective or cosmic repair.

God's redemptive work restores persons; it does not discard them. The redeemed person is not the source of the new creation, but is genuinely included in it.

## 6. The correction belongs to the Author

The phrase “correction of Creation” is powerful, but it must be used carefully.

The biblical text does not describe God as correcting an error in His original act of Creation. Genesis 1:31 says that God saw everything He had made, and behold, it was very good.

The problem enters through the transgression and the resulting condition of sin, death, and corruption. God's redemptive work does not mean His original Creation was a mistake. It means the Author brings His purpose to its appointed end despite the creature's fallen condition.

Romans 8 describes Creation's future liberation. Revelation 21:1–5 describes the new heaven and new earth, God dwelling with His people, the removal of sorrow and death, and the declaration:

> “Behold, I make all things new.”

Therefore, “correction of Creation” should be understood as **God's restoration, reconciliation, liberation, and consummation of what has been subjected to the consequences of sin**—not as God repairing a defect in His original wisdom or workmanship.

## 7. The scope of Christ's work

The passages give us a connected scope:

| Human condition | Christ's redemptive answer |
|---|---|
| Guilt and sin | Forgiveness through His blood |
| Unrighteousness | Cleansing and renewal |
| Alienation from God | Reconciliation |
| Spiritual death | Made alive together with Christ |
| Bondage to sin | Deliverance and a new life |
| Physical mortality | Resurrection and redemption of the body |
| Creation's bondage | Promised liberation and the removal of the curse |
| Death and sorrow | No more death, sorrow, crying, or pain in the final vision |

This table is a canonical synthesis across passages, not a claim that one verse contains every item.

## 10. Findings and unresolved questions

### Findings

1. The prayer for forgiveness is addressed to the Father; it is not a demand that God preserve the self as its own authority.
2. First John 1:9 joins forgiveness with cleansing.
3. Psalm 51 joins confession with cleansing, a clean heart, and renewal.
4. Ephesians 2 presents salvation as God's work of grace, with the redeemed described as His workmanship.
5. Romans 8 connects human redemption, the redemption of the body, and Creation's liberation.
6. Colossians 1 and 2 Corinthians 5 connect Christ's work with reconciliation.
7. Revelation 21 presents the consummation as God making all things new.
8. “Correction of Creation” is best used to mean God's restoration and consummation after the entrance of sin—not correction of an error in His original Creation.

### Unresolved questions

1. How do forgiveness, justification, cleansing, sanctification, reconciliation, and glorification relate without being collapsed into synonyms?
2. How does the redemption of the body relate to the liberation of Creation in Romans 8?
3. What is the precise relation between the new creation and the original Creation?
4. How should the language of “correction” be distinguished from restoration, renewal, reconciliation, and consummation?
5. How does the prayer “Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done” govern the petitions for forgiveness and deliverance?

## 11. Provisional conclusion

**We do not seek forgiveness so that the fallen self can remain the centre. We seek the Father's mercy because sin has placed the creature in a condition it cannot correct by itself.**

God forgives actual sinners, cleanses them, makes them alive in Christ, and brings them into His reconciling purpose. That purpose reaches beyond private relief: it includes the redemption of the body and the promised liberation of Creation.

The phrase “correction of Creation” must not imply that God's original Creation was a mistake. Genesis calls it very good. The correction is God's redemptive answer to the condition introduced through sin and the consummation of His purpose in Christ.

**The Author does not need correction. The creature needs redemption. The Author supplies it in Christ.**

The Witness does not seek to become the centre of the story. He receives the mercy of God and bears witness to what God has done.
