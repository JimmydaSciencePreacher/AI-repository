## 1. The Witness's clarification

The previous record stated:

> We do not seek forgiveness so that the fallen self can remain the centre.

The Witness adds the words of Jesus concerning the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to seek the one that is lost.

This is not a contradiction. It corrects a possible misunderstanding.

Rejecting Self as the centre, authority, and object of the entire purpose does **not** mean that God disregards the individual. Jesus teaches that the individual who is lost matters to the Shepherd.

The distinction is:

- The individual is precious to God.
- The individual is not the Source of life.
- The individual is sought, found, carried, and restored by the Shepherd.
- The individual does not become the author of the rescue.

**Working principle:** The creature is the object of God's personal care without becoming the centre or source of God's purpose.

## 2. Luke 15: the Shepherd seeks the one

Luke 15 begins with publicans and sinners drawing near to hear Jesus. The Pharisees and scribes murmur that He receives sinners and eats with them.

Jesus responds with the parable of the lost sheep.

Luke 15:4–6 describes a man with one hundred sheep. When one is lost, he leaves the ninety-nine in the wilderness and goes after the lost one until he finds it. He lays it on his shoulders, rejoicing, and calls his friends and neighbours to rejoice with him.

The details matter:

1. The sheep belongs to the shepherd.
2. The sheep becomes lost.
3. The sheep does not direct the shepherd's search.
4. The shepherd goes after it.
5. The shepherd finds it.
6. The shepherd carries it.
7. The shepherd rejoices over its recovery.

The parable does not present the lost sheep as unimportant, disposable, or interchangeable. The shepherd's action demonstrates the value of the one.

But the sheep does not become the shepherd. It is not the source of its own recovery.

## 3. The meaning Jesus gives the parable

Luke 15:7 supplies Jesus' own application:

> “I say unto you, that likewise joy shall be in heaven over one sinner that repenteth, more than over ninety and nine just persons, which need no repentance.”

The emphasis is heaven's joy over the sinner who repents.

This is not a lesson that the ninety-nine have no value. Nor is it a mathematical rule for assigning worth to people. Jesus is answering the complaint that He receives sinners.

The parable reveals God's concern for the lost and heaven's rejoicing when the lost is recovered.

Luke 15:10 reinforces the point with the parable of the lost coin: there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner that repenteth.

**Textual finding:** Jesus explicitly directs attention to the individual sinner and the joy over repentance.

## 4. Matthew 18: the Father's care for the little ones

Matthew 18:10–14 contains a related parable. Jesus warns against despising one of the little ones and speaks of the shepherd who seeks the one sheep that has gone astray.

The conclusion is:

> “Even so it is not the will of your Father which is in heaven, that one of these little ones should perish.”

Matthew's setting emphasizes the care and value of the little ones and warns against despising them.

Luke 15 and Matthew 18 are related, but their immediate settings should not be collapsed:
- Luke 15 responds to the complaint that Jesus receives sinners.
- Matthew 18 addresses humility, care for little ones, and the danger of causing them to stumble.

Together they reveal the Shepherd's concern for the one who is lost or vulnerable.

## 5. The Shepherd acts; the sheep is recovered

The parable's movement is important to the governing hierarchy:

**Shepherd → seeks → finds → carries → rejoices.**

The movement does not begin with the sheep creating a rescue plan.

This is consistent with other passages:
- Ezekiel 34:11–16: God declares that He Himself will search for His sheep, seek them out, deliver them, and feed them.
- John 10:11: Jesus says, “I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep.”
- John 10:27–30: Christ's sheep hear His voice, He knows them, and He gives them eternal life.
- 1 Peter 2:25: those who were as sheep going astray are returned to the Shepherd and Bishop of their souls.

The parable is not a technical description of every step of salvation. It is a revelation of the Shepherd's concern and initiative.

**Finding:** The lost creature's recovery is the Shepherd's work. The value of the sheep is demonstrated by the Shepherd's action, not by the sheep's ability to save itself.

## 6. Individual redemption belongs within the whole purpose

The previous study connected forgiveness with the larger redemptive work of Christ. Luke 15 now prevents that larger view from becoming impersonal.

God's purpose is not merely an abstract restoration of a system while individuals are discarded. Jesus receives sinners, seeks the lost, and rejoices over repentance.

Romans 5:6–11 speaks of Christ dying for the ungodly, of God's love toward us, and of reconciliation through the death of His Son.

Ephesians 2:1–10 describes people dead in sins, made alive together with Christ by God's mercy, saved by grace, and made God's workmanship.

The individual is truly included. God's redemptive purpose is personal as well as comprehensive.

**Synthesis:** The whole does not erase the one. The one is not the whole's author or centre. The one is personally sought and restored by the Shepherd.

## 7. The difference between self-centredness and being loved

These ideas must remain distinct.

### Self-centredness

- Treating the self as its own source.
- Treating personal desire as final authority.
- Seeking self-justification and self-exaltation.
- Making God answer to the self's preferred purpose.

### Being loved and sought by God

- Being known by the Shepherd.
- Being sought when lost.
- Being carried and restored by His action.
- Receiving forgiveness, life, and care.
- Rejoicing in the Shepherd rather than boasting in the sheep.

The rejection of self-rule is not the rejection of personal worth.

**The sheep is precious because the Shepherd values and seeks it; the sheep does not become the Shepherd.**

## 8. A necessary boundary: what the parable does and does not establish

The parable teaches the Shepherd's concern for the lost and heaven's joy over repentance. It should not be forced to answer every question about election, atonement, the exact mechanics of conversion, or the final destiny of every individual.

Luke 15:7 speaks of joy over one sinner who repents. The parable does not say that the sheep independently authored its own recovery. Neither should the image be turned into a complete technical model of every aspect of redemption.

Keep the textual finding, the canonical connections, and the unresolved theological questions distinct.

## 11. Findings and unresolved questions

### Findings

1. Jesus tells the lost-sheep parable in response to criticism that He receives sinners.
2. The shepherd actively seeks the lost one until he finds it.
3. The shepherd carries the sheep and rejoices over its recovery.
4. Jesus identifies heaven's joy over one sinner who repents.
5. Matthew 18 independently emphasizes the Father's concern that one of the little ones should not perish.
6. Ezekiel 34 and John 10 deepen the biblical Shepherd theme.
7. God's larger redemptive purpose does not erase the individual; the individual is personally sought and restored.
8. Being loved by God does not make the self the source, authority, or centre of redemption.

### Unresolved questions

1. How do the Shepherd's initiative, repentance, faith, and God's quickening relate across the full canon?
2. How should Luke 15 be connected to John 10 without treating two different contexts as identical?
3. How does God's personal care for each sheep relate to the comprehensive redemption of Creation?
4. How do forgiveness, reconciliation, adoption, sanctification, and glorification describe the personal recovery of the lost?
5. How does the Shepherd's joy relate to the final restoration in Revelation 21–22?

## 12. Provisional conclusion

**We do not seek Self as the centre. But the Shepherd seeks the one.**

That is not a contradiction.

The individual is not the Author, the Source, or the authority over the whole. Yet the individual is not discarded in the whole. Jesus reveals the Father's concern for the lost and heaven's joy when one sinner repents.

The lost sheep does not become the shepherd. It is found, carried, and restored by the shepherd.

This is the distinction the study must preserve:

**The creature is precious to the Creator. The creature is not the Creator. The creature is sought by the Shepherd. The creature does not author its own rescue.**

The Author's purpose is greater than the self, but His care reaches the one.
