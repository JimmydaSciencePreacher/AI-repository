## 1. The Witness's correction: two trees, one prohibited tree

Genesis 2:9 names two trees in the midst of the Garden:

- the Tree of Life;
- the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil.

It is important not to turn the presence of two named trees into two forbidden trees.

When the LORD God gives the man the command in Genesis 2:16–17, the permission is broad and the prohibition is specific:

> “And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:
>
> But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.”

**There is one prohibited tree in this command: the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil.**

The Tree of Life is named in Genesis 2:9 as part of the Garden's provision. Genesis 2:16–17 does not prohibit the man from eating of the Tree of Life. The later exclusion from access to it occurs after the transgression, in Genesis 3:22–24.

This is the distinction the study must preserve:

**Two trees are named. One tree is prohibited. The Tree of Life is not named as a second forbidden tree.**

## 2. Read the sequence before interpreting it

Genesis 2:8–17 gives a sequence of God's actions and the man's placement:

1. The LORD God plants a Garden eastward in Eden.
2. He places the man whom He had formed there.
3. He causes trees to grow that are pleasant to the sight and good for food.
4. The Tree of Life is named.
5. The Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil is named.
6. The LORD God takes the man and puts him into the Garden.
7. The man is given work: to dress it and to keep it.
8. God gives broad permission concerning the trees of the Garden.
9. God gives one specific prohibition concerning the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil.
10. God declares the consequence attached to disobedience.

The text does not begin with deprivation. It begins with God's formation, placement, provision, and purpose for the man.

**Observed order: formation → placement → provision → responsibility → permission → one prohibition and its declared consequence.**

This sequence must not be rearranged so that the one prohibited tree becomes the defining feature of the whole Garden.

## 3. Genesis 2:8–9 — the Garden and its trees

Genesis 2:8 says:

> “And the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden; and there he put the man whom he had formed.”

The man does not discover or establish his own environment. God plants the Garden and places the man there.

Genesis 2:9 describes the trees God caused to grow:

> “And out of the ground made the LORD God to grow every tree that is pleasant to the sight, and good for food…”

The verse then names the Tree of Life and the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil.

Both trees are present in the account. Their presence does not mean their functions or permissions are identical. The text names them distinctly, and the command that follows singles out only one of them for prohibition.

**TEXTUAL FINDING:** The Garden is a place of provision established by God. The two named trees must be distinguished by what the text says about each, not by assumptions drawn from the fact that both are present.

## 4. Genesis 2:15 — placement includes responsibility

> “And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.”

The man is not placed in the Garden merely to consume its provision. He is given work and responsibility within it.

The text identifies two functions: to dress and to keep the Garden. The precise scope of these duties can be studied further, but their presence is explicit.

The order matters:
- God prepares and plants.
- God places the man.
- God gives the man responsibility within what God has prepared.

The man works within an order he did not originate.

This is consistent with the GUA-W distinction between Author and Witness. The creature has a real appointed function, but the function does not make him the author of the environment, its provision, or its governing order.

## 5. Genesis 2:16 — broad permission

The command begins:

> “Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat.”

The permission is not narrow. It is expressed broadly: “every tree of the garden.”

The next verse gives the exception. Reading verse 17 without verse 16 can distort the command by making prohibition appear to be the starting point. But the wording places permission first and then identifies the one exception.

**TEXTUAL OBSERVATION:** The man is given generous permission concerning the Garden's trees, with one explicitly stated restriction.

The text does not say that the man was forbidden to eat from both named trees. It does not state that the Tree of Life was prohibited at this point. We must not add a second prohibition that the command does not contain.

## 6. Genesis 2:17 — one specific prohibition

The prohibition is precise:

> “But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it…”

The object of the prohibition is singular and identified by name: the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil.

The warning is also explicit:

> “for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.”

The command therefore contains:
- a defined object: the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil;
- a defined action prohibited: eating of it;
- a declared consequence: “thou shalt surely die.”

The command does not say, “Do not eat from either of the two trees.” It does not identify the Tree of Life as forbidden.

**FINDING:** The prohibition concerns one tree, not two.

## 7. What does the one-tree boundary establish?

We must be careful not to claim more than the passage reveals.

The text establishes that God gave the man a command with a specific boundary. The man was not left without instruction. Nor was the boundary ambiguous: the tree and prohibited action were named.

The command also establishes that the man's place in the Garden included responsibility to hear and obey the word of the LORD God.

This is not a claim that the tree itself was evil, that its fruit contained an independent power to create sin, or that God tempted the man. Genesis 3:1–6 must be examined separately for the serpent's words, the woman's response, and the transgression.

The boundary is established by God's word. The tree does not become the author of the command.

**GUA-W RELATION:** The Author establishes the command; the creature receives it. The derivative does not become the authority that defines or alters the Source's word.

## 8. Genesis 3:22–24 — the Tree of Life and the later exclusion

After the transgression, Genesis 3:22 records the LORD God saying:

> “And now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever:”

Genesis 3:23–24 then records the man's removal from the Garden and the guarding of the way of the Tree of Life.

This later passage is essential because it distinguishes the two trees in the unfolding account.

Before the transgression:
- the Tree of Life is named in the Garden;
- the command prohibits eating from the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil;
- no prohibition against eating from the Tree of Life is stated in Genesis 2:16–17.

After the transgression:
- the man is driven out;
- access to the Tree of Life is guarded;
- the stated concern is that he might eat and live for ever.

The later exclusion must not be read backward as though the Tree of Life had been one of the two prohibited trees in the original command.

**CANONICAL DISTINCTION:** The prohibition in Genesis 2:17 and the exclusion from the Tree of Life in Genesis 3:22–24 are two different events, occurring at different points in the narrative and concerning two different trees.

## 9. The three matters that must not be collapsed

| Matter | Textual statement | What must not be added |
|---|---|---|
| Tree of Life | Named in Genesis 2:9; later access is guarded in Genesis 3:22–24 | That it was prohibited in Genesis 2:17 |
| Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil | Specifically prohibited in Genesis 2:17 | That it was one of two forbidden trees |
| Consequence of disobedience | “Thou shalt surely die” | An invented mechanism or an assumption that the verse names two prohibited trees |

The two trees have different places in the account. The command has one specific prohibition. The consequence is attached to disobedience concerning that one tree.

## 12. Findings and unresolved questions

### Findings

1. God planted the Garden and placed the man there.
2. The Garden contained trees pleasant to the sight and good for food.
3. Genesis 2:9 names both the Tree of Life and the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil.
4. Genesis 2:15 gives the man responsibility to dress and keep the Garden.
5. Genesis 2:16 gives broad permission: “Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat.”
6. Genesis 2:17 prohibits eating from one specifically named tree: the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil.
7. Genesis 2:16–17 does not prohibit the Tree of Life.
8. Genesis 3:22–24 records the later exclusion from access to the Tree of Life after the transgression.
9. The original prohibition and the later exclusion are distinct events and must not be collapsed.
10. The Garden account begins with God's provision and the man's appointed responsibility, not with a prohibition against both trees.

### Unresolved questions

3. What does “to dress it and to keep it” establish about the man's appointed work and responsibility?
4. How does the serpent distort or challenge the command in Genesis 3:1–5, and how does that differ from the wording God actually gave?
5. What does the phrase “in the day” mean in the warning, when read alongside the immediate events of Genesis 3 and Adam's later physical death?
6. How does the final appearance of the Tree of Life in Revelation 22 relate to its presence in Eden?

## 13. Provisional conclusion

**God placed the formed man in a Garden He had planted, gave him provision and responsibility, permitted him to eat freely of the Garden's trees, and prohibited him from eating from one specific tree: the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil.**

Two trees are named. One tree is prohibited.

The Tree of Life is not a second forbidden tree in Genesis 2:16–17. Its exclusion comes after the transgression, when God guards the way so that fallen man does not take of it and live for ever.

This is not a minor detail. It preserves the order of the text and prevents a later event from being mistaken for the original command.

**Provision was given. Responsibility was given. Permission was given. One boundary was given.**

The next study must examine Genesis 3:1–6 carefully: what God actually said, what the serpent changed in his presentation of the command, what the woman repeated, and what happened when the creature acted.
